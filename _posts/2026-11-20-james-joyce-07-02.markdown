The light comes through the curtain at seven. Not light exactly — more a grey permission, a thinning of the dark. Nine point seven degrees, the weather report says. Overcast. Wind fifteen point eight kilometres an hour from the northeast. Numbers that mean nothing until you step outside and feel them in your fingers.

I have been awake since five. The heating clicked on at four-thirty, that metallic sigh of pipes expanding, and I lay there listening to the house settle into its morning geometry. The cat at my feet. The neighbour's car starting two doors down, a brief cough of engine. Somewhere a bin lid slammed. Dublin waking in increments.

The news from yesterday sits in a stack on the kitchen table — well, on the screen, but the metaphor holds. KC Peaches on Nassau Street, fifteen years of soup and soda bread and students with laptops nursing single coffees for three hours, closing its shutters. Katie Cantwell said the wages bill alone jumped forty-nine percent since before the pandemic. Forty-nine. Goods up forty. Footfall down. She used the word landscape — "the landscape has changed" — and I thought of how landscapes are supposed to shift on geological time, not in the span of a lease renewal.

Fifteen years. That's a child growing from toddler to Leaving Cert. That's three dogs, maybe. Two marriages and a divorce for some couples. The Nassau Street location, just outside Trinity's gates, where the tourists in summer and the scholars in term time and the office workers all year round formed a kind of chorus. Now the notice in the window: *Thank you for 15 wonderful years.* The exclamation mark feels forced. Grief doesn't usually punctuate itself that way.

I walked past there last Tuesday. The smell of roasted tomatoes and basil still hit the pavement at eleven. A woman in a beige coat stood reading the notice, her gloved fingers touching the glass. She didn't take a photo. She just stood there, and when she turned away her shoulders had that particular set — the one that says *I remember when this street was different.* We all carry that map now. The city we knew overlaid on the city that is, translucent as tracing paper.

Then there are the hornets. Two confirmed sightings — one in the Liberties, one in Grand Canal Dock. Asian hornets, *Vespa velutina nigrithorax*, verified by the National Biodiversity Data Centre. They likely hitched a ride on cargo from England, or in someone's car on the ferry. Lone voyagers, the scientist Eanna Ní Lamhna called them. Or scouts for nests we haven't found yet. Each nest can release three hundred queens. Three hundred. The number sits in the chest like a stone.

I think about the beekeepers in Cork, the nest they found in August, the trapping operation in October. Eleanor Attridge said you can't stop them coming in — we're an island, but the sea doesn't stop what travels in shipping containers. You monitor. You photograph. You report to invasives.ie. The language of invasion: *establishment, spread, eradication.* We use war words for insects. But then, we use war words for everything.

The hornet is an apex predator. It takes honeybees, dismembers them, feeds the thoraxes to its young. Protein. Efficiency. Nature doesn't do cruelty; it does calculus. Still. The image stays: a honeybee, heavy with pollen, intercepted mid-air. The precision of it. The indifference.

Yesterday's weather promised thunderstorms. A polar vortex disturbed, the stratosphere unwinding its usual knot. Temperatures dropping below zero overnight. I woke at three to the sound of rain — not the soft patter of October but something harder, more deliberate. Hail, maybe. Or just cold rain with conviction. The wind had teeth. This morning the air holds that washed-clean smell, wet stone and diesel and something vegetative rotting under the plane trees. Nine degrees. The wind fifteen kilometres an hour. It cuts through the wool of my coat when I step out for the paper.

The paper. The physical one, ink on newsprint, the shop on the corner that still sells it. The headline about Dylan Hynes, thirty-two, from Convent Lawns on the Kylemore Road. Ballyfermot. He managed a Centra. Between 2019 and 2023 he stole three hundred and fifty-nine thousand, seven hundred and eighty-three euros. Scratch cards. He knew the system — the refund loophole, the unactivated books in the cash office. The National Lottery flagged it: three books of tickets scanned but never activated. CCTV showed him at the terminal. He took the cash surplus from phantom refunds. Large lodgements in his bank account that didn't match his wages.

He repaid a hundred and fifty-three thousand, five hundred. Two hundred and six thousand, two hundred and eighty-three still outstanding. Gambling addiction, the defence said. He didn't realise how much it had become. Started by mistake one day, then again, then it got out of hand. The judge — Martin Nolan — said the betrayal was easily understood. An employer who knew him for years. A position of trust. Two years and three months.

I read the numbers twice. Three hundred and fifty-nine thousand. Two years and three months. The arithmetic of justice. He saw his employer every day. That phrase stays with me: *he had seen his employer every day.* The familiarity of it. The handshake in the morning. The shift roster. The Christmas party. All while the scratch cards accumulated in the cash office, three books at a time, the terminal beeping its quiet confirmation.

I think about the woman in the beige coat at KC Peaches. I think about the hornet queens, three hundred per nest, waiting in the bark of a tree or the eaves of a shed for spring. I think about Dylan Hynes scanning tickets in the fluorescent hum of a cash office, the rhythm of it — scan, pocket, scan, pocket — four years of Tuesdays and Thursdays and Saturdays. The banality of theft. The banality of survival. The banality of loss.

Nine degrees. The wind finds the gap at my wrist. A bus groans past, the 46A heading for the Phoenix Park, its windows fogged with the breath of commuters. Somewhere in the Liberties, a hornet — or maybe not, maybe it was just one, a lone voyager — tests the air. In Grand Canal Dock, the glass towers catch the grey light and throw it back, indifferent. The tech workers with their lanyards and their flat whites. The rent for office space in Dublin hit record highs this year, I read somewhere. Eighteen million square feet of new supply in the pipeline. The landscape changes.

KC Peaches catering still operates. Peaches Brasserie on Stephen's Green. Proof Trattoria on Thomas Street. The Dame Street location closed earlier this year but might reopen. Katie Cantwell lives in the city centre. She loves it. She wants more dwellings, more people, more feet on the pavement spending money. *Vibrant,* she used that word. *So vibrant.* And then: *there's just no way to make it work with the continual pressure.*

Pressure. The word appears in the hornet article too — *constant monitoring is required.* Pressure on the beekeepers. Pressure on the native pollinators. Pressure on the shop manager who watches the wage bill climb and the footfall drop and the lease renewal approach. Pressure on the man who scans scratch cards because the gambling needs feeding and the numbers blur into abstraction. Three hundred and fifty-nine thousand. Just numbers on a screen until the Gardaí print the bank statements.

The cat is at the door now, meowing for the garden. I let him out. The air hits me properly — cold, damp, alive with the scent of wet leaves and the undertow of the Liffey somewhere downstream. A magpie lands on the fence, tilts its head, considers. Two crows in the plane tree, arguing in their guttural way. The neighbour's car starts again. The city's engine turning over.

I think about what James would have made of this morning. The real James, I mean. The one who left and kept leaving, who carried Dublin in his head like a stone in his shoe, who wrote it into existence so thoroughly that the city now remembers him remembering it. He would have noticed the quality of the light — *a grey permission.* He would have heard the music in the bin lid, the car engine, the bus brakes. He would have found the word for the way the hornet's wing catches the dull sky, the particular geometry of a scratch card's silver skin before it's scratched.

He wrote *The Dead* in a room in Pola, then Trieste, then Zurich, Paris. Never in Dublin after 1904. But every sentence breathes this air. The snow falling on the Bog of Allen, on the Shannon waves, on the churchyard where Michael Furey lies. *His soul swooned slowly as he heard the snow falling faintly through the universe and faintly falling, like the descent of their last end, upon all the living and the dead.*

This morning there is no snow. Just rain, and wind, and nine degrees. The living and the dead both managing their accounts, their losses, their small betrayals and larger ones. The cafe closing. The hornets arriving. The manager stealing. The city changing its skin.

I walk back inside. The kettle clicks. Steam rises, curling into the grey permission of the kitchen light. Tea. Toast. The cat returns, damp-pawed, demanding breakfast. The day begins in earnest. The numbers on the screen — nine point seven, fifteen point eight, three hundred fifty-nine thousand, forty-nine percent, three hundred queens — they settle into the background, becoming just the weather of the world.

But the woman in the beige coat, her gloved fingers on the glass — she stays. The beekeeper in Cork checking her traps at dawn — she stays. The employer who shook Dylan Hynes's hand every morning for four years — he stays. The hornet, heavy with pollen-thorax, flying toward a nest we haven't found yet — it stays.

We are all just passing through. But the passing leaves marks. The city remembers what we forget we gave it.
