Government bond markets across the Atlantic and the Channel have had a volatile couple of days. Yields — which move opposite to prices — have swung on a mix of central-bank policy signals, geopolitical shocks, and unprecedented Treasury intervention. Here is what happened, why it matters, and what it means for anyone with a mortgage, a pension, or a savings account.

### United States: The Treasury Steps In, the Fed Watches

The biggest story in US markets is Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's decision to double the government's buyback of long-dated debt. On Wednesday, August 20, the Treasury announced it would raise the maximum size of each buyback operation from $2 billion to at least $4 billion, targeting bonds with 10 to 30 years until maturity. The move came after the 30-year Treasury yield touched 5.33% — a 19-year high — and the 10-year yield climbed above 4.70%, levels that make mortgages, car loans, and corporate borrowing painfully expensive.

Bessent framed the buybacks as a liquidity operation: ensuring there are enough buyers and sellers in the less-traded corners of the market so prices remain reliable. But the mechanics matter. To pay for these purchases without expanding the overall debt stock, the Treasury issues more short-term bills. That shifts the composition of government debt toward shorter maturities, which tends to steepen the yield curve — pushing long-term yields down relative to short-term ones. Critics argue this is monetary policy by another name, encroaching on the Federal Reserve's turf.

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, who took the helm in 2025, has been notably cautious. At his July press conference, he acknowledged inflation has stayed above the 2% target for over five years but declined to raise rates, suggesting the bond market had already done some of the tightening for him. Warsh has also floated rewriting the 1951 Treasury-Fed Accord to give the Treasury more say over the Fed's balance sheet — a proposal that blurs the line between fiscal and monetary authority. Markets are now parsing every word ahead of his Jackson Hole speech later this week, looking for clues on whether the Fed will tolerate, resist, or coordinate with Treasury's yield-capping efforts.

The immediate result: yields dipped slightly on Monday, August 24, with the 10-year easing to 4.72% and the 2-year holding at 4.23%. But the underlying tension remains. If the Treasury keeps buying long bonds while the Fed holds rates steady or cuts, the signal to investors is that the government will backstop the long end — potentially embedding an inflationary bias into the curve.

### United Kingdom: Gilts at the Eye of the Storm

UK government bonds — gilts — have been disproportionately hit by the global sell-off. The 10-year gilt yield closed Friday, August 21 at 5.058%, up 2.1 basis points for the week and holding above the psychologically important 5% threshold. The 30-year and 50-year yields each rose 2 basis points, and the 2/10-year spread widened to 70 basis points, signaling expectations of tighter policy ahead.

Three structural factors make gilts uniquely vulnerable. First, public sector debt sits near 95% of GDP — nearly triple pre-financial-crisis levels — and debt interest alone now exceeds £100 billion a year. That leaves the Chancellor with almost no fiscal headroom. Second, close to a quarter of the gilt market is inflation-linked, the largest share of any major developed economy. When oil prices spike — as they did after the Iran ceasefire collapsed and a vessel was struck in the Strait of Hormuz, pushing crude above $90 — those index-linked bonds automatically raise the government's interest bill in real time. Analysts estimate roughly two-thirds of the yield jump after an inflation shock comes from investors demanding extra compensation to hold UK debt, a penalty the US and Germany don't pay in the same way.

Third, the Bank of England faces a genuine dilemma. Weak growth argues for rate cuts; an oil-driven inflation spike argues for hikes. Markets have swung from pricing cuts to pricing hikes within months. As of late August, swaps imply 14 basis points of hikes through September and 41 basis points through December. The BoE has also quietly backed away from mandatory gilt clearing — a reform meant to strengthen the market — leaving the UK on a different regulatory path from the US, where mandatory Treasury clearing takes effect this December.

For UK households, this isn't abstract. Higher gilt yields feed directly into mortgage rates, and the government's interest bill crowds out spending on public services. Retail investors have been snapping up gilts directly via the National Savings & Investments platform, but institutional demand — especially from overseas — has been fickle.

### Europe: Bunds Lead, Periphery Follows

In the euro area, the German 10-year Bund yield held at 3.26% on August 21, up 8 basis points over the month and 54 basis points over the year. The broader euro-area 10-year benchmark stood at 3.64%. But the real action is in the spreads — the extra yield investors demand to hold French, Italian, or Spanish debt instead of German.

France's 10-year OAT yielded 4.08% on August 21, leaving the OAT-Bund spread at 83.6 basis points. That's relatively tight by historical standards, but political uncertainty — including declining foreign appetite for French debt, notably from Japanese investors — has kept the premium from narrowing further. Italy's BTP-Bund spread has compressed significantly, with some observers noting it has "disappeared" relative to levels seen three years ago, reflecting improved fiscal credibility under the current government.

The catalyst for the recent surge was the same geopolitical shock hitting the UK: the collapse of the US-Iran ceasefire and the Hormuz incident. On August 18, Euronews reported French 10-year yields hit a 17-year high and German Bunds reached their highest level since 2011. The ECB's policy-sensitive two-year Bund yield rose over 25 basis points as traders ramped up bets on further rate hikes, fearing the Middle East conflict could fuel longer-term inflation through energy prices.

The ECB's challenge mirrors the BoE's: growth is sluggish, but energy-driven inflation could force a tightening bias. Unlike the Fed, the ECB has no explicit dual mandate — its primary objective is price stability — which makes it less inclined to look through supply-side shocks. That means European yields may stay elevated longer if oil remains high.

### Why This Matters to You

Bond yields are the price of money across time. When they rise, everything priced off them — mortgages, car loans, corporate debt, government borrowing — gets more expensive. The last two days have shown a world where fiscal authorities (Treasuries, Finance Ministries) are actively managing their yield curves, central banks are navigating independence questions, and geopolitics is injecting volatility that no model fully captures.

For the US, the question is whether Treasury buybacks become a permanent feature — and whether the Fed accommodates or resists. For the UK, it's whether the structural vulnerabilities (high debt, inflation-linked exposure, fiscal constraints) force a crisis or a credible consolidation path. For Europe, it's whether the ECB can maintain credibility on inflation without choking the fragile recovery.

Markets don't price perfection; they price uncertainty. Right now, uncertainty is abundant.
