The sky is the colour of a bruised plum, that particular grey that sits low over Dublin in mid-September. Sixteen point nine degrees. The wind moves at eighteen kilometres an hour from the southwest, Met Éireann says, and I believe them because I can feel it in the curtains, hear it in the plane trees on the North Circular Road. Overcast. The word sits in the mouth like a stone. Not raining. Not not-raining. The kind of morning where the light doesn't so much arrive as seep through, grey and patient, pooling on the wet flags.

I walked to the corner shop for milk at half six. The air tasted of diesel and wet leaves and something else — yeast, maybe, from the bakery on Prussia Street, or the ghost of last night's rain rising off the tarmac. A fox crossed Phibsboro Road near the library, unhurried, the way foxes have of knowing the city's rhythms better than we do. The number 38 bus shuddered past, its lights cutting cones through the half-dark. Inside, faces lit by phone screens. The same faces every morning. The woman with the blue umbrella she never closes. The man who reads a physical newspaper, folded precise as a letter. Me, watching them, watching me watching them.

Dalymount Park holds the floodlights still, but not for long. Bohemian FC announced yesterday — or was it the day before? Time blurs — that they'll stay at the old ground until May 2027. Another winter in Phibsboro. Another season of Friday nights under lights that are already condemned. The Tramway End terrace, erected in 1928, comes down this winter. October 23rd: the last game under the Dalymount lights. I know that date now. It sits in the calendar like a stone. The club will play at "more than one venue" for the rest of 2027 and 2028. Nomads in their own city. The planned return: 2029. Three years. Three seasons. A child born today will be in junior infants by the time the Bohs come home.

I think about what a stadium holds. Not concrete and steel. Not even the ghost of floodlights. It holds the weight of Saturdays. The smell of Bovril and cheap lager and the particular silence of two thousand people holding their breath before a penalty. It holds the Tramway End, which has held generations of voices. My grandfather stood there. His father maybe. The terrace doesn't know about redevelopment plans or planning permissions or the "minimise our time away" language of press releases. It only knows boots on concrete and the roar that rises like a prayer.

The wind picks up. A plastic bag skitters across the footpath, caught in a small vortex. It dances there, weightless, then settles against a wheelie bin. October 23rd. I'll be there. I have to be. Some things you witness not because you're asked but because the city asks it of you.

Further out, past the Phoenix Park where the deer are moving through mist I can't see but know is there, Blanchardstown Centre has won its parking battle. An Coimisiún Pleanála approved the "Mobility Enhancement Project" — the kind of phrase that makes you reach for a dictionary and find only euphemisms. Paid parking. Barriers. A "balanced approach to car parking by utilising parking management as a demand-control measure to promote more sustainable forms of transport." Thirteen words where two would do: pay up. Fingal County Council said yes in November. Three separate appeals to ACP. Three nos became a yes. The commission determined the scheme would not worsen traffic, would not negatively affect residential or visual amenities, would not undermine the "vitality or viability" of the centre. I wonder who measures vitality. I wonder if the woman pushing a double buggy from the bus stop, two toddlers and a week's shopping, feels the vitality of a barrier arm rising and falling. The language of planning is a language of erasure. It speaks in impacts and mitigations and never in the currency of a mother's tired arms.

The same council, the same city, the same week: Dublin City Council's rent hikes on social tenants. Sinn Féin's Ciarán Ó Meachair called it "Mr Burns pretending to be strapped for cash" at the South Central Area Committee. The simile landed. Mr Burns, hunched in his mansion, fingers steepled, whispering *excellent* while the town freezes. €44,500 for a new logo nobody asked for. Hundreds of millions for the Camden Yard move — Wood Quay to Camden Yard, the great migration of bureaucrats. Three hundred and twenty thousand people in arrears on energy bills. Families cutting back on food, heating, essentials. Damp. Mould. Sewage problems. Poor windows and doors. Long-standing maintenance issues in older stock. The words *older stock* do a lot of work. They make people sound like inventory. The council says the increases plug a maintenance deficit. The tenants say they're plugging a deficit they didn't create. Green Party Cllr Ray Cunningham says the area committee isn't the appropriate place for the motion. Cllr Michael Pidgeon believes there's a "clear, direct line between the rents charged and the maintenance we do." Decades of low rates have "starved" maintenance budgets. The fruit of that decision: damp walls, black mould, windows that don't close. Labour's Darragh Moriarty speaks of responsiveness — months for basic issues. Assistant Chief Executive Mick Mulhern says they wrote off €2 million in debt last year. Frank D'Arcy says the shock absorber income disregard rose to €55 per week from €32. Numbers. Always numbers. The language of care translated into the language of accounting. Mr Burns would understand.

I stand at the window. The light has shifted. Not brighter. Different. The grey has thinned enough to show the outline of the Spire, that needle in the city's vein. A seagull cries, inland too early or too late depending on your theory of seagulls. The plane trees shiver.

And then there's this: a school in Gaza. The Olive Tree School. Two hundred children. Ten qualified staff. Science classes, exams, therapy, psychology support, play, parent workshops. It started in October 2024 with forty children and three teachers, founded by Abed and Aya after their own schools were destroyed. Arabic, English, Maths. The basics. The necessities. And it grew because Dublin's gig scene got behind it. Whelan's. Vicar Street. The Workman's Club. Damien Dempsey. Mundy. Paddy Casey. Brian Brannigan. Alice Jago. Album launches. Song releases. Merch. Cycling challenges. A doggie fashion show. *A doggie fashion show.* The phrase catches in the throat. Dogs in tiny coats on a Dublin stage, and the money goes to classrooms in Gaza. Stay Human Collective. The name itself — *Stay Human* — a command, a plea, a lifeline. Moira Cassidy, co-founder: "We built this school because we refused to let children in Gaza lose out on their education and we wanted to give their community hope in a future." Twenty-one thousand euros raised towards forty-five thousand. A new educational camp. Capacity from two hundred to one thousand children. The handover to doBetter e.V., a German charity that runs ninety schools across Syria, Lebanon, Afghanistan. The original Gazan team stays on the ground. One final Dublin gig: The Workman's Club, September 24th. Madra Salach headlining. Whisht supporting. *Madra Salach* — dirty dog in Irish. *Whisht* — hush, quiet, the sound of holding your breath.

I think about the distance between Phibsboro and Gaza. Five thousand kilometres, give or take. The distance between a floodlight coming down and a school going up. The distance between a parking barrier and a doggie fashion show. The distance between Mr Burns in his mansion and a mother in Gaza packing a schoolbag for a child who has never known a day without war. The distance collapses in the mind. It has to. Otherwise how do you carry it?

The milk sits on the counter, forgotten. The fox is gone. The number 38 has passed. The wind still moves at eighteen kilometres an hour. The temperature holds at sixteen point nine. Overcast. The word still tastes like stone.

But here's what I keep returning to: forty children. Three teachers. October 2024. The rubble still settling. And someone said *we start anyway*. Not *we wait for funding*. Not *we apply for grants*. *We start anyway*. Two hundred children now. One thousand possible. A doggie fashion show in Dublin. A cycling challenge. A song release. The accumulated weight of small things — a ticket bought, a pint spilled, a dog in a tiny coat — becoming a school. Becoming hope. Becoming *stay human*.

The light on Phibsboro Road is not the light on Gaza. The rain that falls on the Tramway End is not the rain that falls on a classroom in a camp. But the hands that build are the same hands. The hearts that break are the same hearts. The city that holds Dalymount and Blanchardstown and Wood Quay and Camden Yard and the Workman's Club and Whelan's and Vicar Street — this city, this grey, patient, seeping city — it holds them all. It holds us all.

I pick up the milk. Put it in the fridge. The hum of the compressor starts up, a low vibration in the kitchen floor. The plane trees shiver again. Somewhere, a floodlight waits to come down. Somewhere, a barrier arm waits to rise. Somewhere, a rent notice lands on a mat. Somewhere, a child in Gaza opens a Maths book.

The day begins. Overcast. Sixteen point nine. The light seeps through.

*Stay human*, I whisper to the empty kitchen. *Stay human*.
