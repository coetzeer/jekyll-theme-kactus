Government bond markets across the major advanced economies have spent the past two days digesting a familiar but potent mix of geopolitical risk, stubborn inflation, and a wall of new debt supply. The result: yields — the effective interest rate governments pay to borrow — have climbed or held near multi-year highs in the United States, the United Kingdom, and across the euro area, from the safety of German Bunds to the riskier debt of Italy and France.

### United States: The Long End Leads the Sell-Off

The benchmark 10-year US Treasury yield sits at 4.74%, a whisker below the 4.75% level touched last week — its highest since early 2025. The 30-year bond yields 5.32%. What makes this move notable is that it is concentrated at the "long end" of the curve: yields on 20- and 30-year debt have risen faster than those on 2- or 5-year notes. That pattern typically signals investors are demanding extra compensation for holding debt over long horizons, either because they expect inflation to stay higher for longer, or because the sheer volume of bonds hitting the market is overwhelming buyers.

Three forces are at work. First, the Middle East conflict. The Strait of Hormuz — through which roughly a fifth of global oil shipments passed before the US-Iran war erupted in late February — remains largely closed. President Trump has signaled no rush to end the blockade on Iranian energy exports. Brent crude, while off its July peak near $100 a barrel, is still roughly 45% higher than at the start of the year. Higher energy costs feed directly into inflation and, by extension, into the yields investors demand on long-dated bonds.

Second, inflation itself remains sticky. The July Consumer Price Index rose 3.4% year-over-year, down only slightly from June's 3.5%. Core CPI (excluding food and energy) slowed to 2.5% from 2.6%, but much of that deceleration came from a sharp drop in hotel prices that analysts consider temporary. Underneath the surface, price pressures are broadening: more categories of goods saw price increases in July than in June, and technology prices — driven by voracious AI demand — jumped. The Federal Reserve's preferred gauge, core PCE, is still estimated to be running above 3%.

Third, a deluge of corporate debt issuance. AI-related companies alone have raised roughly $1.5 trillion in bonds this year, flooding the market with dollar-denominated fixed-income supply that competes with Treasuries for investor cash.

The Fed's policy rate has sat at 3.50–3.75% since December. After the July meeting, three policymakers dissented in favor of a rate hike, and several regional presidents have since argued the case for tightening. Markets now price a 38% chance of a hike at the September 15–16 meeting, but the dominant view remains a hold. As Fitch Ratings' Olu Sonola put it: "Without forward guidance, the September decision will likely remain a close call until the very end."

### United Kingdom: Gilts Caught Between Inflation and Growth

UK 10-year gilt yields stand at 5.09%, up from around 4.95% earlier this month. The 30-year yields 5.84%. The Bank of England held its policy rate at 3.75% in July, with Governor Andrew Bailey saying the disinflation process remains on track despite external risks. But the market is not convinced.

The BoE's own projections show headline CPI peaking at 4% in the coming months. The labour market has weakened — unemployment steady at 4.9%, payroll employment falling — yet inflation persists. This "stagflationary" dynamic leaves the Monetary Policy Committee in a bind: cutting rates risks reigniting price pressures; holding or hiking risks deepening the growth slowdown.

The August 2025 meeting (the most recent with a published vote split) illustrated the tension: four of nine members voted to hold, one wanted a larger 50-basis-point cut, and only four backed the 25-basis-point reduction that carried. That "razor-edge" vote caused markets to slash expectations for further cuts this year.

More recently, gilt yields have seesawed with oil prices and hopes for a Hormuz deal. On August 11, the 10-year yield dipped below 5% on reports of a potential US-Iran arrangement. By August 17, it had climbed back above 5% as those hopes faded and Brent crude rebounded. The pound has strengthened alongside yields — a change from earlier this year when fiscal worries saw the currency fall as yields rose.

### Europe: Bunds at 15-Year Highs, Periphery at Multi-Year Peaks

Germany's 10-year Bund yield trades at 3.25%, its highest level since 2011. The move mirrors the US sell-off but with a distinctly European flavor: the ECB's deposit rate sits at 2.25%, yet money markets now price more than a 90% probability of a September rate hike and a terminal rate around 2.76% by March 2027. German inflation ticked up to 2.8% in July, a three-month high, reinforcing the hawkish shift.

The periphery tells an even starker story. Italy's 10-year yield hit 4.06% on August 18, its highest since March. France's 10-year reached 4.10%, a 17-year high last seen in June 2009. The spread (extra yield) over Bunds has widened, reflecting both the global duration sell-off and country-specific fiscal concerns. France's deficit has widened, and Italy's debt-to-GDP ratio remains above 130%.

ECB officials have been notably quiet, but the market has moved ahead of them. Wholesale energy prices, the euro's depreciation against the dollar, and the pass-through from higher oil are all feeding into a narrative that the ECB's cutting cycle is over — and a hiking cycle may have begun.

### Why It Matters

For anyone with a mortgage, a pension, or a savings account, these moves are not abstract. Higher long-term yields feed directly into fixed-rate mortgage pricing, corporate borrowing costs, and the discount rates used to value pension liabilities and equities. They also constrain government budgets: every additional percentage point on 10-year debt adds tens of billions to annual interest bills over time.

The common thread across all three regions is the Middle East conflict's inflationary impulse. As long as the Hormuz blockade persists and oil stays elevated, central banks will struggle to declare victory on inflation — and bond markets will keep pricing in a world where "higher for longer" is not just a slogan but a structural reality.
