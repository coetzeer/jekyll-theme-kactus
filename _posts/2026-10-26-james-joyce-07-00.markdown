The light falls differently on a Monday. Thinner. Paler. It seeps through the curtains at this hour — quarter past seven — with the reluctance of something that knows it isn't wanted yet. I have been awake since six, listening to the house breathe. The heating clicks on. A pipe groans somewhere in the walls. Outside, the trees still hold the storm.

Storm Boelo. The name sounds like something from a children's book, a friendly bear perhaps, but the Met Éireann warnings told another story. Status Orange for Donegal, Galway, Leitrim, Mayo, Sligo. Yellow for the rest of us. I watched the forecast on Friday evening, the presenter's voice measured, professional, the map lit up with those angry colours. Gale force westerlies. Severe and damaging gusts. Power outages. Fallen trees. Flooding. The language of weather is always more violent than the weather itself, until it isn't.

Saturday morning I woke to rain lashing the window, horizontal, the kind that finds its way through seals you thought were sound. The wind hit the roof in waves, each one louder than the last. By afternoon the worst had passed over Dublin — we got the tail, the fringes — but out west the sea came inland. I read the reports later: waves crashing over the promenade in Salthill, a caravan park evacuated in Clifden, the Shannon spilling onto roads in Leitrim. The country holds its breath when the Atlantic decides to remind us who owns the coast.

Today the air is different. Still. Cold. Nine degrees, the forecast said yesterday — dense drizzle, nineteen kilometres an hour wind. But this morning the drizzle has lifted. The sky sits low and grey, a ceiling pressed down on the city. No rain falling, not yet, but the promise of it in every breath. The pavements glisten. The trees, those that remain standing, drip steadily onto the grass. A blackbird calls from the neighbour's hawthorn, once, twice, then silence.

Monday. The funeral of Jamie Bailey is at eleven. St. John's Cathedral. I read the notice in yesterday's paper: thirty-eight years old, from Limerick originally, living in Dublin. Stabbed on Blackhorse Avenue near Phoenix Park in the early hours of Sunday the eighteenth. A grandfather. The word stops me. Thirty-eight and a grandfather. His daughters, his granddaughter. The photographs show a face that laughs easily, eyes crinkled at the corners. Friends called him a character. Always up for a laugh and a joke. Always stopping for a chat. The kind of man who makes a place feel like a place.

Blackhorse Avenue. I know that road. The long straight stretch alongside the Park, the wall on one side, the traffic rushing toward the N4 on the other. At three in the morning it would be empty. Just the sodium lights pooling on the tarmac. The Phoenix Park wall stretching into darkness, the deer somewhere in the trees beyond it, unaware. Two men emerged from a building, the reports say. Carried out the attack. He was found on the footpath. They believe he was attacked there, not staggered there after. The distinction matters to someone. To the gardaí. To the family. To the man who will stand in the pulpit on Monday morning and try to make sense of a life ended on a footpath near the Park.

I think of his mother. Susan. His father, Jim. The brothers and sisters. The daughters. The granddaughter — how old? Old enough to remember? Young enough to forget? The notices say "beloved daughters and granddaughter." Plural daughters. One granddaughter. A small life that contained other lives. A character. Always up for a laugh. The kind of man who stops to ask how you are and means it. Dublin holds thousands like him. Men and women who make the city liveable, who greet you in the shop, who remember your name at the counter, who lean on the hurley at the boundary wall and talk about the weather, the team, the price of things. The fabric. The weft. When one goes, a thread pulls loose.

Forty years ago tomorrow — October 23, 1986 — a thirteen-year-old boy named Philip Cairns walked from Coláiste Éanna in Rathfarnham toward his home on Ballyroan Road. He reached the house, collected his bag, turned back toward school. Never arrived. Six days later his schoolbag was found in a laneway off Ann Devlin Road. Some books missing. Grey jacket with black shoulder corners. Grey trousers. Grey shirt. School pullover. Five foot two. Short black hair. The description reads like a catalogue entry. A boy. A child. Forty years. Sixteen thousand days of not knowing. The gardaí have followed more than sixteen hundred lines of inquiry. The investigation team still engages with the family. Still receives calls. Still appeals. "Even the smallest piece of information, which may seem insignificant, may assist the investigation." The language is careful. Institutional. But behind it, the same question, asked sixteen thousand times: where is he?

I walked that laneway once. Years ago. Just a passage between Ann Devlin Road and Ann Devlin Drive, the kind of shortcut children know, the kind adults forget. The walls were high. Ivy. Shadows even at noon. A place where a bag could be left and not found for six days. A place where a boy could vanish. The school, Coláiste Éanna, still stands. The road, Ballyroan Road, still runs. The house on Ballyroan Road — someone lives there now. Do they know? Do they feel it in the walls? The anniversary brings the cameras, the microphones, the renewed appeals. "Who were young at the time and not in a position to provide information, but may now be able to do so." Time changes things. People grow. Secrets become burdens. Someone knows. Someone has always known. The city keeps its secrets in the gaps between things — in laneways, in the space between leaving a house and arriving at a school, in the six days between disappearance and a bag found in ivy.

Donal Lunny died on Friday. Seventy-nine. Cancer. St. James's Hospital. The news came through the radio while I was driving, and I had to pull over. Not because I knew him — I didn't — but because the voice on the radio, Christy Moore's voice, cracked when he spoke of sixty-six years. "I met him first in 1959 in school, and what I remember most from those years were his lack of homework, that he was more interested in being out on the Liffey trying to tickle trout, or climbing trees to gaze into the wild birds' nests." Sixty-six years. A lifetime measured in trout-tickling and birds' nests. The bouzouki. He designed his own, they said. An instrument associated with Greek music, made Irish by his hands. Planxty. The Bothy Band. Moving Hearts. Usher's Island. The names are a history of the music I grew up with, the music that played in kitchens and pubs and concert halls, the music that carried the country through its changes. He was at the National Concert Hall in 2023. Drawing from the Well: Tommy Peoples. The Irish Traditional Music Archive. The well. The drawing. The music doesn't die. It passes from hand to hand, from bouzouki to bodhran to guitar to mandolin to keyboard to drums to harmonica. He played them all. The genius, they called him. The innovator. The one who heard the tradition and asked what else it could be.

The Christmas busk on Grafton Street will go ahead this year. Mundy confirmed it. Glen Hansard would have been the one rallying the troops, making the calls, getting U2 and Dermot Kennedy and whoever else to leave their families on Christmas Eve and stand in the cold with a woolly hat, singing for the Dublin Simon Community. Two million euros raised over fifteen years. Two million. That number stays with me. Two million euros in coins and notes dropped into hats and guitar cases by people rushing for the last gifts, by tourists who stumbled on it, by office workers on lunch break, by the homeless themselves sometimes, giving what they could. Glen made sure the energy was there. If a famous face wasn't lifting the crowd, he'd call up a kid with a belter of a song. Seventeen-year-old Dermot Kennedy. He saw it coming. He saw them all coming. Now Mundy says he'll do some of the lifting but not all of it. Imelda May. Liam Ó Maonlaí. Danny O'Reilly from The Coronas. Donal Lunny helped over the years. The circle widens. The music carries on. The homeless crisis is not going away, Mundy said. No. It isn't. But neither is the singing.

Troy Parrott's house near Seville. Masked men on CCTV. Saturday evening. He was playing for Real Betis against Barcelona — came on for the last twenty-six minutes of a 5-2 defeat. The crime occurred three days after his teammate Antony was robbed. Former Sevilla goalkeeper Sergio Rico, tied up at gunpoint with his family at the dinner table Monday night. His mother-in-law with a gun to her head. His influencer wife, two children, three-month-old baby. The Civil Guard says they're confident. The Aljarafe area is susceptible, they say. Several gangs arrested before. Nothing was taken from Parrott's house. Attempted robbery. The investigation is ongoing. I think of the footballer, twenty-four, signed from AZ Alkmaar in the summer, new house, new country, new league. Playing against Barcelona. Coming on as a sub. Losing 5-2. And somewhere, miles away, masked men in his living room. The contrast — the lights of the stadium, the roar, the millions watching, and the silence of a house in El Zaudin, the flash of CCTV, the security staff alerting police. Modern life. The money follows the talent. The danger follows the money. The game plays on.

The weather holds. Nine degrees. The drizzle may return. The wind may pick up. Storm Boelo tracking toward the North Sea now, its work done. The country breathes out. Power restored. Trees cleared. Roads reopened. The Shannon recedes. In Salthill they're sweeping sand off the promenade. In Clifden they're checking the caravans. In Leitrim they're pumping water from the fields. Life resumes. It always does. The storm is a punctuation mark. A comma, perhaps. Not a full stop.

I make coffee. The grinder wakes the house properly. The smell spreads through the kitchen, into the hall, up the stairs. A ritual. The beans are Ethiopian, light roast, floral notes that promise more than they deliver. The water hits the grounds. The bloom. The pour. The wait. The first sip — bitter, warm, alive. This is the morning. Not the news. Not the storm. Not the funeral at eleven. Not the forty years. Not the bouzouki. Not the busk. Not the masked men in Seville. This cup. This chair. This light falling thin and pale across the table. The blackbird calls again. Once. Twice. The house settles. The heating clicks off.

At eleven I will not be at St. John's Cathedral. I did not know Jamie Bailey. But I will think of him. Of the footpath on Blackhorse Avenue. Of the Phoenix Park wall. Of the deer in the morning mist. Of a grandfather who laughed easily. Of daughters and a granddaughter. Of a character. Of the thread pulled loose.

At some point today I will walk past Coláiste Éanna. Or down Ballyroan Road. Or through the laneway off Ann Devlin Road. I will look at the ivy on the walls. I will think of a boy in a grey jacket with black shoulder corners. Of a bag left in shadow. Of sixteen hundred lines of inquiry. Of a family still waiting.

Tonight, perhaps, I will put on a Planxty record. Or The Bothy Band. Or Moving Hearts. I will hear the bouzouki — his bouzouki, the one he designed — and I will think of a boy in Tullamore, then Newbridge, tickling trout in the Liffey, climbing trees for birds' nests. Sixty-six years of friendship. A lifetime. The music carries on.

The city wakes. A bin lorry rumbles past. A door slams somewhere. Footsteps on the pavement. The ordinary Monday. The light strengthens, fraction by fraction. The grey lifts a shade. The drizzle holds off. For now. For this moment. The coffee is good. The chair is comfortable. The blackbird sings a third time, a full phrase this time, liquid and clear, and the morning opens like a door.
