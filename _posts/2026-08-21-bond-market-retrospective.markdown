Government bond markets across the world's major economies have spent the past two days flashing warning signals. From Washington to London to Frankfurt, long-term borrowing costs have climbed to levels not seen in years — in some cases, decades. The common thread isn't just inflation or growth. It's a dawning realization that fiscal trajectories are unsustainable, central banks are sending mixed signals, and the era of predictable, low-cost government borrowing may be over.

**United States: The 5% Threshold Looms**

The most dramatic moves have been in US Treasuries. On Tuesday, the yield on the 30-year Treasury bond touched 5.26%, its highest since June 2007. The 10-year yield, the benchmark for everything from mortgages to corporate loans, hovered around 4.71% — a level that in past cycles has drawn official attention.

What's driving the selloff? Three forces, all pushing in the same direction. First, the fiscal picture keeps deteriorating. The Congressional Budget Office last week raised its deficit forecast to $2.1 trillion for the current year, $200 billion above its February estimate. With federal debt nearing $40 trillion, the Treasury must issue ever-larger amounts of debt, and buyers are demanding higher compensation.

Second, corporate borrowing — especially from technology giants building out AI infrastructure — is crowding out sovereign demand. Microsoft and other hyperscalers have flooded the market with bonds, some carrying better credit ratings than the US government itself. Investors have finite capital; every dollar going to corporate debt is a dollar not buying Treasuries.

Third, and perhaps most unsettling, is uncertainty about the Federal Reserve under new Chair Kevin Warsh. Warsh has signaled skepticism toward "forward guidance" — the practice of telegraphing policy intentions to markets. Without that anchor, traders have priced in a larger "uncertainty premium." BofA strategist Mark Cabana put it bluntly: "There is literally a price to be paid for the lack of guidance that Warsh seems so set on. And the price is higher interest rates and a higher cost to the taxpayer."

The Treasury Department, under Secretary Scott Bessent, has responded with an unusual intervention: expanding its buyback program for long-dated debt, doubling the maximum operation size from $2 billion to at least $4 billion. The stated goal is improving liquidity in off-the-run securities. But critics warn the move effectively manipulates the yield curve by swapping long-term bonds for short-term bills, potentially stoking inflation and pressuring the Fed to accommodate fiscal needs — a dynamic some economists describe as fiscal dominance creeping into monetary policy.

All eyes now turn to Jackson Hole, Wyoming, where Warsh will deliver his first major address as Fed chair. Markets are pricing in rate cuts by September, but the bond market's message is less sanguine: long-term yields are rising even as short-term rate expectations fall, a classic "bear steepening" that signals concern about the long-run fiscal and inflation outlook.

**United Kingdom: A Split Committee, Rising Yields**

Across the Atlantic, the Bank of England delivered its own surprise on August 7. The Monetary Policy Committee voted 5–4 to cut rates by 25 basis points — but the split was historic. Four members voted to hold rates steady, while one dissenter wanted a larger 50-basis-point cut. It was the first time in the MPC's history that a decision required a second vote to break a deadlock.

The message from the committee was clear: inflation remains the primary concern. The BoE's updated projections show headline CPI peaking at 4% in the coming months, even as the labor market weakens. Governor Andrew Bailey acknowledged "genuine uncertainty now about the course of that direction of rates."

Markets reacted instantly. The 2-year gilt yield jumped 5 basis points to 3.88%; the 10-year rose above 4.57%. Traders who had fully priced in another cut this year pulled back — the implied probability dropped to around 75%. The pound strengthened, a typical reaction when rate expectations firm relative to peers.

Compounding the picture, the BoE's annual quantitative tightening review revealed that its bond-sale programme has likely added 15–25 basis points to 10-year gilt yields — slightly more than the 10–20 basis points estimated a year ago. Since 2022, the BoE has reduced its gilt holdings by over £300 billion. Analysts at Citi now expect the annual QT pace to be trimmed from £100 billion to £75 billion in September, with longer-dated sales potentially curtailed entirely given the sharp rise in 30-year yields (up nearly a full percentage point over the past year).

For UK borrowers, the math is stark: mortgage rates track gilt yields, and the government's own borrowing costs are climbing just as fiscal pressures mount ahead of the Autumn Budget.

**Europe: Bunds at Multi-Year Highs, Periphery Holds — For Now**

In the eurozone, the selloff has been broad-based. Germany's 10-year Bund yield touched its highest since 2011. French 10-year yields hit levels last seen in 2008. Even the traditionally resilient periphery — Italy, Spain, Portugal — has seen spreads tighten less than core yields have risen, meaning absolute borrowing costs are up everywhere.

The European Central Bank held rates steady at its July 24 meeting, with President Christine Lagarde describing the economy as in a "good place" and growth "in line with projections or a little bit better." That upbeat assessment prompted markets to slash expectations for autumn rate cuts — from fully priced to roughly 80% probability, with some economists now seeing no cuts at all until 2026. Short-dated German yields posted their largest daily rise in two months.

Yet beneath the surface, a reordering is underway. French spreads over Bunds have widened to levels implying an 'A-' rating — three notches below France's actual 'AA-' — as political instability and fiscal slippage weigh. Meanwhile, Italian BTP spreads have compressed to their tightest since 2021, benefiting from relative political stability and hopes of rating upgrades. The old core-periphery hierarchy is fracturing.

The ECB's quantitative tightening — the end of pandemic-era bond reinvestments — adds a structural headwind. With the Transmission Protection Mechanism (TPI) as a backstop, the ECB has tools to contain disorderly spreads, but the era of unlimited central bank support is receding.

Geopolitics compounds the fiscal strain. The Iran conflict has pushed oil back above $90, reviving inflation fears just as central banks hoped to declare victory. Foreign holders of US Treasuries — led by Japan — have been reducing positions, raising the specter that currency intervention could trigger further bond sales.

**Why It Matters**

Bond yields are the price of money across time. When they rise, mortgages get more expensive, corporate investment slows, government interest bills balloon, and equity valuations compress. The current move isn't a routine cyclical adjustment — it reflects a structural shift: higher deficits, more volatile geopolitics, less central bank transparency, and a global savings glut being absorbed by AI capex rather than sovereign debt.

For households, it means higher borrowing costs. For governments, it means a larger share of tax revenue going to interest rather than services. For investors, it means the "risk-free" rate is no longer low — and the risk premium for everything else must adjust.

The next two weeks will be pivotal. Warsh at Jackson Hole, the BoE's September QT decision, and the ECB's next policy meeting will collectively shape whether this selloff stabilizes or accelerates. One thing is clear: the bond market is no longer taking fiscal and policy discipline for granted.
