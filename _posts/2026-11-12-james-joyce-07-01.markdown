Seven degrees. Partly cloudy. Wind thirteen kilometres an hour from somewhere that doesn't matter. Met Éireann's language — "mixed conditions across regions" — reads like a horoscope for people who commute. I stood at the window at six forty. The light was that particular November grey that doesn't promise rain but carries it in its pockets. The trees on the quays don't move much. A gull rides the current above the Samuel Beckett Bridge, wings fixed, barely a feather twitching. The cables don't harp today. Not enough wind.

The air tastes of wet leaf and diesel and something older. Coal smoke from a century ago, maybe, still in the brick. My father would have called it a soft day. He'd have said it standing at the kitchen window in his socks, mug in hand, reading the same sky I'm reading now. The furrow between his brows. The paper does not remain.

Inside the Public Accounts Committee report, six hundred and seven million euro overspent on international protection accommodation in twenty twenty-four. Two point three billion total. One point six billion on accommodation alone — one hundred and fifty percent over the four hundred and nine million allocated. Thirty-three thousand beds where seven thousand once were. Eighty-four euro a night per applicant in twenty twenty-four. Ninety-seven in twenty twenty-five. The numbers sit in the report like stones in a pocket. You feel their weight walking past the International Protection Office on Mount Street. Five thousand people still in centres after being granted status. Five hundred and fifty-nine individuals and twenty-nine families who left IPAS and presented to homeless services in twenty twenty-four. Four hundred and fifty in twenty twenty-five. Twenty-five percent of presentations. The department says they don't allow exits to homelessness. People present for reasons unrelated. The language of institutions is always clean. The lives are not.

I walk Talbot Street at seven fifteen. The shutters on Pizza Max are down. Closed since October twenty-ninth. "Extensive active food infestation" — the Food Safety Authority's phrase, clinical as a coroner's report. Rodent droppings under the chest freezers in the dough preparation areas. Dough being prepared at the time. Gnawed packaging. Food likely unfit for human consumption. The smell of yeast and tomato and garlic that used to hit you at the corner of Marlborough Street — gone. The Smoky Island in Cork. JJ Ramen in Stillorgan. The list grows. Greg Dempsey of the FSAI calls it deeply concerning. The Christmas period approaching. Robust procedures. The language of prevention. But the shutters are down now, and the rats don't read reports.

At the corner of Talbot and North Earl Street, a woman sells roses from a bucket. Red. White. Pink. She wears a coat that has seen winters. Her fingers are rough. She doesn't look at the Pizza Max shutters. She looks at the feet passing. Seven degrees. The wind finds the gaps in her gloves.

Radie Peat's voice comes through the headphones. "No Human Tongue Can Tell" — the album drops tomorrow. Rough Trade. Vinyl, CD, digital. She plays Vicar Street on the twentieth. I heard her on "Hares on the Mountain" — the old folk song alchemised. The female has all the power in the song. Courtship with a hint of violence. Hunting imagery. Guns. Natural cycles. She was pregnant when she started recording. Had the baby. Became a mother. Came back to the music. The producer had a baby too. Five years of coming away and coming back. Layer upon layer of her own voice on her own voice. Trapped in her own brain, she calls it. Seeing strengths, weaknesses, blind spots. The title track — "Luisne" — the first blush of light before dawn. Rebirth. A birth into a different way of doing music. Not collaborative. Solo. The shadow work of making something alone. You interact with versions of yourself. Past selves. Future selves. The domino effect of creation — one woman's voice representing the voices of many through the ages. Communicating hundreds of years of other people's experiences, absorbed from generations before you. Binds you with a worldliness beyond the sum of your own experience.

I think of my father's furrow. The numbers in the PAC report. The rats under the freezer. The rose seller's rough fingers. Radie Peat layering her voice over her voice over five years. The city holds all of it at once. The overspend and the album. The closure order and the first blush of light before dawn. The strike suspended — ten point seven five percent over two years and nine months, running to March twenty twenty-nine. Work-to-rule since September thirtieth. Strike on October fourteenth. Planned for today, Thursday, the same day as the European Political Community summit. Suspended in the early hours. Ballots to come. Jack Chambers calls it fiscally sustainable. The unions meet to analyse. The language of agreements. The language of compromise. The language that keeps the city functioning.

At the newsagent on Aston Quay, the headlines don't change. Crime. Weather. Housing. The same words in different orders. I buy nothing. The bridge still harps when the wind rises. The Liffey still carries its brown silence.

A man in a hi-vis vest checks his phone at the Luas stop. The green snake clatters past. The woman with the roses wraps her coat tighter. Seven degrees. Partly cloudy. The light shifts, hazier now, gilding the spires and the cranes alike. The gull banks, turns, catches a thermal I cannot feel.

Some mornings the city feels like a sentence you're still learning to read. Today it reads like numbers and roses and a woman's voice layered five years deep. Today it reads like the first blush of light before dawn.

I turn for home. The city breathes. I breathe with it.
