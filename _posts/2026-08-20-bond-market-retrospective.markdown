Over the past two days, government bond markets across the US, UK, and Europe have seen a familiar but uneasy pattern: yields — the effective interest rate governments pay to borrow — have climbed to multi-year highs. For anyone with a mortgage, a pension, or savings, this matters because bond yields set the baseline for borrowing costs across the economy.

**United States: The 30-Year Hits a 19-Year High**

The biggest headline came from the US, where the 30-year Treasury yield touched 5.33% on 18 August — its highest level since 2007. The 10-year yield, which anchors everything from mortgage rates to corporate loans, sat at 4.64% on 20 August.

Two forces are pushing yields up. First, the US fiscal picture keeps deteriorating. The Treasury reported that July's monthly deficit was the largest since March, adding to a debt pile that already exceeds $36 trillion. More borrowing means more bonds flooding the market, and buyers demand higher yields to absorb that supply.

Second, inflation hasn't been vanquished. While the Federal Reserve has held its policy rate steady in recent meetings, traders worry that sticky services inflation and a resilient labour force could force the Fed to keep rates higher for longer — or even hike again. The market is pricing in fewer rate cuts than it was a month ago.

**United Kingdom: Gilts Follow the US Lead, But With Local Twists**

UK 10-year gilt yields eased to around 5.0% on 18 August, dipping slightly as US yields paused. But the underlying pressure remains. The Bank of England has signalled that rate cuts will be gradual, and the UK's own fiscal plans — including higher borrowing to fund investment — keep gilt supply elevated.

A specific UK concern is the Middle East. While energy markets started the week calmly, the risk of a wider conflict that disrupts oil and gas supplies has not gone away. The UK imports a significant share of its energy, so any spike in global prices feeds directly into British inflation — and that keeps the Bank of England cautious about cutting rates too quickly.

**Europe: Bunds at 15-Year Peaks, France at 2009 Levels**

In the eurozone, Germany's 10-year Bund yield climbed above 3.25% — a 15-year high. France's equivalent yield hit 4.10%, its highest since June 2009. The driver is similar to the US: fiscal spending is rising across the continent, particularly in Germany where a new infrastructure fund is being debated, and in France where political fragmentation makes deficit reduction harder.

But there's a silver lining. The spread — the gap — between German Bunds and Italian 10-year bonds has narrowed from 116 basis points at the start of the year to around 72 basis points. This suggests investors are less worried about a eurozone fragmentation crisis than they were in 2022–23. The ECB's "transmission protection" backstop, though untested, continues to provide a psychological floor.

**The Common Thread: Geopolitics Meets Fiscal Reality**

What ties these three markets together? Two things. First, governments everywhere are spending more — on defence, on green transition, on ageing populations — and bond markets are demanding a higher price to finance it. Second, the Middle East conflict has introduced a fresh inflation wildcard. If oil prices surge, central banks may have to keep rates restrictive even as growth slows.

For now, the message from bond markets is clear: the era of ultra-cheap borrowing is over, and the new normal for yields is higher than most policymakers — and households — have planned for.
