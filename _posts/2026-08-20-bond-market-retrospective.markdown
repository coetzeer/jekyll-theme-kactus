Government bond markets across the United States, United Kingdom, and eurozone experienced significant volatility over the past two days, driven by a mix of geopolitical tension, fiscal policy moves, and shifting expectations about central bank interest rate paths. The common thread: yields — the interest rates governments pay to borrow — have been climbing toward multi-year highs, prompting policy responses from finance ministries and central banks alike.

## United States: Treasury Steps In as Yields Spike

The biggest story came from Washington, where Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced an expanded bond buyback operation on August 19, aiming to calm a selloff that had pushed 30-year Treasury yields to their highest level since 2007. The Treasury said it would at least double the size of its regular buybacks, targeting longer-dated debt — bonds maturing in 10 to 30 years — that had been under heavy selling pressure since late June.

The immediate reaction was dramatic: yields tumbled and stock market futures surged. The 30-year yield, which had topped 5.31% just days earlier — a 19-year high — dropped sharply on the news. However, the rally proved short-lived. By midday Wednesday, the 10-year yield was already inching higher again, underscoring skepticism about whether buybacks alone can reverse the underlying forces pushing rates up.

Those forces are threefold. First, the US federal deficit continues to widen, meaning the Treasury must issue ever-larger amounts of new debt. Second, corporations have been borrowing heavily, adding to the supply of bonds competing for investor money. Third, uncertainty about the Federal Reserve's next moves under Chair Kevin Warsh has left investors demanding a higher "term premium" — extra yield to compensate for the risk of holding long-term bonds when policy direction is unclear.

Bessent's buyback expansion is essentially a liquidity operation: by purchasing existing long-term bonds, the Treasury reduces the amount investors must absorb, temporarily easing upward pressure on yields. But as Politico noted, Wall Street sees this as a "drop in the bucket" compared to the trillion-dollar scale of new issuance. The real test will be whether the Fed signals a pause or pivot on rates, which would address the root cause more directly.

## United Kingdom: Gilts Follow US Lead, Inflation Looms

UK government bonds, known as gilts, mirrored the US pattern. The 10-year gilt yield eased to around 5.0% on August 18, down from a recent peak near 5.1%, largely tracking the move in US Treasuries. The 30-year gilt yield stood at 5.80% on August 19, down 0.03 percentage points from the prior session.

However, the UK faces its own domestic catalyst: the upcoming inflation report. Investors are bracing for headline inflation to accelerate to a four-month high, even as underlying price growth may ease. This creates a dilemma for the Bank of England. Goldman Sachs has highlighted that the Middle East energy shock — specifically the Iran conflict — has sent gilt yields to 18-year highs, and the Bank may need to deliver what Goldman calls a "material about-turn" in monetary policy to prevent inflation expectations from becoming unanchored.

The Bank of England's policy rate currently sits at 3.75%, well below the 10-year gilt yield of roughly 4.8–5.0%. This wide gap means the market is pricing in higher rates ahead, or at least a higher risk premium for holding UK debt. Unlike the US, where the Treasury can deploy buybacks, the UK's Debt Management Office has fewer tools to directly influence yields, leaving the Bank of England as the primary line of defense.

## Europe: Bunds Hit 15-Year Peaks on War Fears

In the eurozone, the benchmark German 10-year Bund yield climbed above 3.25% in mid-August — a 15-year high — while France's 10-year yield rose to 4.10%, its highest since June 2009. The trigger, according to Euronews, was renewed selling pressure across global bond markets amplified by fears that the Iran war could drive up energy prices and reignite inflation.

The European Central Bank (ECB) now faces a familiar bind: if energy prices surge, headline inflation will rise, potentially forcing rate hikes even as economic growth slows. Germany's Bund yield dipped slightly to 3.07% by August 20 but remained near multi-year highs, reflecting elevated energy prices reinforcing expectations of faster inflation and ECB rate hikes.

The periphery is feeling the strain too. French yields at their highest since the financial crisis signal growing concern about fiscal trajectories in major eurozone economies. With the ECB's policy rate at 3.5% and inflation still above target in several member states, the central bank has limited room to ease even if growth falters.

## Why This Matters

For anyone with a mortgage, car loan, or business credit line, these yield moves are not abstract. Long-term government bond yields serve as the benchmark for consumer and corporate borrowing costs. When the 10-year Treasury yield rises, mortgage rates typically follow within days. The same transmission operates in the UK and Europe.

The past 48 hours illustrate a broader shift: after years of ultra-low rates, governments now face a structurally higher cost of borrowing. Fiscal deficits are larger, geopolitical risks are elevated, and central banks are less willing to suppress yields through quantitative easing. The Treasury's buyback operation, the Bank of England's inflation dilemma, and the ECB's energy-price exposure all point to a new era where bond markets demand higher compensation for risk — and policymakers have fewer tools to push back.
