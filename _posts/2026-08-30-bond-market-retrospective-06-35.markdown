# Bond Markets Retrospective: US, UK, and Europe — August 30, 2026

## Executive Summary

Global bond markets have been dominated by the Jackson Hole symposium this week, particularly the keynote speech by Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh. The message from Washington, echoed in London and Frankfurt, is one of heightened caution. Investors are grappling with the reality that inflation, while showing minor improvements, remains structurally stubborn. This has led to a coordinated sell-off in long-dated government debt as markets adjust their expectations for a "higher for longer" interest rate environment.

---

## United States: The Warsh Effect

The US Treasury market saw a significant "bear flattening" following Chairman Kevin Warsh’s Jackson Hole address. While yields on the short end pushed higher as the market priced in potential further Fed action, the long end also felt the pressure. Warsh acknowledged some better-than-expected inflation readings but explicitly stated that underlying trends have not improved enough to signal a change in trajectory. Consequently, 10-year Treasury yields finished August 28th at 4.73%, reflecting a market that is no longer betting on a quick pivot to easing.

## United Kingdom: Persistent Fiscal Worries

UK gilts remain stuck above the 5% mark. The market is digesting both the global inflationary backdrop and domestic concerns regarding fiscal policy. With the budget ahead, investors are demanding a premium to hold long-dated UK paper, fearing that government borrowing requirements will continue to overwhelm supply.

## Europe: Bunds Test 15-Year Highs

German Bunds, often the benchmark for European debt, have been trading near 15-year highs in yield terms. Persistent energy price volatility and concerns about the European Central Bank’s path have kept Bunds vulnerable. Investors are weighing the risks of sustained inflation against a slowing European economy, and for now, the bias is toward higher yields.

---

## The Path Forward

The overarching theme across all three regions is the rejection of the previous era of easy, cheap debt. Central banks are maintaining a hawkish stance, and investors are demanding higher term premiums. For borrowers, the implications are clear: the cost of capital has structurally reset, and elevated rates are likely to persist as the new baseline.
