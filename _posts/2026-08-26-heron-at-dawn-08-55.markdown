The hosepipe ban stays. That was the word this morning, scrolling through the phone while the kettle clicked. Dublin and twelve other counties — the map of thirst spreading westward from the Wicklow foothills. Nineteen degrees they promised, five hours of sunshine if you believe the forecasters, but the rain that fell overnight has already vanished into cracked soil and the city's old pipes. You can smell it in the air, that particular Dublin scent after rain on hot pavement: tar and diesel and something older, something that remembers when the Liffey ran cleaner.

I walked the quays at seven. The light was that pale gold only late August knows how to make, thin and stretched, gilding the Custom House dome and catching in the ripples where the river turns toward the sea. A heron stood motionless at the water's edge, one grey feather out of place. The Samuel Beckett Bridge harped its cables in the light breeze, someone's idea of a lyric made steel. Tourists with wheeled cases clustered at the Famine Memorial, reading the plaques in languages I didn't recognise. The bronze figures walk forever toward ships that sailed generations ago.

National Heritage Week, the posters say. History made accessible, digestible, scheduled. The Dublin Horse Show opens at the RDS — three days of polished leather and braided manes, the click of hooves on sand, money changing hands in ways that have nothing to do with betting slips. I passed the venue yesterday. Grooms in navy polos wiped sweat from necks of animals worth more than the houses their great-grandparents were evicted from. The class system survives in bloodlines now, equine and human both.

Tonight Earl Sweatshirt plays Vicar Street. The posters have been up since spring, that stark typography, the name alone enough. I'll go. The venue holds maybe fifteen hundred, low ceiling, the sound piling up in the rafters. He doesn't tour much. When he does, the songs arrive different — slower, heavier, the spaces between bars stretching until they become the point. Last time he played Dublin he didn't say much between tracks. Just let the beats breathe. The crowd knew the words anyway. We always know the words here.

Friday brings the Americans to Aviva Stadium. TCU versus North Carolina, the Aer Lingus College Football Classic. Forty thousand tickets sold, they say. Helmets and padding on a pitch that usually sees eighteen men chase a ball with their feet. The grass has been relaid. The lines painted fresh. I've nothing against the spectacle — sport is sport, ceremony is ceremony — but there's something strange about importing a ritual that means nothing here and watching the city pretend it always belonged. The pubs will fill early. The jerseys will be unfamiliar colours. By Saturday morning the pitch will be scarred in ways the groundskeepers will curse.

A community meeting tonight about affordable housing at Francis Ranch. Two sessions, six and eight, same information both times. The survey for naming the new linear park closes September tenth. I'll probably forget to vote. The city builds and builds — cranes like prayer wheels against the skyline — and the people who taught me how to pour a proper pint, how to pronounce *síle* so it doesn't sound like *seal*, how to navigate the Luas without looking at a map, they're moving to Kildare, to Meath, to the commuter belts where the rent is a fraction and the commute eats two hours a day.

The Lobster Festival starts in Dalkey this weekend. I've never been. Always meant to. The Dart ride out along the coast, the cliffs at Killiney, the harbour where U2 recorded a video once and everyone still points. Maybe this year. Maybe the rain holds off. The sea temperature is fifteen degrees — cold enough to take your breath, clean enough to wash the city off your skin.

The kettle has boiled twice now. I should make tea. The light has shifted, deeper, the gold turning toward something closer to honey. The heron is gone. Somewhere a church bell strikes seven, then eight, the sound travelling over rooftops and satellite dishes and the new builds with their floor-to-ceiling windows that reflect a sky they don't let you touch.

This is the city. Thirsty and building and remembering and forgetting, all at once. I'll walk the quays again this evening, before the show. The river will be darker then, reflecting streetlights instead of sky. The heron will be somewhere else, or the same place, doing what herons do — waiting, perfectly still, for the moment to strike.
