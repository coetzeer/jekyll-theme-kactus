The kettle clicks off. Steam rises in a thin column, catching the pale light that seeps through the curtain gap — nine degrees, the weather report said, light drizzle, four kilometres an hour wind from the southwest. Nine point seven, to be precise. The kind of precision that means nothing when you step outside and feel the wet cold settle in the hollow of your throat.

I have been sitting here since six, watching the street wake. Marlborough Street. The number 16 bus groans past, tyres hissing on wet tarmac. A woman in a red raincoat hurries past the newsagent's, umbrella tilted against a rain that isn't really rain yet — more a suspension, a dampness that hangs in the air like breath held too long. Her shoes slap the pavement. *Slap, slap, slap.* The rhythm of a city that never quite dried out from October.

The light at this hour. Grey-white, directionless. It doesn't fall so much as accumulate. It pools in the gutters, reflects off the wet cobblestones near the old fruit market, turns the Liffey into something that looks like brushed steel. You could photograph it and call it *Marlborough Street, 07:03, November* and no one would know the difference between this morning and the one three years ago when I stood in the same spot watching a seagull pull a chip wrapper from a bin.

Memory is not in the photograph. Memory is in the cold that bit my fingers that morning, the smell of yeast from Brennan's bakery on the wind, the way the light caught the edge of a pigeon's wing — *that* pigeon, *that* wing, *that* morning. The photograph only holds the shell.

---

The phone on the table lights up. A message from my sister in Wivenhoe. *Cold here too. 3.9. Fog thick enough to lose the estuary.* I type back: *9.7 here. Drizzle. The usual.* She sends a thumbs-up emoji. We are forty years old and this is how we speak now. Thumbs up. Weather reports. The estuary lost in fog.

Wivenhoe. I haven't been in three years. The last time, my father was still walking the seawall at low tide, picking up shards of blue glass the sea had rounded. *Sea glass,* he called it. *The sea makes jewels of our rubbish.* He'd hold a piece up to the November light — the same grey-white light, but there it came off the water, doubled, and his eyes would crinkle at the corners. He died in February. The seawall is just a wall now. The glass is just glass.

---

Three articles I read yesterday from the news feed. They sit in the mind like stones in a pocket.

*First:* A WhatsApp scam. "Vote for Mia." A link sent from a contact you trust — your sister, your cousin, the lad you sat beside in fifth year — asking you to vote for a child named Mia in a competition that doesn't exist. Click the link and your account is theirs. Your contacts become targets. The Gardaí in Kildare put out the warning: *Think before you click. One click could compromise your account.*

I think of the woman in the red raincoat. Her phone probably buzzes in her pocket right now. A message from "Mam" or "Sarah" or "Dave from the pub." *Vote for Mia please x.* The thumb hovers. The rain makes the screen slick. She clicks. She doesn't click. The difference between a morning like any other and a morning where your life spills out into the hands of someone you'll never see, sitting in a room somewhere, scrolling through your sister's wedding photos, your father's last voice note, the group chat where you all still call each other by nicknames from school.

*One click.* The phrase has a literary quality. Joyce would have liked it. *One click.* The fall compressed into a gesture. The apple, the coin, the link. *Think before you click.* As if thinking were a shield. As if the scam didn't know exactly how we think — through trust, through affection, through the small daily pulse of *someone needs me, someone thought of me.*

---

*Second:* Ciara Mageean. Thirty-four. Stage four cancer diagnosed May 2025. Married her partner Thomas in October at Darver Castle. Died November 1st at 6:30 a.m., surrounded by loved ones. *Ar dheis Dé go raibh a hanam.*

I never met her. I watched her run the 1500 in Rio on a screen in a pub on Camden Street. The pint settled. The commentary washed over us. *The Portaferry flyer,* they called her. She ran like she was chasing something the rest of us couldn't see. Tokyo 2020. European gold in Rome. Paris 2024 — a calf injury at the last minute. The shape of a career written in near-misses and one moment of perfection in Rome.

She spoke to Brendan O'Connor in May. *I'll never forget that day on the 20th of May. Going in to have what I thought was a routine little scope...*

A routine little scope. The words sit in the throat. How many of us have thought that? *Routine. Little. Just a check.* And the surgeon shakes his head. *No, it's not great. This is not a good diagnosis, Ciara.*

*This isn't good, is it?* Her words. A question that already knows the answer.

She was thirty-four. I am forty. Six years. Six Novembers. Six times the light has fallen on Marlborough Street at 07:03, six times the kettle has clicked, six times the 16 bus has hissed past. She got thirty-four. Her husband got a wedding in October and a widowhood in November. Her sister Máire got the memory of that room, the surgeon's head shaking, the words *stage four* landing like stones in water.

The light doesn't care. It falls on the wet cobblestones the same way. It pools in the gutters. It catches the edge of a pigeon's wing. *Ar dheis Dé go raibh a hanam.*

---

*Third:* Six planes. Bomb threats. A Ryanair flight from Dublin — FR9611 — escorted by Italian Air Force jets into Rome Fiumicino. Another from Manchester to Murcia. Two into Warsaw. One into Sofia. Six flights across Europe, all on the same Tuesday. Nothing found. All clear. The threats were empty. The fear was not.

I think of the passengers on FR9611. Boarding in Dublin. The usual chaos — overhead lockers, the safety demonstration nobody watches, the seatbelt sign, the acceleration, the lift. Then the announcement. *Ladies and gentlemen, we are being escorted by military aircraft as a precaution.* The Italian Air Force jets alongside, grey shapes in the cloud. The passengers look out. Some pray. Some text. Some stare at the seatback in front of them, counting the holes in the fabric, waiting for the world to make sense again.

Nothing found. *All clear.* The planes land. The passengers disembark. They collect their bags. They go to hotels, to homes, to meetings. They carry the morning with them. The grey shapes beside the window. The announcement. The moment the ordinary cracked.

Six planes. One Tuesday. The coordination suggests something — someone — testing the system. Probing. Or just chaos for chaos's sake. The identity of those responsible remains unclear. *Remains unclear.* The phrase that closes every story like this. The unclear remains. We walk through it.

---

The kettle has gone cold. I pour anyway. The tea is bitter, over-steeped. I drink it standing at the window.

The woman in the red raincoat is gone. The 16 bus is gone. A cyclist passes, water spraying from the back wheel in a perfect arc. *There,* I think. *That arc. That is the morning.*

The New Year's Festival lineup was announced too. Three days, December 30 to January 1. Aaron Rowe, The Academic, Sharon Shannon's Big Band, Jerry Fish, Camille O'Sullivan. Dublin Castle. Meeting House Square. The Guinness NYF Music Trail through Fibbers, Whelan's, The Workman's Club. Free events. Ticketed events. A procession through the streets with marching bands and street performers. *A touch of New Year magic,* the press release says.

Orla Carroll from Fáilte Ireland: *showcasing the very best of Ireland's culture, live music and entertainment.* Kevin "Boxer" Moran: *creativity, energy and atmosphere.*

The words are smooth. They slide off the tongue. *Culture. Entertainment. Atmosphere.* They don't mention the drizzle. They don't mention the scam links. They don't mention the thirty-four-year-old runner who won't see the fireworks over Dublin Castle. They don't mention the passengers on FR9611 who will flinch at every announcement for the rest of their lives.

But the festival will happen. The lights will go up. The bands will march. The music will play. People will stand in the cold with plastic cups of mulled wine, breath pluming, cheeks pink, and for a few hours the unclear will recede. The light will fall on the spires of Dublin Castle, on the cobblestones of Meeting House Square, on the faces turned up to the fireworks — *white, gold, green, red* — and the city will hold itself together with noise and light and the simple, stubborn act of showing up.

---

My father used to say: *The sea makes jewels of our rubbish.*

He meant the glass. The bottles thrown overboard a hundred years ago, the jars, the plates, the things we threw away because they were broken or used up or simply no longer wanted. The sea took them, tumbled them, rounded their edges, frosted their surfaces, and gave them back as treasures. Blue glass. Green glass. Rare red glass from old port bottles. *Sea glass.* He had a jar of it on the windowsill in Wivenhoe. Caught the light.

I think perhaps the city does the same thing. It takes our broken mornings — the scam clicks, the diagnoses, the bomb scares, the empty threats, the thirty-four years that should have been eighty — and it tumbles them. The light falls. The rain falls. The buses hiss. The cyclists arc water. The festivals are announced. The processions process. The music plays.

And somewhere in the tumbling, the sharp edges wear down. Not gone. Never gone. But rounded. Frosted. The kind of thing you might pick up on a seawall in November, hold to the grey-white light, and see — for a moment — something that looks like meaning.

*Ar dheis Dé go raibh a hanam.*

---

The tea is finished. The mug sits on the windowsill, a brown ring on the wood. The light has shifted — slightly warmer now, slightly more directional. The drizzle has stopped. The air holds that post-rain clarity, the smell of wet brick and exhaust and something floral from the window boxes three floors up.

My phone buzzes. My sister again. *Dad's jar of sea glass. Mum found it in the cupboard. She's sending it to me.*

I type: *Keep the blue pieces. He always said the blue ones were rarest.*

Thumbs up emoji. The estuary is still lost in fog.

I put on my coat. The 07:45 bus. The city waits. The light falls. The day begins.
