Government bond yields across the major advanced economies moved higher over the past two trading sessions, extending a trend that has been building through the summer. From Washington to London to Frankfurt, the message from the bond market is consistent: investors are demanding more compensation to lend to governments, and the reasons trace back to a mix of sticky inflation, heavy government borrowing, and central banks that are no longer buying bonds to suppress yields.

**United States: The 10-Year Touches 4.75%**

The benchmark US 10-year Treasury yield climbed to roughly 4.75% on August 18, its highest level since January 2025. Short-term rates are anchored by the Federal Reserve's policy rate, which sits at 3.75%. But longer-term yields reflect something different: the "term premium" — the extra return investors want for locking money up for a decade.

Three forces are pushing that premium up. First, the US federal deficit remains enormous, and the Treasury must issue ever-larger volumes of debt to fund it. More supply means lower prices and higher yields, all else equal. Second, corporate borrowing has surged as companies lock in financing before rates potentially rise further, competing with the government for investor cash. Third, the Fed under new Chair Kevin Warsh has signaled less willingness to use its balance sheet to cap yields, leaving the market to find its own clearing level.

The practical upshot: mortgage rates, corporate loan pricing, and the cost of financing the national debt all move in tandem with the 10-year. Households looking to buy homes and companies planning capital expenditure face a higher hurdle.

**United Kingdom: Gilts Outpace Peers**

UK 10-year gilt yields stood at 5.08% on August 18, with the 30-year at 5.83%. Both are materially above their US and German counterparts. The Bank of England's policy rate is 3.75%, and headline inflation has fallen from 3.3% to 2.6% — yet long-term yields remain elevated.

Two UK-specific factors explain the gap. The Bank of England's quantitative tightening (QT) programme — actively selling the bonds it bought during the pandemic — is now estimated to add 0.15 to 0.25 percentage points to the 10-year yield, up from the previous estimate of 0.1 to 0.2 points. At the same time, the Debt Management Office has been issuing shorter-dated debt, reducing the average maturity of new gilt supply from over 20 years a decade ago to below 10 years today. Shorter maturities mean more frequent refinancing and greater sensitivity to short-term rate moves.

For UK borrowers, this translates directly into higher fixed-rate mortgage pricing and more expensive government borrowing. For the Chancellor, it narrows fiscal room for maneuver.

**Europe: Bunds Rise, Periphery Spreads Hold**

Germany's 10-year Bund yield reached 3.26% on August 18, up 0.11 points over the past month and 0.51 points higher than a year ago. The European Central Bank's policy rate sits at 2.25%, with inflation projected at 3.0% for 2026 before falling to 2.3% in 2027 and 2.0% in 2028.

France's 10-year OAT yield climbed to 4.12%, widening the spread over Bunds to roughly 86 basis points — up from around 68 points a month ago. Italy's 10-year BTP yield hit 4.06%, a spread of about 80 basis points over Germany. Both peripherals have seen yields rise in line with core Europe, suggesting the move is driven by common ECB policy expectations and global term-premium dynamics rather than renewed credit fears.

The ECB's June meeting maintained a data-dependent stance, and the July 23 decision kept rates unchanged. Markets are pricing limited further cuts this year, which keeps a floor under European yields.

**Why It Matters**

Bond yields are the price of time and risk. When they rise across the US, UK, and Europe simultaneously, it signals a global repricing of what it costs to borrow for the long term. For governments, higher yields mean more tax revenue goes to interest payments and less to services or tax cuts. For households, it means pricier mortgages and car loans. For companies, it raises the hurdle rate for new investment. And for investors, it creates both losses on existing bond holdings and better entry points for new money.

The common thread: after years of central banks suppressing term premiums through quantitative easing, the reverse process — quantitative tightening and reduced balance sheets — is letting those premiums normalize. The result is a bond market that looks more like the pre-2008 era than the post-2008 one. Adjusting to that reality is the work of this cycle.
