The air this morning carries twelve and a half degrees of something that isn't quite cold and isn't quite mild. Mainly clear, the forecast said. Eight point three kilometres per hour of wind from whatever direction wind comes from when it doesn't announce itself. I stood at the window watching the light spread across the street — pale, thin, the particular grey-white of an October morning that has forgotten summer but hasn't committed to winter. The trees on the green hold their leaves still, though the edges have gone crisp, brown as old manuscript pages.

Twelve point five degrees. In London it's twelve point three. In Wivenhoe, where the estuary breathes, eight point seven. Seattle wears overcast like a wool coat at twelve point six. Austin, Texas basks at twenty-nine point three under clear sky. The same planet, different arrangements of air and light. I think about this sometimes — how the weather writes its own poetry on the skin of the day, and we just live inside the lines.

---

I walked down to Grafton Street before the shops opened. The street was empty save for the early ones — the woman setting out chairs for the café that serves coffee in cups too small for the price, the delivery van blocking the lane with crates of avocados and oat milk, the busker tuning a guitar that has seen more rain than sunshine. And there it stood, the thing they call the Dublin D.

A sculpture, they said. A visual landmark celebrating everything that makes the capital dynamic. Councillor Moore called it a glorified billboard and I think she may have been kind. It sits at the top of the street like a question nobody asked. One side bears a URL — dublin.ie — in lettering large enough to read from the Luas tracks. The other side... I walked around it. The other side is blank. Silver-grey metal, the colour of a sky that can't decide. No artist's name. No plaque. No date. It appeared, the councillors say, with no warning, no consultation, no open call to artists, no selection panel. A mural celebrating mothers breastfeeding took years to navigate the proper channels. This arrived in a press release.

Art is subjective, Moore said. But this isn't art. It's branding wearing a sculpture's coat.

I touched the metal. Cold. Smooth. Impersonal. The wind moved through the street and the thing didn't tremble, didn't breathe. A pigeon landed on top, considered, departed. The URL caught the light. Dublin.ie. As if the city needed a website to tell you what it is. As if the smell of frying rashers from the café, the echo of footsteps on pavement, the way the light falls at 7:43 on an October morning — as if any of that could be captured in a domain name.

The councillors are angry. Rightly. But underneath the procedural outrage — the missing Arts Office consultation, the planning permission questions, the conservation concerns that vanish for advertising but appear for toilets — there's something quieter. A sense that the city is being sold back to itself in pieces. That the things that make Dublin Dublin — the music spilling from a pub on a Tuesday, the particular cadence of a northside accent, the way strangers talk to each other at bus stops — these aren't brand assets. They're the soul. And souls don't scale.

---

The radio in the kitchen this morning carried Waterford. Two adults. Two children. Dead in a house fire on Birchwood Avenue, Kilcohan. Two more children critical in hospital. Five thirteen a.m. Tuesday. The fire service got the call at five thirteen. A well-developed fire, they called it. A two-storey house. The town plunged into utter devastation and sadness, the Minister said. The Taoiseach called it deeply disturbing.

I stood at the sink, a cup of tea going cold in my hands, listening to words that have been spoken before and will be spoken again. Utter devastation. Deeply disturbing. Thoughts and prayers. The community will rally. Kindness and solidarity. The Butler Community Centre opened as a Support Hub. Professional supports, refreshments, facilities available.

The language of tragedy is so well rehearsed. We know the script. The politicians arrive with their prepared statements. The cameras capture the grief in wide shots. The neighbours bring casseroles and silence. And in a week, in a month, the world moves on to the next fire, the next flood, the next accident. But in that house on Birchwood Avenue, four people — a man, a woman, two children — woke for the last time at five in the morning to smoke and heat and fear. Two more children fight for breath in hospital beds. A family erased or altered beyond recognition in the time it takes to boil a kettle.

I think about the ordinary-ness of it. A house like any house. A street like any street. A Tuesday morning. The randomness that sits at the centre of every life and waits. We build our days on the assumption of continuity — I will finish this tea, I will walk to the shop, I will come home — and the fire doesn't care about assumptions. It eats wood and plaster and flesh with the same indifference.

The gardaí are appealing for witnesses. Anyone in the vicinity of Birch Avenue in the early hours. An incident room at Waterford Garda Station. A senior investigating officer. Family Liaison Officers appointed. The machinery of investigation grinds forward. But no investigation restores what burned.

---

In Darndale, they're preparing for Halloween. Darndale Together After Dark, they call it. Saturday, October thirty-first, three-thirty to six-thirty at the Belcamp Village Centre. Music and dance acts. Street performers. A community firework display at Belcamp Park at seven-fifteen. One of the biggest and best Halloween celebrations in Dublin, Dr Jack Nolan says, Chair of the Darndale Implementation Oversight Group. The commitment, support and participation of local people. Creative Places, the Arts Council, Sphere 17, Archways, Northside Partnership, the Department of Justice and Equality, Darndale Belcamp Village Centre and An Garda Síochána — all listed as partners.

I read the press release and something in me softens. Not the bureaucratic language — the initiatives and oversight groups and partnership frameworks. But underneath: children in costumes. Fireworks painting the sky over Belcamp Park. Music that makes strangers dance together. A community that has known its share of neglect, of bad press, of being written off — choosing to gather, to celebrate, to make light in the darkening year.

The festival is free. That matters. In a city where everything costs, where the rent consumes the wage and the coffee costs four euro and the sculpture on Grafton Street cost someone's budget — here is something given freely. For the children. For the families. For the night.

I think of the children in Waterford. The two who died. The two fighting. The parents who won't come home. And the children in Darndale who will watch fireworks on Halloween, their faces lit by colours they didn't earn and don't have to pay for. The same country. The same October. The cruelty and the kindness running through the same veins.

---

Joe Jewell is hanging up his guitar. After more than forty years with Aslan. His final live performance: Vicar Street, December twenty-seventh. He announced it on social media — the modern way, the quiet way. No farewell tour with fanfare, no victory lap. Just a statement. After more than forty years with Aslan, I've decided it's time to step back from live gigs and touring. The band and management have been incredibly supportive and I'll still be proudly cheering the lads on from the background.

Forty years. Let that settle. Forty years of chords and calluses, of van rides and soundchecks, of songs that became the soundtrack to other people's lives — This Is, Crazy World, Shine On. Forty years of Christy Dignam's voice beside him, until death took Christy in twenty-twenty-three. Tony McGuinness on bass. Billy McGuinness on guitar. Alan Downey on drums. Five young men from Dublin who formed a band in nineteen-eighty-two and kept going, through addiction and recovery, through grief and glory, through the ordinary miracle of showing up.

Over the years, we've sadly lost many dear friends and colleagues, Jewell wrote. Yet continuing to play our music and sing our songs has been a great comfort and a kind of therapy for us all. It has brought me an incredible amount of joy and I want to thank all of our fans, both old and new, who have been there with me throughout the years. It's been an incredible journey and I've loved every minute of it.

I've never met Joe Jewell. I've stood in crowds at Vicar Street and sung along with thousands of others, my voice lost in the collective, feeling something I couldn't name. Belonging, maybe. The recognition that someone else has carried the same sorrow, the same hope. That a song written in a rehearsal room in Dublin can travel across decades and borders and land in a stranger's chest like a key turning.

Music does that. It outlives the moment. It carries the dead forward. When Jewell plays his last show in December, Christy's voice will be in the room. Not on the stage, but in the harmonies, in the spaces between notes, in the way the audience holds its breath during the quiet parts. Forty years of shared life compressed into a final chord.

I wonder what it feels like. To know the last time is coming. To count the rehearsals. To feel the strings under your fingers and think: this passage, this bend, this harmonic — I will do this one more time and then never again. Not the way grief arrives unannounced, but grief with a date on the calendar. Grief you can prepare for, and that makes it worse, somehow. The anticipation sharpening the loss.

---

The wind has picked up. The trees on the green move now, not dramatically, just the restless shifting of things alive. A woman passes with a red umbrella, closed. A man in a high-vis vest checks his phone. The busker on Grafton Street has started playing — something familiar, something old. The opening bars of a song I know but can't name. The melody drifts up the street, past the Dublin D with its blank face and its URL, past the café chairs and the avocado crates, past the window where I stood this morning watching the light arrive.

Twelve point five degrees. Mainly clear. The forecast doesn't mention the weight of history in the air, or the way a city holds its grief and its joy simultaneously, like a man carrying two heavy bags — one in each hand — walking steady because he has no choice.

The busker launches into the chorus. A few people stop. A girl in a school uniform, backpack bouncing, mouths the words. An older man leans against a railing, eyes closed. The song belongs to them now, not to the writer, not to the singer. It belongs to the moment.

This is what the Dublin D doesn't understand. This is what the brand managers and the councillors and the press releases miss. The city isn't in the sculpture. It's in the busker's fingers on worn frets. It's in the free fireworks over Belcamp Park. It's in the casseroles arriving at a house in Waterford where the silence has a new shape. It's in Joe Jewell's final chord at Vicar Street, and the way the audience will hold the silence after it ends, not wanting to let go.

The city is what happens between the official versions.

---

I walk home by the long way. Past the canal where the water holds the sky. Past the row of houses with their painted doors — red, blue, yellow, green — each one a small defiance of the grey. The light has strengthened, or my eyes have adjusted. The temperature hasn't changed. Twelve point five degrees. The wind eight point three kilometres per hour. Mainly clear.

In Wivenhoe the estuary is clearer, colder. In Seattle the overcast presses down. In Austin the sun hammers the pavement. Here, the air is balanced on a knife-edge. The promise of rain in the scent of wet leaves. The memory of summer in the late roses blooming in a front garden. The anticipation of winter in the early darkness that will come at five.

I think of the Dublin D again, standing silent at the top of Grafton Street. Waiting for tourists to photograph it. Waiting for the URL to be typed into phones. Waiting for the brand to activate. It will wait a long time. The city moves around it, through it, under it. The busker plays. The children laugh. The fireworks will bloom over Darndale. The music will rise at Vicar Street one last time. The casseroles will be eaten. The investigations will continue. The mornings will keep arriving, twelve point five degrees, mainly clear, mainly unclear, mainly miracle.

My tea will be stone cold by now. I'll make another. The kettle clicks. The water boils. Steam rises, carrying the scent of the day — wet pavement, exhaust, baking bread, the particular alchemy of a city breathing. I stand at the window again. The light has climbed higher. The Dublin D catches it, throws it back, indifferent. A pigeon returns. The busker changes songs.

Ordinary morning. Ordinary miracle. I write it down so I don't forget. So that when the next October comes, and the light falls this way again, I'll remember: I was here. I saw. I felt. The city whispered and I listened.

The kettle clicks off. I pour. The steam rises. The day begins.
