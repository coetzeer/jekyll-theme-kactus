The light comes in sideways at this hour, pale and thin as cigarette paper. Sixteen point three degrees, the weather service says. Overcast. Nine kilometres an hour of wind moving through the plane trees on the North Circular. I know this without checking. The body knows. The damp in the knee joint. The way the air sits heavy on the skin, not cold yet but promising it.

September twenty-third. The equinox has passed and the days fold inward now. I watched the sun rise at seven thirty-four, if you could call it rising — more a brightening of the grey, a thinning of the ceiling. The city wakes in stages. The 46A rattling past the window. A fox slipping across the green at the Mater, gone before the thought forms. Somewhere a scrambler bike tears the morning quiet, that particular whine of two-stroke engine, high and sharp as a dentist's drill.

The Mater. I think of the Mater because the paper this morning carries the doctors' words. Dr Broderick, Dr Phillips, consultants in emergency medicine and trauma, sitting before the Oireachtas justice committee. Their testimony reads like a litany. E-scooter presentations doubled in a year. Quadrupled in Drogheda. Three hundred and eighty trauma patients in two years. Three thousand hours in the department. Three hundred days as admitted inpatients. The numbers accumulate. Fourteen percent admitted. Eleven percent needing operations. Eighteen days average for a child in hospital — eighteen days, the length of a small eternity.

Eight percent pedestrians. That figure stays with me. Eight percent struck by someone else's machine. Walking the pavement, minding your business, and the world arrives at speed. A third of traumatic brain injuries in children now e-scooter related. A third. The phrase "exponential rate" appears in the report, clinical and clean, but behind it the waiting rooms fill. A major trauma takes an hour or two of a big team's work. Three or four doctors. At night, that's the whole emergency department. Three or four nurses plus radiology, inpatient teams, emergency theatre. The next day's surgeries cancelled. The downstream effects rippling.

Dr Phillips calls the new regulations "extraordinary." Dr Broderick says a law is only as good as its enforcement. Otherwise lip service. The age limit rises to under-eighteen this month. Parents buying scooters for children — illegal, he says, simple as that. There should be consequences. Sinn Féin TD Mark Ward says they've been terrorising communities for ten years. Transporting drugs. Intimidating. The word "terrorising" hangs in the air, heavier than the grey light.

I think of the scrambler bikes on the green at Darndale, on the waste ground behind Ballymun. The sound carries. Young men, mostly. Helmets sometimes. The particular posture — forward, intent, the machine an extension of will. Velocity. That's what Dr Broderick names it. Higher velocity, more severe injury. Longer in hospital. The physics is simple. Mass times acceleration. The body was not made for this.

But the body is adaptable. The city is adaptable. We paved the cow paths and called them streets. We laid tracks for trams, ripped them up, laid them again. Now the scooters. Silent, mostly. Until they're not. The pedestrian's ears have not caught up. Eight percent.

The wind shifts. A few drops hit the window, not rain exactly, more the air weeping. Sixteen degrees. The plane trees shiver. Their leaves, broad and dusty, catch the city's grime. I think of the children in CHI — Children's Health Ireland — eighteen days average. Eighteen days of hospital smell, of beeping machines, of parents sleeping in chairs. Eighteen days of a childhood measured in IV lines and scans. The breadth of trauma, Dr Broderick calls it. Five to seven days for the average trauma patient. But these are not average.

The light thickens slightly. Seven fifteen. The 46A again. A woman with a pushchair struggles up the kerb at the bus stop, the front wheel catching. No one helps. The bus driver waits, engine idling. Diesel fumes join the damp. This is the city. This is the morning. The doctors' words and the scrambler's whine and the fox on the green and the woman with the pushchair — all of it true at once. The particular containing the universal, as the man said. Though which man, and which book, slips away.

I should walk. The day will not brighten. Overcast, the report says. Mainly clear in Seattle, clear sky in Austin where it's thirty-one degrees. Different worlds, same hour. London eighteen degrees, overcast. Wivenhoe fifteen, overcast. A band of grey across the islands. The continent perhaps brighter. But here, this light, this weight. The plane trees. The Mater's red brick catching nothing.

Three thousand hours. The number returns. Three thousand hours of consultants and registrars and nurses and porters and cleaners and radiographers and anaesthetists and surgeons. Three thousand hours that were not spent on the fall on the stairs, the chest pain, the stroke, the child with the fever. The opportunity cost measured in suffering deferred. Dr Phillips: "It cancels out like theatre for the next day sometimes." The phrase "cancels out" doing heavy lifting. A clean phrase for a messy reality.

The age limit rises. Enforcement follows, or doesn't. The scramblers remain illegal, unregistered, uninsured. The e-scooters now legal, regulated, still silent. The pedestrians adapt. We walk wider. We listen for the whine. We teach the children. Look left, look right, listen for the electric hum. The city rewrites its unwritten rules.

A gull cries, harsh and distant. The fox is gone. The woman boards the bus. The light has not changed, not really, but the morning has advanced. I feel the damp in the knee. Sixteen degrees. The day stretches ahead, grey and ordinary, carrying its burden of the particular. Three hundred and eighty patients. Three thousand hours. Eight percent. Eighteen days. A third. The numbers are the story, and the story is the numbers, and the light falls on the plane trees regardless.

I will make tea. The kettle's whistle will cut the silence. The day will proceed. The scramblers will whine. The scooters will hum. The Mater will receive. The light will thin and thicken and thin again. This is the city. This is the morning. This is the weight of grey light on a September Wednesday, and I am here, noticing.
