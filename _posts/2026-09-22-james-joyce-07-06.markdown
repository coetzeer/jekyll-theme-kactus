The light this morning does not so much fall as seep. Grey-white and slow, it pushes through the overcast like breath through cloth. Eighteen degrees. The wind moves at twelve kilometres an hour from somewhere west, rattling the sash window in its frame. I have been awake since six, watching the sky refuse to choose between rain and clarity. This is September in Dublin now — not the crisp turning of leaves I knew as a boy, but a long, reluctant exhale of a summer that broke every record the Met Office keeps.

Summer twenty-six. The hottest Ireland has ever seen. The phrase sits in the council minutes like a stone in a shoe. I read it this morning in the paper Fiona Connelly brought to the Community, Gaeilge, Sport, Arts & Culture SPC — Labour councillor for Kimmage-Rathmines, a woman who speaks in the measured cadence of someone who has watched the same streets change under her feet. She tabled a motion for cooling spaces. A network of them. Libraries, community centres, sports facilities, arts venues, shopping centres — any publicly accessible building with air conditioning and a door that opens without a reason attached.

*An elderly neighbour. Rathmines Library. A cool down.*

The words stay with me. Not the policy language — the *network of potential cooling spaces*, the *citywide cooling spaces map or directory*, the *engagement with a broad range of public, voluntary and private stakeholders*. Those are the bones. But the flesh is Mrs. whoever-she-is on the third floor of a Rathmines walk-up, windows sealed against pollen and noise, the air thickening behind glass until her breathing sounds different in her own ears. Connelly would tell her: walk to the library. Sit in the reference section. Let the chilled air find your skin. No purchase necessary. No membership card. Just the simple mercy of a building designed to hold books that now also holds bodies.

Donna Cooney seconded the motion. Green Party, arts centres and libraries on her mind — *significant amounts of glass*, she said, *there's a lot of heat in them and it's difficult to keep them cool*. I think of the Hugh Lane, the light wells trapping August. The Lexicon in Dún Laoghaire, that great glass prow facing the Irish Sea, gorgeous in October and a greenhouse in July. The funding has to come from the Department. Retrofit. Shades. Planting. The language of adaptation. *Not designed for the heat we are getting.* No. They were designed for a climate that exists now only in photographs and the older generations' memories.

Darragh Moriarty spoke after. Labour again. *Hotter autumns than ever before. The new normal of our heat.* The phrase lands heavy. New normal. As if normality were a thing that could be negotiated, signed off on, filed. The council already has extreme weather plans. Met Éireann issues a warning, arrangements activate. But Moriarty is right — a warning is a siren. It sounds when the damage has already begun its quiet work. The prolonged exposure. The body that does not recover overnight because the night does not cool. The autumn that arrives on the calendar but not in the air.

The manager's response: scope exists. Stakeholders must be engaged. Referred to the Climate and Urban Resilience SPC. Summer twenty-seven for the map. The machinery turns. Slowly. Deliberately. As it should, perhaps. But Mrs. whoever-she-is does not live in summer twenty-seven. She lives in this morning, eighteen degrees and overcast, the wind finding the gaps in the window frame.

I walk the city when the light is like this. Not today — today I write. But other mornings. The quays at seven, the Liffey holding the sky's reflection like a secret. The Georgian doors on Merrion Square, fanlights catching the grey. The luas trams sliding past, red and silver against stone. In the park at St Stephen's Green, the plane trees shed bark in pale curls, revealing green-white underneath. A heron stands motionless at the pond's edge, one leg folded, eye bright as a bead. It does not know about cooling spaces. It knows water. It knows shade. It knows the wind.

The weather report sits on my desk. Dublin eighteen point one, overcast. London seventeen point one, mainly clear. Wivenhoe fourteen point two, overcast. Seattle fifteen point seven, clear sky. Austin twenty-nine point eight, clear sky. Five cities. Five latitudes. The same atmosphere binding them. I think of the heat dome that sat over Texas in July, the grid groaning, the nights that never dropped below thirty. I think of Seattle, where they still build without air conditioning, where the new normal arrives as shock. London, where the Underground becomes a kiln. Wivenhoe, where the estuary mud bakes and the smell changes, salt and rot and something older.

Here, the overcast is a kindness. The cloud ceiling holds the night's warmth but blocks the day's fire. Eighteen degrees. A temperature the body knows how to manage. No emergency. No map required. Just a jacket, perhaps. A cup of tea. The radio playing something old — The Chieftains, maybe, or Luke Kelly's voice cracking on *Raglan Road*. The ordinary mercy of a day that asks nothing of you.

But the record summer writes itself into the city's fabric. The plane trees leafed early. The chestnuts browned in August. The grass in Phoenix Park went the colour of straw and stayed there. The water mains burst in Drumcondra and Rathgar and Cabra — old pipes, thermal expansion, the ground shifting as it dried. The smell of chlorine in the tap water for weeks. The swifts left early, or so it seemed; the sky over the Liberties emptied by mid-August. The gulls, always the gulls, adapted. They always do. I watched one drop a mussel on the cobbles outside the Brazen Head, crack it open, eat with the indifference of a creature that has survived worse than us.

The cooling spaces motion. It will take two years to map. Two summers. Maybe three. By then the hottest summer on record will have been surpassed, probably. The new normal will have new normals. The map will be a snapshot of a moving target. But the motion itself — that matters. The acknowledgment that the city has a duty to its bodies, not just its ratepayers. That a library is not only for books. That a community centre is not only for classes. That public space means *public* — the old woman in Rathmines, the father with a pram in Ballymun, the student in a box room in the North Inner City, the worker on a break from the Luas depot at Broombridge.

I think of the word *refuge*. It comes from the Latin *refugium* — a place to flee back to. *Fugere*, to flee. The city builds refuges now. Not from war, not from famine, not from plague. From heat. From the sky. From the atmosphere we thickened. The irony is not lost on the councillors, I think. Connelly knows. Cooney knows. Moriarty knows. They use the language of infrastructure because that is the language that unlocks budgets. But the human language underneath — *I would love to tell her to pop down to Rathmines Library* — that is the language that matters. That is the language the map will never capture.

The wind shifts. A few drops hit the window. Not rain, not yet. The promise of it. The air smells of wet brick and diesel and something floral, late roses maybe, or the jasmine on the neighbour's trellis. The light changes, deepens. The grey gains a blue undertone. Eight seventeen. The city wakes in stages — the early buses, the cyclists with their high-vis vests, the parents herding small humans toward school gates, the delivery vans double-parked on Dorset Street. The rhythm. The ordinary, stubborn, beautiful rhythm.

A heron at the pond. A library in Rathmines. A motion in a committee room. A map promised for twenty-seven. The body that does not cool overnight. The glass that traps the sun. The pipes that burst. The swifts that left. The gulls that stay. The overcast morning at eighteen degrees. The tea cooling in the cup. The words arriving, one by one, like footsteps on a stair.

I will walk later. Past the library on Pearse Street, past the cooling centre that is not yet named as such. Past the Georgian windows reflecting a sky that cannot decide. I will feel the wind on my face, twelve kilometres an hour from the west. I will breathe air that has travelled the Atlantic, crossed continents, carried dust from the Sahara and salt from the sea and pollen from a thousand meadows. The same air that moves over London, Wivenhoe, Seattle, Austin. The same atmosphere. The one we share. The one we changed.

The map will come. The funding will come, or it will not. The retrofits will happen, or they will not. The summers will come, each one writing its record in the rings of the plane trees, in the cracks of the water mains, in the bodies that remember what the thermometers forget. And in the meantime, on an overcast Tuesday in September, a woman in Rathmines walks to the library because someone in a committee room spoke her need into the record. *Pop down. Have a little cool down.* The simplest words. The most human. The ones that make a city not just a machine for living, but a place where life finds shelter.

The rain begins properly now. A soft hiss on the slates. The window rattles once, settles. I put the tea down, cold now. The page fills. The morning passes. The city breathes.
