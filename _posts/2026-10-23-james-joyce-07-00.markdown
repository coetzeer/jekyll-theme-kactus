The light arrives grey and grudging. Nine degrees, the thermometer says, but numbers lie about cold. This cold seeps. It sits in the mortar between bricks, in the gap where window meets frame, in the marrow of anyone walking the quays at this hour. Dense drizzle, the forecast called it yesterday. *Dense* — as though the air itself has thickened, grown viscous with suspended water, each droplet a tiny lens refracting the sodium glow of streetlamps into halos that bloom and die on wet cobblestones.

I have been awake since five. Not by choice. The body keeps its own calendar, older than any wall chart, and this morning it insisted on rising with the dark. The flat is quiet save for the kettle's sigh and the occasional rush of tyres on the N11, a sound that carries differently in damp air — softer, stretched, like a cello note bowed too slow. I made tea. Stood at the window. Watched the city refuse to wake properly.

Forty years ago today, a boy left his school in Rathfarnham at twelve-fifty. Coláiste Éanna. He walked home to Ballyroan Road, collected his bag, turned back toward the classroom, and stepped into a laneway between Ann Devlin Road and Ann Devlin Drive. Six days later the bag was found there. Some books missing. The boy — Philip Cairns, thirteen, five foot two, black hair, grey jacket with black shoulder corners — was not.

One thousand six hundred lines of inquiry. The number sits in the article like a stone in a shoe. Each line a question asked, a door knocked, a name written in a notebook. The investigation team still engages with the family. Still receives calls. The recent media attention — documentaries, podcasts, the long shadow of true crime — has stirred something. New pieces of information, they say. People who were young then, who couldn't speak, who might now. *With the passage of time and changing circumstances.* The phrasing is careful, garda language, but beneath it: hope, that stubborn weed cracking through concrete.

I think of that laneway. Not the one on the map — the one that exists in the mind of a mother who has waited four decades. The weight of a schoolbag. The particular grey of a school pullover. The way a thirteen-year-old walks, that loose-limbed gait not yet stiffened by expectation. The ordinary Friday-ness of it. October 23, 1986. The clocks would have gone back the weekend before. The same dying light. The same drizzle, perhaps.

*Dense drizzle.* The words repeat themselves in me.

Across the city, at the Irish Museum of Modern Art, another opening today. *Eltiqa: How to Work Together?* — sixty-plus works by six artists from Gaza City. Mohamed Abusal. Abdel Raouf Al-Ajouri. Mohammed Al-Hawajri. Raed Issa. Dina Matar. Sohail Salem. The collective has existed over twenty years. Twenty years of shared practice, of making space for younger artists, of *working together* while the world around them unspooled. The title means *encounter* in Arabic. Some works smuggled out. Some carried by artists who managed to leave. Some held in institutions abroad because return was impossible. The Palestinian Ambassador, Dr Jilan Wahba Abdalmajid, stood in the galleries yesterday and spoke of resilience and hope. *Amplify their voices.* The timeline on the wall stretches 1948 to 2026. History and biography braided. An outdoor film programme follows — Basma Alsharif, Rosalind Nashashibi, Oraib Toukan — each asking whether images can carry the weight of siege without becoming the familiar visual shorthand for suffering.

I have not seen the exhibition. I will. But I have stood in that courtyard at IMMA when rain falls — the seventeenth-century walls channelling water, the modern glass reflecting grey sky, the old and new damp together. Art survives. That is not sentiment. It is record. The notebook sketches made with found materials. The large-scale paintings. The photographs. They exist. They were made. That making is its own argument against erasure.

And Bill Murray is in Ireland. Seventy-six years old, ninety-eight percent Irish by DNA, tracing the voyage his great-grandmother began on a boat — born *on* the boat, the article says, *that's as far back we go*. *A Course Called Ireland*, six episodes, golf as vehicle. He stands in Kinsale handing out ice creams. He stands in Cobh where the water and sky and clouds and sun remind him what was given and what was taken. *So wicked, so incomprehensible.* His words. Not a scriptwriter's. He speaks of caddying as the greatest formal education he ever had — cards, gambling, cigarettes, how to treat people. He speaks of Chicago dyeing the river green, of an Irish mayor, of pride. *We sing and we laugh and we're tough.*

I think of my own great-grandparents. The names blur. The boats blur. The percentage of Irish blood is a calculation I have never made. But I know the feeling he describes — that shock of recognition, the first time, *visually and physically emotionally, all of it at once*. The city as mirror. The streets as bloodline. The pub on the corner where my grandfather drank, the church where my mother was married, the hospital where I was born — the Coombe, two hundred years old this year, its bicentenary marked by an exhibition at the Little Museum. *Born in Dublin: 200 Years of The Coombe Hospital.* Queen Victoria's royal charter in 1867. Dr Drumm and Dr FitzGerald pioneering Doppler ultrasound in 1977. The Family Planning Clinic in the early seventies, controversial then, essential now. Two pregnant women lost in a snowstorm near Thomas Street in 1825, and from that tragedy, a group of citizens led by Mrs Margaret Boyle built something that has delivered generations. *Woven into the story of our city,* the Lord Mayor said.

Two hundred years. Forty years. Twenty years. Six episodes. Six artists. One thousand six hundred lines of inquiry.

The numbers accumulate. They do not explain.

Meanwhile, in a different register of the city's life: gardaí seized two BMW X5s worth a quarter of a million euro, Louis Vuitton bags, Rolex watches, jewellery totalling forty thousand — all from a Dublin-based charity. Money laundering investigation. Charities Regulator involved. Searches across Dublin, Eastern and Southern regions. The images show the bags laid out on a table — the familiar monogram, the familiar crown, the familiar interlocking Cs — ordinary luxury made evidentiary by context. A charity. The word does its work twice: the stated purpose, and the irony that accumulates around it like frost.

I sip tea gone cold. The drizzle has not lifted. It will not lift. This is October in Dublin — the month that strips illusion. The trees on the canal hold their last leaves like secrets. The swans move through water the colour of pencil lead. A cyclist passes, head down, hi-vis vest dark with wet. The number 16 bus groans up the hill, its diesel cough hanging in the still air.

Memory is not stored in the grand gestures. It pools in the laneway where a schoolbag was found. It lives in the brushstroke a Gaza artist made in 2002, in the caddie's lesson about how to treat people, in the snowstorm that killed two women and birthed a hospital, in the €250,000 cars seized from a charitable trust. It is the drizzle on my window right now, each droplet a minute convex mirror showing the streetlamp, the tree, the silhouette of my own face watching.

The city does not remember for us. It simply *holds*. The laneway holds the bag's ghost. The museum holds the smuggled paintings. The golf course holds the footsteps of a man walking his ancestors' ground. The hospital holds two centuries of first breaths. The charity's seized assets hold the betrayal of trust. The investigation holds its one thousand six hundred lines, each a thread that might, someday, pull the whole tapestry into coherence.

Or not. Coherence is not guaranteed. The boy may never be found. The artists may never return to their gallery in Gaza. The charity's victims may never see restitution. The great-grandmother's boat may never yield its full story. The drizzle may never resolve into rain proper, or clear into sun.

But the kettle clicks again. I rise. The day begins in earnest — emails, meetings, the thousand small negotiations of a Friday. The flat will empty. The street will fill. The laneway in Rathfarnham will see commuters, dog-walkers, a child on a scooter, none of them knowing. The IMMA doors will open at noon. The first episode airs tonight. The Coombe exhibition runs through the winter. The garda investigation continues.

Nine degrees. Dense drizzle. The light spreads, thin and grey, across the floorboards.

I put on my coat. I step out. The air tastes of wet stone and diesel and something older — the river, perhaps, carrying its sediment of history seaward. My footsteps join the day's first thousand. The city receives them without comment. It has received so many. It will receive so many more.

*Eltiqa*. Encounter. The word stays with me. Every step an encounter. Every face. Every window lit behind a curtain. Every laneway that might hold a story, or might only hold damp leaves and the echo of footsteps.

I walk.
