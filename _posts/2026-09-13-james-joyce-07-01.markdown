Seventeen point three degrees. Overcast. The wind at ten point four kilometres per hour, which means nothing and everything. It means the air has weight today. It means the light doesn't so much fall as settle, a grey wool blanket draped over the city's shoulders. I know this temperature in my bones before I check the report. The damp cool that seeps through window frames. The way breath hangs a moment too long. September 13th, and summer has packed its bags without telling anyone it was leaving.

The radio in the kitchen murmurs about Trump. Still. Always still. Two days in Dublin and the city roared. Thousands at the Garden of Remembrance, banners snapped in that same ten-kilometre wind — "Trump out of Ireland. US military out of Shannon." Chants rising: *Donald Trump, can you hear? We don't want you here.* An orange jumpsuit, a Trump mask, handcuffs. *Complicit in genocide* taped to a chest. College Street outside Trinity, voices bouncing off stone older than the concept of America: *Donald Trump should be on trial. He stands on the Epstein Files.* Rapists not welcome, one sign read, Trump's face beside Conor McGregor's. Blood of Palestine on his hands. Irish Govt craven to Imperialist Facist Predator SHAME.

He called it friendly. *I haven't seen any protests. It's a friendly protest.* The words float up from the radio, untethered from reality, untethered from the Garden of Remembrance where people stood in the damp September air saying no with their bodies. Friendly. The word does violence to the morning. Friendly like a blade wrapped in velvet.

Then he sat with Micheál Martin at Farmleigh House and spoke of a united Ireland. *I'd love to see it unified. I think it would be a great feather in everybody's cap if that happened. It's going to happen eventually. We're talking about it and you got the right man over here to get it moving.* A feather in the cap. The phrase lodges. As if centuries of blood and border and broken promise were a hat decoration. Downing Street bristled. Andy Burnham had said border poll off the table just days before. Alex Burghart called Trump *very wrong* — Northern Ireland *an integral part* of the UK. Gavin Robinson: *Northern Ireland's future would be decided by its people, not commentary in London, Dublin or Washington.* Jim Allister: *Northern Ireland is not a pawn in Trump's fallout with the UK.* Mary Lou McDonald: *timely. Martin must now begin planning for a united Ireland.*

The radio moves on. The weather. Seventeen point three. Overcast. I already knew.

---

There is a street piano in Brisbane. I have never been to Brisbane. The sun there is different — harsher, closer, the kind that burns through cloud in minutes. But the piano. A man sits at it, playing *High Hopes*. The chords rise into air thick with humidity. A stranger approaches. Russell from Singapore. He offers tips. *Try it this way. The bridge goes like this.* The man at the piano lets him. Lets him teach the song. *You know that's my song, yeah? I wrote that song.* The stranger's face — *Kodaline?* — the dawn of recognition. The singer's wife, Diana, filming: *He's the singer and the writer of that song!*

Steve Garrigan. Kodaline. Dublin band. *High Hopes* — the song that carried a generation of Irish emigrants through London flats and Australian share houses and Canadian basements. The song that played at weddings and funerals and the quiet moments between. And Russell, who had played those chords *by hand because that's just how much I played it and how much it meant to me in a particularly emotional episode in life.* He didn't mean to teach the creator. *He was changing keys.*

I think about keys. The keys we change without knowing. The songs we teach to their writers. The stranger on the street piano who carries a song through an emotional episode in life — a phrase that holds universes. *Episode.* As if grief or heartbreak or whatever it was comes in discrete television segments. But no. It means: a time. A season. The song was a rope. He held it. He learned every chord by hand. Muscle memory as devotion.

And then the creator sits before him, playing it differently. Changing keys. The stranger teaches the writer his own song back to him, not knowing. The irony is gentle. Almost tender. *Thanks for the up close and personal piano concert, and talking to you and Diana was lovely. This was one of the biggest surprises I've ever had, you can see it from my dumb face!*

Dumb face. The honesty of it. The unguarded joy of being seen in your mistake. Of discovering the divine in the ordinary moment. A street piano. A Saturday morning. A song that carried you through the dark now played by the man who wrote it, letting you teach him.

---

Garlic water. The radio — or was it the phone, the scroll, the endless feed — mentions garlic water. A woman named Gail Morgan. Dahlias. *As soon as there was a shoot it was eaten.* Wool pellets, copper tape, gel, midnight hunts. Nothing worked. Then garlic water. *A saucepan of water to half a bulb of garlic. Just put it into a spray bottle and spray when I water my plants.* Michelle Kelly: *Brilliant, this brings me much hope.* Alex Duxbury: *I think I read once that was what the gardeners use at Buckingham Palace.* Deborah Davies: *My Dad tried mixing garlic paste with water this year and watering his hostas with it and yes it worked.* Karen Quinn: *Garlic spray definitely helps on hostas but I hadn't thought to try it on the dahlias. It's so cheap too. I picked a garlic bulb up at Sainsbury's for 24p.* Rose Gilbert: *I've been putting pine cones around my hydrangeas. Apparently will help keep blue as well as keep slugs off as it acts as mulch too.* Lucy Lu: *Yes I have tried it too. It's effective but needs sort of daily application.* Even experts at Sienna Hosta swear by it. Weekly applications.

Twenty-four pence for a garlic bulb. The economy of survival. The slugs and snails *love to pop out when the weather is mild and damp* — seventeen point three degrees, overcast, Dublin. Perfect slug weather. They devour several times their body weight in a single day. Voracious. The word clings. *Voracious creatures.* We use it for readers, for consumers, for the appetite that cannot be filled. But here: actual slugs. Actual dahlias. Actual garlic water in a spray bottle made from a saucepan and half a bulb.

I think of my grandmother's garden in Rathgar. The mint she grew in pots because it would take over. The rosemary woody and ancient. The slugs she caught at midnight by torchlight, dropping them into salt. The cruelty of it, the necessity. She would have laughed at garlic water. *Sure isn't that what we put in the stew?* But she would have tried it. Desperation makes scientists of us all. The daily application. The ritual. Morning coffee, morning garlic spray. The dahlias surviving. The shoots growing past the danger zone.

---

The light in this room at 7:01 AM. Grey-white. Directionless. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. The overcast sky is a vast softbox. No shadows. No drama. Just revelation — every texture visible, every dust mote, the grain of the wooden table, the scratch on the mug, the fine lines at the corner of my own eyes when I catch my reflection in the dark window.

This is the light Joyce would have known. The light of *Dubliners*, of *The Dead*, of snow falling faintly through the universe. But also not. The snow is not falling. The rain is not falling. The overcast holds. Suspended. Seventeen point three degrees. The temperature of hesitation. Of waiting.

Wivenhoe has moderate drizzle. Seventeen point seven. London nineteen, overcast. Seattle fourteen point seven, overcast. Austin thirty-two, clear sky. The world spins on. Texas bakes. Dublin waits. The same sky, apparently, stretches over Dublin and London and Seattle — one vast grey ceiling. Wivenhoe gets the drizzle. We get the promise of it. The air thick with unshed rain.

---

The protest chants return. *Donald Trump, can you hear? We don't want you here.* The rhythm of it. The call and response. Bodies in the street. The Garden of Remembrance — built for the dead of 1916, 1921, the Civil War. Now the living stand there saying no to a living man. The orange jumpsuit. The handcuffs. The mask. Theatre. Ritual. The ancient form of the effigy burned, here worn. *Complicit in genocide.* The words heavy, precise. Not hyperbolic. A legal term. A moral verdict.

And Trump at Farmleigh: *How can it be bad?* The question of a man who has never had to live with the consequences of a border. Who has never watched a checkpoint go up, come down, go up again. Who has never had a cousin in the North, a mother in the South, a life split by a line on a map drawn by men in London in 1921. *A great feather in everybody's cap.* The cap. The feather. The lightness of it.

President Catherine Connolly at Áras an Uachtaráin: *conveyed the strong view of the Irish people that the normalisation of war and genocide can never be accepted.* Last year she called him *volatile* and *acting like a bully.* The words of a president. The words of a woman who has seen power up close and named it. *Normalisation.* That is the word. The slow creep. The frog in the pot. The protest that becomes friendly. The unacceptable that becomes routine.

---

The garlic water sits on the counter in my imagination. A spray bottle. Half a bulb. The scent of garlic rising — sharp, alive, the smell of cooking, of survival, of every cuisine that ever sustained a people through winter. The dahlias pushing up. The slugs retreating. The daily application. The discipline of it.

Russell in Brisbane. *I can only remember the chords by hand because that's just how much I played it.* Muscle memory. The body knowing what the mind cannot articulate. The song as rope. The song as map. The song as the thing you return to when the episode — the emotional episode — has no other exit.

Thousands in Dublin streets. *We don't want you here.* The body politic moving as one body. The chant as prayer. The banner as scripture. *US military out of Shannon.* The specificity. Shannon Airport. The rendition flights. The wars we facilitate while speaking of peace. The neutrality that is not neutral. The Garden of Remembrance holding the dead and the living both.

Seventeen point three degrees. Overcast. The light that reveals everything and warms nothing.

---

I think of the man who wrote *High Hopes*. Sitting at a piano in Brisbane. Changing keys. Letting a stranger teach him. The humility. The playfulness. *He was changing keys.* The phrase echoes. We are all changing keys. The song remains. The chords remain. But the key shifts. The voice shifts. The moment shifts. Russell from Singapore — Singapore, the heat, the humidity, the street pianos in malls and MRT stations — hears the song, knows the song, loves the song, teaches the song. The song travels. The song carries. The song *is* the carrying.

And the garlic. The ancient remedy. The kitchen wisdom. The woman on Facebook — Gail Morgan — sharing her win. *I have to share this win.* The language of gaming, of competition, applied to dahlias. But also the language of community. *Brilliant, this brings me much hope.* Hope. For dahlias. For hostas. For the small green things that push up through soil in September, defying the slug, the snail, the coming winter.

---

The radio falls silent. Or I stop listening. The morning stretches. The overcast holds. Seventeen point three. The wind ten point four. The day ahead — Sunday. The weekly shop. The laundry. The walk along the canal perhaps, if the drizzle doesn't come. The light on the water. The swans. The mud. The beer cans in the reeds. The city waking slow.

Trump flies to Doonbeg. His golf course. The Irish Open. The security operation — *one of the biggest ever implemented by An Garda Síochána.* Garda Commissioner Justin Kelly: *peaceful protest would be facilitated as much as possible, but there would be no tolerance for public disorder or attempts to impact critical movements.* Critical movements. The phrase of the state. The language of logistics applied to a president's whims. The roads cleared. The airspace restricted. The city held its breath for two days.

And now he leaves. Or stays. The news cycle turns. The protest signs come down. The orange jumpsuit folded, stored. The chants echo in the bones of those who chanted them. *We don't want you here.* The simplest sentence. The most complete.

---

I make coffee. The steam rises into the overcast light. The smell — dark, bitter, alive. The mug warm in my hands. The garlic bulb on the counter, waiting. I might make the spray today. For the few plants on the balcony. The mint. The rosemary. The chives that come back every year no matter what. The resilience of herbs. The resilience of songs. The resilience of people who stand in the Garden of Remembrance on a damp September Saturday and say no.

The light doesn't change. It doesn't need to. It is enough. The grey revealing. The temperature holding. The city breathing. Seventeen point three degrees. Overcast. Dublin. Sunday. Here. Now. This.
