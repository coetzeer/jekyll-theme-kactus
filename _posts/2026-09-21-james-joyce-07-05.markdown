The light this morning is the colour of weak tea poured into a white cup. Overcast, the meteorologists call it — 16.3 degrees, wind barely stirring at 2.5 kilometres an hour. The kind of grey that doesn't press down so much as settle into the pores of the city. Dublin breathes differently under this ceiling. The Liffey runs slate-smooth beneath the bridges, reflecting nothing recognisable. Gulls cry over the quays but sound muffled, as though the air itself has grown thicker, more deliberate.

I woke to the sound of a bin lorry grinding through the early hours, its hydraulics sighing like some great beast settling into sleep. The curtains in my room stay drawn. Not from laziness — from a habit of delaying the world's first demand on the eyes. When I finally pull them back, the sky offers no drama. No shafts of gold, no theatrical clouds. Just this even, democratic grey that makes all rooftops equal, all chimneys anonymous.

Eighty-five percent. That number has been turning in my mind since I read it over tea. Eighty-five percent of Dubliners want more CCTV cameras in high-crime areas. The survey landed today, or yesterday, or whenever the digital edition decides today begins. Two hundred and nine Garda-operated cameras across the metropolitan area. One for every six thousand souls. Do the maths and it feels both too many and not nearly enough. A camera for every six thousand lives — every first kiss, every argument on a doorstep, every child learning to cycle, every old man feeding pigeons with trembling hands. The lens doesn't distinguish. It swallows all into its silent archive.

Deputy Geoghegan calls it a clear message. The public wants An Garda Síochána to have access. CCTV is not a replacement for Gardaí on our streets, he says, but an important tool. I think of the parks named in the article — Sean Moore Park, Phoenix Park, Fairview Park, Mountjoy Square. Honeypark in Dún Laoghaire, where they removed cameras and anti-social behaviour increased. The parks. The green lungs of the city. Places where we go to escape being watched, only to find the watching has followed us there. A third of respondents worried about privacy. Two-thirds did not. Or perhaps they simply weighed the ledger differently — safety on one side, something harder to name on the other. The freedom to be anonymous in a crowd. The right to scratch your nose, adjust your collar, kiss your lover goodbye without a pixelated record.

I walked through Phoenix Park on Sunday. Not for the half marathon — though thousands were there, legs pumping, lungs burning, the air thick with effort and that particular smell of hot skin and synthetic fabric and deep breathing. James Hyland won in sixty-eight minutes fifty-two seconds. Twenty-four years old, Raheny Shamrock, training for his first marathon in October. He kicked on the last mile. Four of them together until then. Meghan Ryan took the women's race in seventy-six forty-six, a minute clear of her clubmate Ruby Wall. Dundrum South Dublin Athletic Club, both of them. The atmosphere was incredible, she said. The buzz around the course. Mountain runners Eddie O'Connor and Matthew McConnell — sixty-nine-oh-eight and sixty-nine-thirty-two — said they'd never heard crowds like it. Mountain racing is isolated. Here, people cheered.

I stood near the Papal Cross, not running, not cheering. Just watching the river of bodies flow past. The park holds memory in its grass. 1979, Pope John Paul II, one point two million people on this same ground. 1982, the nurse murdered in the woods, the case still cold. The deer that have wandered these acres since the seventeenth century, unbothered by popes or killers or half-marathons. Yesterday the runners' feet struck earth that has absorbed centuries of footsteps — soldiers drilling, lovers meeting, children falling from trees, the bomb that didn't go off in 1976, the one that did in 1974. The park forgets nothing. It only buries.

Speaking of bombs. Finglas, Saturday evening. North Way Estate Park. A viable suspicious device. The words themselves feel clinical, stripped of terror. Viable. Suspicious. Device. The Explosive Ordnance Disposal unit. Óglaigh na hÉireann. A controlled explosion. Homes evacuated. Paul Brown got a knock on his door. Just said there was some kind of explosive found. A cordon. A closed-off area. Gardaí keeping people back. By evening the cordon lifted, no injuries, no damage. The device — whatever it was, whoever left it, whatever grudge or game or mistake birthed it — is gone. The park is just a park again today. Children on swings. Dog walkers. An old woman with a tartan trolley. The grass grows over the scorch marks. The city is astonishingly good at returning to itself.

Balbriggan. Fifteen million euros. Harbour Square. Nine thousand three hundred square metres of civic plaza, linear park, enhanced public realm. The former RNLI boathouse restored, given new purpose. The River Bracken re-engineered, landscaped, made visible again after decades of culvert and neglect. Quay Street, Mill Street, Harbour Road stitched together into a connected public realm linking town centre to harbour to coastline. Mayor Tony Murphy called it a heartbeat. Minister James Browne called it urban regeneration in action. Twenty-five percent of Balbriggan residents engaged in the 2019 consultation. They wanted the harbour reimagined. Now it is. Play facilities coming in the months ahead. The tide comes in, the tide goes out. The square waits for markets, festivals, the ordinary magic of people showing up.

I think about the boathouse. The men who launched from there into the Irish Sea, scanning the horizon for the upturned hull, the drowning swimmer, the flare in the night. Their ghosts must be thin now, worn by salt and time. But the stones remember. The mortar holds the shape of their urgency. Now families will eat ice cream where the lifeboat once slept. Children will climb on cannons that never fired in anger. The regeneration is real, and it is also a kind of erasure. We build the new atop the old and call it progress. The harbour doesn't care. The sea doesn't care. The light on the water at 7:05 this morning — pale, diffuse, directionless — falls on the new paving and the old stones alike.

Two hundred and nine cameras. Six thousand people per lens. The half marathon runners, the evacuated families of Finglas, the children who will play in Harbour Square next summer — all of them passing, at some point, beneath the unblinking eye. The survey says we want this. Eighty-five percent. A clear message. But I wonder what the fifteen percent see that the rest don't. Or what they remember. The woman who walked through Phoenix Park in 1979 with a rosary in her pocket. The boy who found the bomb in 1976 and lived to tell it. The lifeboat crew who rowed into darkness and came back with empty stretchers. The deer. The river. The grey sky that watches without recording.

The tea is cold. The bin lorry has finished its round. Somewhere a church bell strikes seven — once, twice, three times — and the sound spreads across the rooftops, unhurried, unmonitored. I pull the curtains fully now. The day begins in earnest. The city watches itself watching itself. And I, small and temporary, watch it back.
