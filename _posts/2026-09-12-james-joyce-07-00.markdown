The alarm didn't wake me. The light did — or what passes for light at seven on a September Saturday when the sky has decided on grey as its only colour. Seventeen degrees. Overcast. The wind moving at eighteen kilometres an hour across the city, not enough to clear the clouds, just enough to make them bruise and shift. I lay there listening to the house breathe. The fridge humming its steady note. A car passing on the road outside, tyres hissing on wet asphalt. Somewhere a neighbour's washing machine entering its spin cycle, that rhythmic thump-thump-thump that marks domestic time in a way no clock ever could.

Seventeen degrees. The weather app tells me this like it means something. But the body knows better. The body knows the particular weight of Dublin air in mid-September — thick with salt from the bay, carrying the ghost of summer and the promise of winter in the same breath. It's the temperature of hesitation. Not cold enough for the heavy coat, not warm enough to pretend the light will hold. The kind of morning where you stand at the window with a mug of tea and watch the light fail to arrive properly, and something in you recognises this as the city's true face.

The radio in the kitchen has been on since six. Trump arrives today. The 47th President, they keep saying, as if the number makes it more real. Dublin Airport. Morning. Ten thousand Garda tours of duty over three days. The Phoenix Park closed since Thursday night — all those gates locked, Castleknock to Knockmaroon, the deer browsing undisturbed while the city holds its breath. The M50 will pulse with rolling closures between junctions three and six. Perimeter roads around the airport: R108, R132, Old Airport Road. No parking. One-way systems. The Mound at the South Runway viewpoint open to pedestrians only — imagine that, the plane spotters with their folding chairs and thermoses, watching Air Force One touch down while the rest of the city reroutes its morning.

I think about the man in Doonbeg waiting for him at the golf resort. The Irish Open happening there this weekend, the fairways manicured for cameras. Trump owns the place. Has for years. The coincidence feels manufactured, though I know better than to trust coincidences in this city. Everything connects if you look long enough. The Garda Commissioner on the radio yesterday: water cannon on standby, hopes it won't be needed. No tolerance for public disorder. Peaceful protest facilitated as much as possible. The phrasing does the work of both permission and threat in the same breath.

Outside my window, the overcast sky presses down. No shadows. No sharp edges. The trees in the neighbour's garden stand still, leaves heavy with whatever rain fell in the night. This is the light that makes Dublin Dublin — not the postcard sun breaking through clouds over the Ha'penny Bridge, not the golden hour tourists chase along the quays. This. The flat grey that renders everything in subtle tones, that makes the red brick of the Georgian houses deeper, the wet pavement a mirror for streetlamps that haven't yet switched off. The light that reveals rather than illuminates.

Down in Inchicore, Richmond Barracks opens its gates at eleven. The Harvest Fair. Eat, Grow, Make — three verbs that feel like a prayer for autumn. Púca masks and pumpkin patch plushies. Bean mosaics. Shadow theatre with The Selfish Giant on Saturday, Good Morning Garden on Sunday. St James's Brass and Reed Band playing live. Free entry. Dogs welcome. Guided tours of Goldenbridge Cemetery, walking tours to Kilmainham Gaol. I've walked that route. The stones remember. The cemetery holds generations of Dublin dead, their names weathering on limestone while the city builds upward around them. Kilmainham's stone breakers yard where the leaders of 1916 were shot — the cross still marks the spot. The fair sets up its stalls in the shadow of that history, children making autumn prints with paint-stained fingers while the brass band plays something bright and anachronistic.

Ten years ago, Simon Moore and Erwin Pollard opened Brickyard in Dundrum. A gastropub. A coffee shop called Grindstone beside it. Quality food, independent craft beer, Irish suppliers. This month they celebrate a decade. Wing Fest started Thursday — duck wings, cauliflower wings, sauces old and new. Saturday the 19th, the big birthday party. Sunday the 20th, BBQ under the canopy, come rain or shine. September 26th, Bricktoberfest — steins, bratwurst, German beers. Simon Moore said: "Reaching ten years feels quite surreal because there were definitely times, particularly in the early days, when we wondered whether we would make it." I know that feeling. The wonder of persistence. The quiet pride of still being here. They've watched families grow up. Built relationships with local businesses and sports clubs. Their customers became part of Brickyard. That's the sentence that stays with me: *Our customers really became part of Brickyard.* Not transactions. Not footfall. People. The ones who came for a pint and stayed for the conversation, the ones who brought their parents, then their children. The pub as living room, as witness, as anchor in a neighbourhood that keeps changing.

At the 3Arena, Westlife began their thirteen-night residency on Wednesday. Thirteen nights. Twenty-five years since they formed in Dublin, Shane Filan and Nicky Byrne and Kian Egan barely eighteen, nineteen. They played Croke Park last weekend before Katie Taylor's farewell fight. Now this — a record-breaking run at the 3Arena. The fans make the same comment: they look good. They look fantastic. They haven't changed. Magical. Class. I think about what it means to be witnessed across decades. To have your face become a shared memory for millions. The boys on stage, singing the same choruses, the crowd singing back — not nostalgia exactly, but something closer to recognition. *We were young together. We're older together. The songs held.*

The radio in the kitchen switches to the cost of living. Electric airers versus tumble dryers. The exact price difference. A heated airer: 230 watts per hour, holds twelve kilos of washing. Four to five hours for a full load. One kilowatt-hour. Thirty-five cent. A tumble dryer: four and a half kilowatts per cycle, two and a half hours. One euro fifty-seven. Less than a quarter the price. But open the windows to vent the humidity, and the central heating runs longer. Or buy a dehumidifier — salt blocks, desiccant, electricity. The arithmetic of domestic survival. Thirty-five cent versus one euro fifty-seven. The kind of calculation that accumulates into a life. My grandmother would have known the price of coal per hundredweight, the burn time of turf, the exact day the oil ran out. We know the wattage of our airers. Different fuels, same accounting.

The wind shifts. A gust finds the window frame, rattles the glass. Somewhere a door slams. The washing machine next door enters its final spin, faster now, the thump becoming a whine. Seventeen degrees. Overcast. The light still refusing to properly arrive.

I think about the woman in the park whose sister was found dead. The article from yesterday — her message to the killer: *Own up.* Three words. The weight of them. The silence they imply. The Garda search continuing. The park cordoned off. The normal dog walkers and runners rerouted, their routines broken by someone else's violence. The sister speaking to cameras, to the public, to the one person who isn't listening. *Own up.* As if saying it might make it happen. As if language could reach across that distance.

Two status yellow warnings for the weekend. Met Éireann forecasting a washout. Rain. Wind. The harvest fair in Inchicore will need its canopies. The Trump motorcade will move through wet streets. The Westlife fans queueing at the 3Arena will pull up hoods, check phones, shift weight from foot to foot. The Brickyard BBQ on Sunday — *come rain or shine, the grills will be blazing.* That phrase. A declaration. A refusal to let weather write the story.

My tea has gone cold. The mug warms my hands still, ceramic holding heat the way stone holds memory. I should get up. Make fresh tea. Maybe walk to the shop for milk. The city is waking properly now — more cars, the first bus grinding up the hill, a cyclist in high-vis cutting through the grey. The overcast sky holds. Seventeen degrees. The wind eighteen kilometres an hour from the southwest, same as the forecast said. Dublin. Saturday. September 12th, 2026.

The light falls differently today. Not because of the cloud cover, not because of the angle of the sun I can't see. It falls differently because I'm watching it fall. Because the city is full of stories I know the outlines of — Trump and the motorcades, the harvest fair and the brass band, the pub in Dundrum counting its tenth year, the boy band singing to the same crowd twenty-five years later, the sister asking for an admission that may never come, the households calculating thirty-five cent versus one euro fifty-seven — and all of it happening at once, layered, simultaneous, the city as palimpsest writing and rewriting itself in real time.

I put the mug down. Stand. Walk to the window. The neighbour's cat on the fence, motionless, one paw raised. A pigeon lands, considers, takes off again. The trees still holding last night's rain. No sun. No shadows. Just this — the grey, the seventeen degrees, the weight of air, the quiet certainty that the day will unfold whether I witness it or not. But I will witness it. That's the work. That's the only work that matters. To be here. To see. To let the light fall, grey as it is, on the page.

The radio in the kitchen mentions the temperature again. Seventeen degrees. Overcast. As if repeating it makes it true. As if the forecast is the weather. I know better. The weather is what the body knows — the salt on the lips, the damp in the bones, the way the light fails to arrive and arrives anyway, differently, on a Saturday in September.
