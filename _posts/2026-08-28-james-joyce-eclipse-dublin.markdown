The moon was going. Not gone — going.

I stood at the window at five twelve, the hour the almanacs swore the shadow would be deepest, and watched a slice of pearl eaten by something that wasn't there. Earth's shadow. The word feels too solid for what it is: a darkness cast by light. The moon didn't dim so much as hollow out. A bite taken from a fruit no hand held.

Below, Charleville Avenue slept behind its barriers. The closure signs had gone up yesterday — North Strand to Ballybough, two days of digging, the city chewing on its own veins. A lorry groaned past at four, the only engine for miles. The drivers know. They reroute. They carry on.

Down by the Bernard Shaw they were already frying crisps for the festival. Crisp sandwiches. The king of carb-on-carb cuisine, the listing called it, and I laughed at the phrase carrying through the dark. A crisp sandwich at midnight. Only Dublin. Only August. The smell of oil and salt drifting up from Portobello, mixing with the wet stone scent that rises when rain has passed and the air remembers it.

Portmarnock Beach would be filling now. Five K at six, the runners' breath making ghosts in the cold. I used to run that stretch. Sand firm at low tide. Wind off the Irish Sea stripping everything down to rhythm. Footfall. Breath. The lighthouse flash every twelve seconds. A metronome for the lonely.

Electric Picnic starts today in Stradbally. Eighty thousand people descending on a field in Laois, carrying tents and expectation and the particular chemistry of a weekend where normal rules suspend. Sofia Kourtesis plays tonight in Collins Barracks — Peruvian electronic music in a former British military complex. History loves its ironies. The stones have heard bagpipes and drills and now synthesisers. They'll hear Bon Jovi on Sunday, eighty thousand voices on "Livin' on a Prayer," and the walls will take that too.

A man walked past the window. Dark coat. Hands in pockets. Head tilted up, just for a moment, checking the moon like I was. We do that. Check the sky as if it might have changed its mind about us.

The eclipse peaks and passes. The moon reclaims itself, slice by slice. By six the shadow retreats, leaving the full face pale and whole again, as if nothing happened. As if we didn't all stand in dark rooms and on cold beaches and in festival fields looking up at the same wound healing in real time.

I make tea. The kettle sighs. The day begins properly now: ordinary, miraculous, bearing the weight of everything that happened while we slept.
