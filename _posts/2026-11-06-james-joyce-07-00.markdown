The light fails differently now. That is the first thing I notice, waking at this hour when the city still holds the night in its corners. Eleven degrees. Mainly clear, the report says, but "mainly clear" in a Dublin November means something specific — a sky the colour of weak tea, the kind that promises nothing and delivers exactly that. The wind at nineteen kilometres an hour, coming off the Irish Sea, carrying salt and the memory of rain.

I lay there listening to the heating click on in the flat below. The pipes groan. Somewhere a door closes. A car passes, tyres hissing on wet tarmac though the sky shows no rain. Not yet. The air holds it though, that particular Dublin weight — moisture suspended, waiting. You feel it in the fingers first, a stiffness that has nothing to do with age.

September feels like a different country. The news from then arrives now as fragments, headlines detached from their urgency, settled into the sediment of memory. Katie Taylor's farewell fight at Croke Park — eighty thousand voices rising in that bowl of concrete and history, Westlife singing her to the ring. Shane Filan, Nicky Byrne, Kian Egan. The band she chose, the band that chose her. "She has inspired the whole country," they said, and I wonder what that phrase means now, two months later, when the ring is empty and the grass has grown back over the canvas. Eighty thousand people breathing the same air for one night. A collective inhalation. Then the exhale, the dispersal, each body carrying its piece of the moment back to Phibsborough, to Tallaght, to the outer rings where the rent eats the wage.

The rent. Nine point one percent inflation on new tenancies in the first quarter. Two thousand three hundred thirty-five euros a month for the average Dublin flat, up from thirteen thirty-seven a decade ago. Four thousand and thirty-one eviction notices between April and June. I know these numbers. I have watched them climb the walls of this city like ivy, pretty from a distance, strangling up close. A councillor in Phibsborough — Feljin Jose, Green Party — told the papers his own commute doubled from one euro fifty to two euros. Twice a day. Three hundred euro a year. "I didn't get an excise cut," he said. "I didn't get a VAT cut on my restaurant." The words have a rhythm, a cadence that belongs to this place. The specific always contains the universal. Phibsborough. The short fare. The euro a day. The three hundred a year. This is how we measure the cost of living — not in percentages but in the coin that disappears from the pocket before the week ends.

The fare increase took effect while the Katie Taylor posters were still peeling from the lampposts. Fifteen percent average. The ninety-minute leap card fare: two euro to two thirty. Children and young adults see some costs fall, the councillors noted, but specific fares rise thirty percent. The National Transport Authority calls it sustainability. The People Before Profit councillor, Conor Reddy, calls it outrageous. Hazel Chu calls it irresponsible. Daithí Doolan says it hits the poorest hardest. The language of politics, the language of the street — they rarely meet, but on the 14 bus at seven in the morning you hear both in the same breath. A woman counting coins. A student tapping a card. The driver who has heard it all, who drives the same loop through a city that grows more expensive by the month.

I think of the Liffey Festival in September. The inaugural one. Twelve and thirteen September, Custom House Quay to George's Dock, the Pelican of London tall ship moored at Sir John Rogerson's Quay, her masts cutting the same sky I see now from this window. The 106th Liffey Swim opening the weekend — swimmers cutting through the brown water from Rory O'More Bridge to Custom House Quay, a tradition since 1920. One hundred and six years. The river has seen the trams come and go, the quays rebuilt, the containers replace the coal boats, the glass towers rise where warehouses stood. The swimmers still swim. The river still flows. The festival brought jet packs and hydrobikes, historians and archaeologists, Pat Liddy telling the story of the river that made Dublin, Donal Fallon digging up the docklands' secrets, the Dublin Dock Workers Preservation Society remembering aloud. A flotilla on the Sunday, boats and rowing clubs moving toward the port with an eight-piece orchestral accompaniment from Glasshouse Ensemble. Free entry. Some paid activities. The invitation: get closer to it.

I did not go. I watched from the window of a bus crossing O'Connell Bridge, the quays crowded with a different energy than the commuter crush. Families. Tourists. Old men in flat caps who remember when the Liffey Swim was the only show in town. The river, for two days, became the centre instead of the boundary. Northside, southside — the old division blurred by water. But Monday came and the barriers returned, the traffic cones, the rush hour logic. The river went back to being what it always is: the thing we cross, the thing we look at, the thing that divides while pretending to connect.

Eleven degrees. Mainly clear. The wind finds the gaps in the window frame.

The housing minister, Micheál Martin, stood at the Child Poverty Summit in Ballymun and said supply is the fundamental challenge. "We want to make more progress this year," he said. "We did have to make that change to the rental market. It has had potentially impacts." Potentially impacts. The phrase hangs in the air like the moisture. Four thousand eviction notices. Nine percent rent inflation. Three hundred six thousand registered tenancies. The RTB will fine landlords two hundred euros for non-compliance from September fourteen. Two hundred euros. The cost of a week's rent in some rooms. The language of bureaucracy: fixed payment notice powers. Compliance. Non-compliance. The eviction notices keep coming. The rents keep climbing. The city keeps filling with people who cannot afford to stay and people who cannot afford to leave.

Westlife begins their thirteen-night residency at the 3Arena tonight. Thirteen nights. A record. The same band that sang Katie Taylor to her final fight. The circle closes. The Bray fighter retires undisputed, three-weight world champion, the ring empty now, the canvas rolled up, the grass growing. Eighty thousand people went home that night with the sound of "You Raise Me Up" in their ears and the image of a woman in green gloves touching them all in her final ring walk. Inspiration. The word gets used until it means nothing. But I think of the girl in the flat above me, twelve maybe, shadowboxing in the hallway last week. Her mother laughed. "Katie Taylor," she said. The girl didn't answer. She just kept moving, feet light, hands up, breathing the way she'd seen on television. That is what inspiration looks like when it lands in a body. Not a headline. A habit.

The light is changing now. The weak tea sky lightens at the edges, a bruise turning yellow. I can see the Custom House dome catching it, the copper green glowing faint against the grey. The Liffey reflects nothing recognisable — just broken light, the colour of the sky chopped into pieces by the current. A gull cries. The heating clicks off below. The pipes settle.

November in Dublin. The news of September has become the weather of November — present, palpable, the atmosphere you move through. The fare increase is the cold wind at the bus stop. The rent figures are the damp in the walls. Katie Taylor's gloves are the ghost in the hallway where a girl practices combinations. The Liffey Festival is the memory of the river as something other than a barrier, two days when the water belonged to everyone, before Monday made it a boundary again.

I should get up. The day begins in the usual way — tea, the paper, the walk to the shop where the Polish woman behind the counter knows how I take it. The city wakes in layers. The early shift. The school run. The ones who have nowhere to go and nowhere to be. The light fails differently now, but it still arrives, thin and sufficient, revealing what the night concealed: the same streets, the same river, the same calculation in every pocket — what stays, what goes, what we can afford to keep.

The gull cries again. The wind rises. Somewhere in Croke Park the grass grows over the spot where a woman in green gloves stood alone in the centre of eighty thousand hearts, and the sound of that silence is the truest music this city has made in years.
