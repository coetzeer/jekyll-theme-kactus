# Bond Markets Retrospective: August 28–29, 2026

## Executive Summary

Global bond markets entered the final week of August 2026 under renewed pressure, with yields climbing across the United States, United Kingdom, and eurozone. The common thread: sticky inflation data, heavy government borrowing, and central banks signalling they are not done tightening. For anyone with a mortgage, a pension, or savings in fixed income, the moves matter — they set the floor for borrowing costs across the economy.

---

## United States: Treasuries Test Multi-Year Highs

The US Treasury market has been the epicentre of the global sell-off. The 30-year bond yield touched **5.33% on August 18**, a 19-year high, before pulling back slightly. The 10-year benchmark — the reference rate for mortgages, auto loans, and corporate debt — climbed to **4.73%** after Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh warned that inflation has not meaningfully slowed and policymakers still have "work to do."

### What Happened

Two forces collided. First, the US fiscal picture keeps deteriorating: the federal deficit is running above $2 trillion annually, and the Treasury must issue ever-larger amounts of debt to fund it. Second, inflation data remains stubborn. Core services prices are still rising faster than the Fed's 2% target, and Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks made clear the central bank is not pivoting to cuts anytime soon.

### The Treasury's Buyback Surprise

On August 19, the Treasury Department announced it would **double the size of its weekly buyback operations** from $2 billion to $4 billion. The idea was to inject demand for long-dated bonds, push prices up, and pull yields down. It worked — briefly. Yields fell on the announcement, but the relief evaporated within two days. By August 20, the 10-year yield was back above **4.70%**, wiping out the entire decline.

Analysts at the Council on Foreign Relations noted that buybacks are a band-aid: without a material economic slowdown or a change in fiscal policy, upward pressure on yields will persist. The market knows this — the buyback was digested as a tactical operation, not a strategic shift.

### Why It Matters

Higher Treasury yields feed directly into US mortgage rates (now above 7% for 30-year fixed), corporate borrowing costs, and the discount rates used to value equities. They also export pressure abroad: when US yields rise, global capital flows toward dollars, pushing up yields in the UK and Europe even if their domestic fundamentals haven't changed.

## United Kingdom: Gilts Caught Between Inflation and a Cooling Labour Market

UK 10-year gilt yields have been volatile, oscillating around the **5.05–5.06%** mark — the highest since late July. The Bank of England held rates at 3.75% in July, but markets are pricing roughly **24 basis points of tightening by December** and 36 basis points by February 2027.

### What Happened

July CPI came in at **2.9%**, a four-month high driven by household energy bills. Core inflation held at 2.6%. At the same time, the labour market showed cracks: unemployment sat at 4.9%, payroll employment fell 86,000 year-on-year, and regular wage growth was 3.5% — slightly above expectations but decelerating.

The BoE faces a dilemma. Inflation is above target and energy risks (Strait of Hormuz tensions) could push it higher. But the labour market is softening, and growth is anaemic. Most economists expect the BoE to hold through 2026, but markets have been pricing a hike — a discrepancy that keeps gilt yields elevated.

On August 26, reports of potential diplomacy to reopen the Strait of Hormuz sent oil prices lower and pulled gilt yields briefly **below 5%**. The relief was short-lived; by August 28 yields were back at **5.06%** as traders refocused on the inflation trajectory and the government's October budget under new Prime Minister Andy Burnham.

### Why It Matters

Gilt yields are the benchmark for UK mortgage pricing. Five-year fixed deals are now **4.5–5.2%** for borrowers with good equity — a structural shift from the sub-2% rates of 2021–22. Homeowners rolling off two-year fixes face payment shocks. For pension funds, higher gilt yields reduce the present value of liabilities (a positive), but the transition has been painful for those caught in the 2022 LDI crisis aftermath.

## Eurozone: Bunds Hit 15-Year Highs, Periphery Spreads Compress

German 10-year Bund yields surged to **3.27% on August 28** — the highest since May 2011. The move reflects a broader repricing: the 2s10s spread is **+48 basis points**, the steepest in the developed world, and money markets now price **40+ basis points of additional ECB tightening by year-end**, targeting a 2.80% deposit rate by March 2027.

### What Happened

The catalyst was **hot August inflation data from France and Spain**, which dashed hopes for a dovish ECB pause. Eurozone CPI accelerated to **2.9% in July**, well above the 2% target. Meanwhile, energy costs remain elevated: European natural gas hit its highest since January 2023 on Middle East supply disruptions.

The ECB has cut rates eight times to 2.25% since the peak, but President Christine Lagarde has signalled the easing cycle may be over. Markets are now betting on a *resumption* of hikes — a remarkable turnaround from just months ago.

### Periphery: Spreads Narrow Despite Risks

Paradoxically, **Italy's 10-year yield spread over Bunds has compressed to ~136 basis points**, well below the 2023 peaks above 200bp. Spain and Greece have seen similar convergence. Morningstar notes that credit rating upgrades (Greece already upgraded; Italy and Spain potential candidates) and declining deficit-to-GDP ratios are driving the compression. Investors are being paid less extra yield to hold periphery debt — a vote of confidence in fiscal consolidation, but one that leaves less margin for error if the ECB does hike further.

### Why It Matters

Bund yields are the risk-free benchmark for the entire eurozone. Higher yields raise borrowing costs for governments, corporates, and households across the bloc. For the ECB, the steepening curve signals markets expect tighter policy — but also that the transmission mechanism is working. The periphery spread compression is a double-edged sword: it lowers debt servicing for Italy and Spain, but reduces the buffer if sentiment turns.

---

## The Common Thread: A Global Duration Sell-Off

Three regions, three central banks, one story: **investors are demanding higher term premiums to hold long-dated government debt**. The era of "lower for longer" is over. Fiscal deficits are structural, not cyclical. Inflation is proving sticky in services and energy. And central banks, having pivoted from hikes to holds, are now being forced to consider whether holds become hikes again.

For the bond investor, the playbook has changed:
- **Short duration** (2–5 year maturities) offers yield with less price volatility
- **Inflation-linked bonds** protect against the upside surprise scenario
- **Quality over reach-for-yield** — periphery spread compression means you're not being paid much extra for credit risk

For everyone else: expect mortgage rates, car loans, and business credit to stay elevated. The cost of capital has structurally reset.
