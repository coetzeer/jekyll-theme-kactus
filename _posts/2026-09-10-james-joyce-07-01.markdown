The light this morning is the colour of weak tea. Fourteen degrees and overcast, the wind eight kilometres an hour from somewhere west — the Met Éireann numbers I read on my phone before I put the kettle on. But numbers don't tell you how the air sits heavy on the skin, or how the grey presses against the windows of the Timberyard complex on Cork Street where the lift has been broken for weeks and the basement exit was nailed shut by the council, a door that fire brigade had to break down to free a grandmother and her granddaughter trapped in the dark.

I think about that door. Nailed shut. The handle on the ground. Denise pushing against it with her shoulder, her fourteen-year-old granddaughter sobbing beside her in the lift that went down instead of up, minus one floor, basement, stuck. The council nailed it shut because of anti-social behaviour, they said. But the main entrance door? Open at all times. Fob access broken for years. So the people who live there — Olive in her eighties, three weeks without leaving her flat, her mobility scooter too heavy to carry down four flights; Pat with his two bypasses and last year's stroke carrying her down anyway; James carrying his wheelchair-bound sister Carol up and down stairs for six weeks straight when the lift gave out entirely — they get nailed in. The escape route nailed shut. And the fire brigade, who could have been called to something else more urgent, breaking down a door the council themselves nailed shut.

Councillor Darragh Moriarty called it shocking. Said he'd asked for a full review. Said lessons must be learned. But lessons in this city have a way of not being learned, or learned only by the people who pay for them with their bodies. Olive misses her bingo night. Misses her doctor appointments. Watches tennis on the television so she doesn't go mad. Pat says when he rings the council they just fob him off. There are ground floor apartments vacant for years, he says. They don't know who their tenants are or what apartments are vacant.

I walked past the Timberyard once, years ago. Didn't notice it then. Just another brick complex in the Liberties, the kind you walk past without seeing. Now I see it everywhere. The weight of a broken lift. The sound of a door that won't open. The particular gravity of a grandmother trapped in a basement with her granddaughter, the handle on the floor, the dark, the rain hammering outside.

The rain. It's not raining now, but it was. Monday night, intense rainfall, many Dublin streets flooded. The kind of rain that finds every crack. The kind that gets into the lift shaft and shorts the electrics, the council van pulls away and half an hour later the lift is gone again. Water finds its level. It always does.

Fourteen degrees. Overcast. The light flat and featureless, the kind that makes the city look like a pencil sketch someone rubbed out halfway through. I stand at the window with my tea and watch a woman on the street below pull her collar up against the wind. Eight kilometres an hour. Not much. But enough to move the air. Enough to make you feel it on your face.

Bord Gáis announced yesterday they're raising prices from October. Nine point one percent on electricity unit rates, seven point two on standing charges. Eight point eight percent on the average bill — fourteen euro seventy-five a month. Gas up ten point six percent on unit rates, nine point three percent average — eleven sixty-seven a month. First gas increase in four years. Hundreds of thousands of customers. Network charges, wholesale volatility, inflationary pressures. The words they use to explain why the money must move from your pocket to theirs. Olive on her fixed income, Pat on his pension, the fourteen seventy-five and the eleven sixty-seven adding up to twenty-six forty-two a month before you've turned on a single light or lit a single burner. Twenty-six forty-two is a week's shopping. A month's medicine. The difference between the lift getting fixed and not.

I think about the pilot who saw the drone off the Wicklow coast. Tuesday, five twenty pm, six thousand feet, one point six two miles left of the Aer Lingus Airbus. Large, black, unidentified. AirNav filed a safety report, reported it to the Gardaí. Over a thousand flights scrapped at UK airports, the Nats system malfunction, tens of thousands of passengers trapped — Josie Donoghue three hours on the tarmac at Gatwick, "like trying to get out of Alcatraz." Dublin Airport ninety flights cancelled. The drone at six thousand feet, watching. Or just there. A black shape in the sky over the Irish Sea while the systems below failed. The investigation initiated. The authorities notified. The spokespersons speaking in the language of procedures followed.

Thomasina and Patrick Tobin. Cabra, Castleblayney, Co Monaghan. Wellington, New Zealand. London, two teenage children, Rhea and Seamus. Holiday in Cullera near Valencia. Hot tub. Monday August twenty-fourth. Found dead. Children raised the alarm. Suction system examined. Toxicology. Forensic tests. Foul play ruled out. Funeral Thursday September seventeenth, Ward's Funeral Home, Ballybay, two pm to seven pm. Cortege from the family home in Cabra Friday morning September eighteenth. Our Lady of Knock Church, Ballintra, eleven am Mass. Private cremation. Kevin Bell Repatriation Trust donations. "Shared a life filled with love, family and friendship." "Immeasurable void." The words in the RIP.ie notice. The siblings listed — Kathy, Julianne, John, Elizabeth, Anne Marie, Cera, Septima, Duana. Predeceased nephew Lewis and niece Sophia. Patrick's sisters Selena, Natasha, Katische. Niece Grace, nephew Cooper. Extended family and friends in Ireland, New Zealand, the UK and beyond. The family home strictly private. Family flowers only.

I didn't know them. But I know the shape of that void. I know how a life shared becomes a structure, and when one beam goes, the whole thing shudders. Two beams at once. The children, Rhea and Seamus, teenage, orphaned in a holiday villa in Spain. The hot tub. The suction system. The toxicology results not back yet. The not knowing. The waiting. The funeral cortege travelling from Cabra to Ballintra, the same roads I've driven, the same grey light on the fields.

Leo Burdock's. Christchurch. 1913. Bruce Springsteen, Daniel Day Lewis, Snoop Dogg, Serena and Venus Williams, Tom Cruise, Sandra Bullock. Rod Stewart. Bono. The wall of fame at the entrance. Paul-IsItWorthIt, eighty-four thousand eight hundred TikTok followers, someone else collecting the food so he wouldn't be recognised. Eighteen euro ninety-five for fresh cod, chips and curry sauce. "Oh my God." The fish mushy. The batter not bad. The chips slightly undercooked. The curry sauce tasting of MiWadi, citrusy, apple maybe. Four point three out of ten. "Below average." Rick Stein: best fish and chips he's ever had. Anthony Bourdain: truly honoured. Marco Pierre White: similarly praised. The 1916 Rising. Two World Wars. 1939, only one shop survived. Charlie McGrachan: "When he comes to town, over the last ten or twelve times, we've managed to get him in. He's become a regular. He's such a nice guy. Just genuine. Real nice person."

Eighteen euro ninety-five. I think about what eighteen euro ninety-five means to Olive. To Pat. To the grandmother in the basement. The price of a meal at Dublin's oldest chipper, the one with the wall of fame, the one Springsteen goes to when he's in town. The one that survived the Rising and the wars but couldn't survive the food reviewer with the TikTok following. Four point three out of ten. Below average. But the wall of fame stays up. The celebrities still go. The price stays eighteen ninety-five.

The light hasn't changed. Still the colour of weak tea. Still fourteen degrees. Still overcast. The wind still eight kilometres an hour. I finish my tea. The cup warm in my hands. The city breathing through its cracks — the broken lift, the nailed door, the rising bills, the drone in the sky, the couple in the hot tub, the mushy cod at eighteen ninety-five. All of it happening at once. All of it connected by nothing more than the date. September tenth, twenty twenty-six. A Thursday. The light over Cork Street. The weight of the air. The handle on the floor.

I put the cup down. The day begins.
