The kettle sighs. Steam rises in a slow curl, catching the grey light that seeps through the window — not light exactly, more a kind of suspended hesitation, the sky unable to decide between rain and something else, something nameless. Eleven and a half degrees. The thermometer on the sill says so. The wind moves at sixteen point nine kilometres per hour, though who measures wind in kilometres per hour? Fishermen. Sailors. People who know the sea by its moods. I know it by the way the drizzle tastes on the back of the hand when you step out for the morning paper: cold, faintly metallic, carrying the Liffey's breath.

November twenty-fourth. The year thinning out. Leaves on the plane trees outside the flat have mostly given up, gone the colour of old parchment, the kind you find in secondhand shops on Ormond Quay, brittle with someone else's marginalia. A few cling on, stubborn as old habits. The drizzle doesn't fall so much as hover, a fine mist that settles on eyelashes, on the wool of a scarf, on the shoulders of the woman waiting for the 16 bus at the stop across the street. She pulls her collar up. The bus is late. The bus is always late. The timetable is a fiction we agree to believe in.

I think about Navan. Thursday last, four fifty in the morning. Four masked men outside a house in Meath. CCTV caught it — the grainy theology of modern witness. They wore face coverings. Some brandished weapons. The man, in his forties, taken to hospital with injuries not life-threatening. Nothing taken. That last detail sits in the throat. Nothing taken. So what was it then? A message? A mistake? The particular cruelty of violence that doesn't even want your things, only your peace. The Garda statement reads like a liturgy: "investigations are ongoing." They always are. The phone number for Navan Garda Station — 046 907 9930 — sits in the article like a prayer card. Call if you know. Call if you saw. The Confidential Line: 1800 666 111. Six six six. The number of the beast, or just a coincidence of digits? The mind makes patterns where none exist. That's what the mind does. Makes patterns. Calls them meaning.

The drizzle thickens. A drop falls from the gutter, hits the pavement with a sound like a full stop.

Robert Rooba has played more than six hundred games of professional ice hockey in Finland. Six hundred. The number accumulates in the mind — six hundred nights in locker rooms, six hundred bus rides through Finnish winter dark, six hundred times pulling on the jersey, the weight of the C on the chest. Captain of the Estonian national team. He shook hands with President Stubb of Finland, a serious hockey fan, the day after an overtime playoff win. The president wanted to talk about the game. Rooba told him: right now I'm in a far more tense moment. He was at a reception. The Estonian president's state visit. The handshake. The cameras. The podcast came later — *Külmalt ja keerutamata*, cold and without spin. Kaarel Nurmsalu, former Nordic combined athlete, ski jumper, his physiotherapy and meetings at the Sochi Olympics all in the Finnish team house. The world is small when you look at it sideways. Rooba talked about golf, about going back to school, about dressing rooms. Basketball is still Estonians' number one team sport, he said. Not hockey. The honesty of it. The man on the ice and the man at home are not quite the same person. He admitted it on air. Didn't mean to make a case. Just spoke. Forty minutes into an hour-long episode. The kind of thing that only surfaces when you stop performing.

Second Captains. Ken Early, Simon Hick, Mark Horgan, Eoin McDevitt, Ciarán Murphy. March 2013 they started. December 2023, the National Stadium on the South Circular Road, home of Irish boxing. October 2025, the Gaiety Theatre, tickets from forty-two ninety. Twenty twenty-five was their best year yet on the road — sold out in Cork, Belfast, London, Dublin. A podcast. Something people pay theatre prices to watch being recorded. The Blindboy Podcast, Limerick satirist, Vicar Street, February and April, forty-five eighty a ticket. The Gaiety listing gave Second Captains a running time of about two and a half hours, interval included. Interval. The word carries the weight of ice cream tubs and stretched legs and the murmur of a thousand conversations about what was just said, what it meant, whether the early bit about the referee was fair. We used to gather around radios. Now we gather around podcasts in theatres. The technology changes. The gathering doesn't.

The kettle clicks off. Silence rushes back in, full of the hum of the fridge, the distant drone of traffic on the quays, the drizzle's soft percussion on the roof.

Christmas comes earlier every year. Or maybe I just get older faster. Totally Dublin's gift guide landed this morning — *All We Want For Christmas*, the 2026 edition. Jam Art Factory in the Liberties and Temple Bar, colourful prints, bold illustrations, unique design pieces by local artists. Screenprints, laser cuts, giclée, photography. The Grand Irish Gourmet Hamper from GiftsDirect.com — one hundred eighty-nine ninety-nine. Butler's chocolates, Keogh's crisps, Bewley's coffee, tempting selections from their own Irish Pantry range. Order by the fifteenth for international, eighteenth for Ireland. Nature's Acre by Ciarán De Buitléar — when he was handed the key to an overgrown Victorian walled garden in Stamullen, Co Meath, he and his two sons started growing food, leaving corners wild, making room for neighbours. Local schoolchildren now come through the garden gate. Eleven ninety-nine paperback from GardeningWell.ie. Photo Museum Ireland in Meeting House Square, Temple Bar — photobook shop, membership for year-round culture, workshops from experimental darkroom to smartphone street photography. The Donegal Shop on St Stephen's Green Shopping Centre, family-owned since nineteen ninety-five, hand-spun tweeds and knitwear, Celtic influence, ten percent off with code TOTALLYDUBLIN. Rascals Merry Six Mix Pack — Happy Days Pale Ale, Sidekick IPA, Jailbreak Lager, Pash-N-Fruit NEIPA, Bullseye Stout, Yankee White IPA, plus two vouchers for the brewery tour in Inchicore. Great beer and great craic. Golden Discs, sixty years in Dublin's music DNA, vinyl and CDs, cult classics and deep cuts, turntables and headphones, POP section for books, posters, Funko Pops, Kenji, plush. Stores across the city — Stephen's Green, Jervis, Dundrum, Blanchardstown, Liffey Valley, Tallaght, Swords. Friend of the National Gallery — thirty-five for students and eighteen to twenty-fives, one hundred individual, eighty-five concession, one fifty joint. IFI Film Shop in Temple Bar — DVDs, Blu-rays, 4Ks, Ultra HDs, special edition box sets, books, gift cards. Lily O'Brien's Chocolate Truffle Advent House — twenty-nine indulgent chocolates, Salted Caramel, Milk Chocolate, Vanilla truffles. Proudly Irish since nineteen ninety-two. Pandora Bell's Honey Nougat — original Italian recipe for Soft Torrone, honey, Italian nuts, free-range egg whites, sugar, cocoa butter. Almond and Pistachio bar, two-star award twice at the UK Great Taste Awards. Light House Cinema in Smithfield — vouchers for tickets, food, drink, membership. The Hoxton on Exchequer Street — Hox Currency vouchers for a night away, a memorable meal, a little indulgence. Rachel Merrigan prints — striking colours, shapes, textures, screenprinting and etching, Irish folklore and heritage, Our Lady of Dublin, botanical designs, love, Grá. Anthem Vinyl — Irish artists, exclusive releases, limited-edition pressings, signed vinyl.

The list goes on. The guide goes on. Christmas as catalogue. Christmas as commerce. But also Christmas as connection — the hamper sent to family abroad, the membership gifted to a friend who loves art, the vinyl for the brother who collects, the advent house for the niece who counts days. The things we buy to say *I thought of you*. The things we make to say *I made this with my hands*. Ciarán De Buitléar's walled garden in Stamullen — corners left wild, neighbours coming through the gate, schoolchildren learning where food comes from. That's not in the gift guide, not really. It's in the book the gift guide mentions. The book is the gift. The garden is the life.

A seagull cries, sharp and sudden, cutting through the drizzle. The woman at the bus stop looks up. The 16 rounds the corner, labouring up the hill. She gathers her bag, steps onto the pavement. The bus sighs to a halt, doors folding open. She climbs in. The bus pulls away. The stop is empty again. The drizzle continues its slow work on the pavement, on the roof, on the plane trees' last leaves.

Eleven and a half degrees. Sixteen point nine kilometres per hour. Light drizzle. The Open-Meteo data, fetched at five this morning, no API key required. Texas resolved to the nearest matched location for lookup. Mixed conditions across regions. London three degrees, partly cloudy. Wivenhoe three point four, overcast. Seattle eight point six, overcast. Austin nine point three, clear sky. The world turns. Different skies over different cities. Same planet. Same physics. Different weather. Different lives. A man in Navan wakes in a hospital bed, the memory of four masked faces seared behind his eyelids. Robert Rooba laces his skates in Finland, six hundred and one games coming up. Ken Early adjusts a microphone at the Gaiety, two and a half hours to fill with talk that matters to people who showed up. Ciarán De Buitléar walks his walled garden in Stamullen, corners wild, gate open. The drizzle doesn't care. The drizzle just falls. Or hovers. Or suspends.

I pour the tea. The steam rises again, a different curl this time. The mug warms my hands. The flavour — builder's tea, strong, a splash of milk — tastes like every morning I've ever had. The sameness is the miracle. The variation is the illusion. Four masked men in Navan. Six hundred games in Finland. A walled garden in Meath. A podcast in a theatre. A gift guide. A seagull. A bus. The drizzle. Me at the window, watching, remembering, making patterns.

The light changes. Not brighter. Just... different. The grey thins to something almost silver at the edges. The drizzle pauses, holds its breath. For a moment the plane trees stand sharp against the sky, every bare branch articulated, every remaining leaf distinct. Then the mist closes in again, softening everything. The woman at the bus stop is gone. The bus is gone. The seagull is gone. The kettle is cold.

Investigations are ongoing. The season turns. The year thins. I drink my tea and watch the window breathe.
