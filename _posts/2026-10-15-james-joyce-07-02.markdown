The kettle clicks off. Steam rises in a thin column, catching the grey light that seeps through the kitchen window. Sixteen degrees. Overcast. The wind moves at twenty-one kilometres an hour across the city — I know this because the weather report tells me, and because I can hear it in the chimney, a low persistent breath against the pots.

October 15th. The year thinning out.

I read the news on my phone while the water steeps. Three stories, maybe four, all arriving in the same breath.

First: the porridge recall. Ready Brek Original and Tesco Super Smooth Porridge, pulled from shelves because flour beetles might have made a home in the grain. Flour beetles. *Tribolium confusum*, the confused flour beetle, small and reddish-brown, the kind that turn a breakfast into something you'd rather not think about. And SuperValu smoked salmon — forty grams and one-fifty grams, use-by October 17th — withdrawn for listeria. The bacterium that slips past the healthy and settles in the vulnerable. Pregnant women. The elderly. The immunocompromised. A use-by date two days away. Someone's breakfast, someone's lunch, sitting in a fridge right now, waiting.

I stir the tea. The spoon touches ceramic. A small sound in a quiet kitchen.

Second story: the strike. Thousands of public sector workers on picket lines across Dublin yesterday. Nurses, doctors, teachers, civil servants. Fórsa and SIPTU and the teaching unions, all walking out. Most schools closed. Colleges and universities shuttered. Thousands of medical appointments cancelled — the HSE's phrase, "significant and widespread disruption." The Taoiseach urging dialogue. The Minister for Health saying the impact falls "most acutely" on scheduled care. One point two billion euro set aside in the budget for a pay deal. A strong foundation, he called it. The unions said there was no basis to postpone.

I think of the empty classrooms. The quiet corridors of hospitals where only emergencies move. The picket lines in the wind. People standing together in the cold, holding signs, drinking thermos tea, talking about mortgages and rent and the price of milk. The same milk that might have gone into porridge this morning, if the porridge hadn't been recalled.

Third story, and this one sits heavier: Waterford. Birch Avenue in Kilcohan. Tuesday morning, five a.m. A petrol bomb through the window of a family home. Philip English, his wife Kim, their boys Callum and Jayden — gone. A girl and a boy pulled from the flames, critical in Crumlin. The father trying to settle a drugs debt for an associate. The car vandalised in August 2023. Petrol-bombed again on September 18th. Third time in three years.

Chief Superintendent Anthony Pettit at a press conference Wednesday afternoon: *There are people in the community who know the person or persons who carried out this arson attack in which four people have been murdered.*

The words hang. People know. Someone knows. Maybe several someones. They live on the same streets, shop at the same SuperValu, send their children to the same schools that closed yesterday for the strike. They know, and the knowledge sits in them like a stone.

The wind outside shifts. Rain spits against the glass, brief and sharp. The Met Éireann warning from yesterday — Status Yellow for rain in Donegal, Galway, Leitrim, Mayo, Sligo. Six p.m. to six a.m. Difficult travelling conditions. Localised flooding. Poor visibility. Dublin: cloudy, dry, windy, seventeen degrees. Tonight wet and windy, lows of eight. Thursday: wet morning, heavy rain, flooding in Atlantic coastal counties. Twelve to sixteen degrees. Friday cooler, eleven to fourteen, sunny spells and scattered showers. Weekend: mist, fog, dry for most.

The forecast reads like a litany. We measure the sky in degrees and millimetres and kilometres per hour. We name the warnings by colour. Yellow. Orange. Red. The wind that moves at twenty-one kilometres an hour across my kitchen window is the same wind that blew across the picket lines yesterday, the same wind that carries smoke from Birch Avenue, the same wind that will bring rain tonight and clearer skies by Friday.

High pressure building. That's what the forecaster says. Settled weather coming.

I drink the tea. It tastes of bergamot and heat and the particular mineral note of Dublin water. The light in the kitchen has deepened slightly — not brighter, just more itself. The overcast sky holds its colour, a uniform grey that makes the brick walls across the way look warmer by contrast, the wet pavement darker, the few early walkers moving with heads down, collars up.

A woman passes with a buggy. A man in a hi-vis vest cycles past, bag panniers full. Somewhere a bin lid clatters. The city waking in its ordinary way, carrying its extraordinary burdens.

The porridge recall. The strike. The family on Birch Avenue. Three stories. Three thousand stories. Three million. All of them held in this same light, this same wind, this same morning.

I remember reading once — maybe it was Joyce himself, or maybe it was someone quoting him — that the particular contains the universal. The flour beetle in the oats. The listeria in the salmon. The nurse on the picket line who hasn't slept properly in weeks. The garda at Waterford Garda Station staring at dashcam footage at three a.m. The community that knows and doesn't speak. The high pressure building over the Atlantic, pushing the rain eastward, bringing cold clear nights and misty mornings.

All of it particular. All of it universal.

The kettle clicks again. I've let the tea go cold. Or maybe it's just reached the temperature where you can drink it properly — hot enough to warm the hands through the mug, cool enough to taste.

Outside, the wind continues its twenty-one kilometres an hour. The sky stays grey. Somewhere in Waterford, a house stands cordoned off, crime scene tape fluttering. Somewhere in Dublin, a picket sign leans against a wall, waiting for the next shift. Somewhere in a supermarket, a shelf gap where porridge used to be.

The day will unfold. Rain will come. High pressure will build. The weekend will bring fog and sunshine and the odd stray shower. Temperatures eleven to fourteen. Light variable breezes.

I finish the tea. Set the mug down. The spoon rests in the saucer.

Time to write it down. Time to let the particular speak.

The light falls on Birch Avenue. The light falls on the picket line. The light falls on the empty porridge shelf. The light falls here, on this kitchen table, on this page.

Same light. Same morning. All of it happening at once.
