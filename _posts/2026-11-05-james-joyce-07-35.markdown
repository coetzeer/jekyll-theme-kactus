The light doesn't arrive so much as hesitate. Eleven degrees and the air thick with rain that never quite commits. This is Dublin in November: the sky a low ceiling of grey gauze pressing against the top floors of the Georgian houses on Merrion Square, making the chimneys disappear. I woke at six to the bin lorry on the cross street, its hydraulic whine and clatter a kind of urban birdsong. I lay there listening to the city breathe. The heating had clicked off. The flat was cold in that specific way only old Dublin flats achieve — damp cold, bone cold, the cold that lives in the walls.

Eleven point three degrees. Eighteen kilometres per hour wind from the southwest. Overcast. The weather report reads like a shipping forecast, terse and impersonal. But the body knows what the numbers mean: the particular weight of wet air on skin, the way it catches in the throat, the smell of wet leaves and diesel and something older, something that might be the river rising through the foundations of the city. The Liffey runs high this time of year. You feel it in the soles of your feet when you walk the quays.

I am thinking about Deirdre Burke, sixty-six, from Craughwell in County Galway. Eight years she waited. Eight Septembers since she stepped from flight EI 611, Amsterdam to Dublin, and misplaced her foot on the mobile stairs in what the court heard was "almost total darkness." The stairs sat lower than the aircraft floor. A gap. A misalignment. The kind of thing that happens in the gaps between systems: between plane and ground, between airline and airport, between duty of care and bottom line. She suffered a fracture dislocation of the right ankle. Open reduction surgery. A plate inserted. By 2021 the X-rays showed arthritis. Complex regional pain syndrome, the expert said. "It has tarnished the golden years of her life," her counsel told Mr Justice Paul Coffey. She uses a stick now. Uncertain walking. Difficulty with vacuuming, with bringing clothes to the washing line. The small domestic rituals that make a life, disrupted by a gap in the dark.

The case settled yesterday. Second day of the hearing. Liability not contested, only damages to assess. Eight years for a settlement. I think about what eight years looks like in a body. The slow architecture of compensation. The way justice, when it arrives, often resembles exhaustion more than vindication. She was attended by a doctor who happened to be a passenger on the flight. Chance. The random kindness of strangers in the dark. Paramedics came. Beaumont Hospital. The machinery of the state clicking into gear, belatedly.

I wonder if Aer Lingus still uses those same mobile stairs. If the lights have been improved. If someone checks the alignment now, flush to the exit, as the claim contended they should have been. Systems change when they cost money. Sometimes not even then.

The news settles on the kitchen table alongside the tea. Clanbrassil Close, Dublin 8. Tuesday morning, 2:30am. A petrol bomb through the window of a house where a grandmother and three children slept. A family of seven: two young children, an infant only months old. The intended target was a local man with a suspected drug debt. Wrong address. Wrong family. Dublin Fire Brigade in six minutes. Neighbours climbing the back wall. A woman and two males jumping from a back window, the woman seriously injured. The roof caved in. The front covered in ash. Councillor Darragh Moriarty called it a miracle no one died. A minor miracle. A miracle with caveats. Twenty-two houses in the close, everyone knows everyone. The community that held them. The community that will hold what comes after.

Gardaí reviewing CCTV. Enquiries ongoing. The language of investigation is passive, procedural, buying time. "Criminal damage by fire at a residential premises." The words drain the horror. A family of seven hospitalised for smoke inhalation. Non-life threatening injuries, the statement says. But life threatens in other ways. The child who will not sleep. The grandmother who flinches at the sound of a car door slamming. The neighbours who looked into the mouth of hell and pulled people out. They carry it now. We all carry it, whether we know it or not. The city is a palimpsest of such nights, written over and over in smoke and ash and the quiet heroism of people who did not hesitate.

I think about the car embedded in the Luas tracks at Blackhorse. Eight o'clock Tuesday evening. Red Line disrupted. A crane brought in to lift it out. Embedded, the word they used, as though the vehicle had grown there, organic, part of the infrastructure. The intersection of Davitt Road and the Naas Road in Bluebell. Commuters diverted. Tickets valid on Dublin Bus. The crane on its way. We will keep you updated. The mundane absurdity of it. A car where a train should be. The city rejecting the intrusion, steel rails biting into tyres, holding fast. Someone made a wrong turn. Someone followed a satnav into the tracks. Someone was tired, or distracted, or simply human in the way that means error. The crane arrives. The car is removed. Services resume. The tracks remember nothing.

But Bunsen. Bunsen is second best in the world. Big 7 Travel, the worldwide platform. Hamburger America in New York took the crown. Black Bear Burger in London third. Smash Things in Tokyo fourth. Eat ROBs in Sydney fifth. Bunsen, the Dublin institution with the business card menu, the stripped-back philosophy, the freshly ground beef and specially made buns and pickles and cheese and a handful of carefully chosen toppings. Shoestring fries or sweet potato or hand-cut. Thick shake. Simplicity and quality, the judges said. They've opened in Cork and Belfast too. A Dublin burger, second in the world. I have eaten there. The Wexford Street location. The queue out the door. The noise. The grease on the fingers. The satisfaction that is pure and uncomplicated. In a city that specialises in complication, in layers of history and trauma and bureaucracy and weather, a burger that is simply good feels like a small rebellion. A reminder that not everything must be earned through suffering. Sometimes you just get a good burger.

The EU comes next week. November 10 to 13. The European Political Community summit. An informal meeting of EU heads of state. The Convention Centre Dublin. The largest policing and security operation in the history of the State. A ring of steel, they call it. North Wall Quay closed from the Samuel Beckett Bridge to Spencer Place, 7am Tuesday to 6pm Friday. The exclusion zone expanding into Commons Street. Merrion Square shut Thursday for summit events at the National Gallery. Rolling road closures for motorcades. The Docklands sealed. Commuters told to walk, cycle, use public transport. Allow substantial additional travel time. Four days of commuter hell. The city put on pause for the visiting powerful. The Convention Centre gleams in the rain. I have seen it, the angled glass catching the grey light, a ship beached in the Liffey's mud. Inside, the decisions that shape the continent. Outside, the woman pushing a buggy up a diverted footpath, the cyclist dodging a Garda checkpoint, the bus driver rerouted for the third time this week. The city serves. The city endures. The city waits for the motorcades to pass.

Eleven degrees. Overcast. The wind picks up, rattling the sash window in its frame. I should dress. I should walk. The day will not write itself, though it feels like it might. The city writing itself in petrol bombs and burger rankings and settled lawsuits and cars on tram tracks and the approaching thunder of motorcades. All of it true. All of it happening simultaneously in the same few square kilometres. The grandmother in Clanbrassil Close and the judge in the High Court and the crane operator at Blackhorse and the chef flipping patties at Bunsen and the Garda planning the ring of steel. They breathe the same air. They walk the same streets. Their stories touch at the edges, invisible threads in the weave.

I think about the woman on the stairs in 2019. The darkness. The gap between plane and ground. The doctor passenger. The eight years. I think about the infant in the petrol-bombed house, months old, lungs full of smoke. I think about the crane lifting the car, the burger ranking, the motorcades. I think about the weather: eleven degrees, overcast, the rain that threatens and does not fall. The light that fails to arrive.

There is a phrase in Ulysses: "history is a nightmare from which I am trying to awake." But the nightmare is also the waking. The ordinary Tuesday. The tea going cold. The bin lorry gone quiet. The wind in the sash. The city does not wake. The city simply is. And I am in it, writing this, trying to hold the morning in words before it slips into afternoon, into evening, into the next November day when the light will hesitate again, and the news will settle fresh on the kitchen table, and someone will step from a plane into darkness, and someone will throw a petrol bomb, and someone will flip a burger, and the crane will come, and the motorcades will pass, and the city will endure, and I will sit at this window, watching the light that fails to arrive, and wondering what it all means, and knowing the question is the only answer that matters.

The kettle clicks. Steam rises. The day begins.
