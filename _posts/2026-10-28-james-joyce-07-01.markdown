The light arrives different at this hour. Not gold, not white — something the colour of wet slate, pressed flat against the window by a sky that has forgotten how to break. Twelve degrees. The thermometer says 12.1, but the body knows better. The body feels the wind at twenty-one kilometres an hour slipping through the gap where the sash doesn't quite meet the frame, and calls it October.

I have been awake since before the clocks turned back. The house holds its breath in that particular silence that only exists when the heating hasn't clicked on yet and the neighbourhood hasn't started its engines. Somewhere a bin lid clatters. A door slaps. The city waking in increments, each one audible.

---

The barber's chair in Coolock has gone cold.

Forty-two years. Ridgeway's Barbers, Northside Shopping Centre. Mark and Colin, brothers, the kind of men who remember how you take your tea and which football club breaks your heart. Boca Juniors. River Plate. They would ask about the Argentine league while the clippers buzzed, and you'd leave lighter in the pocket and heavier in the spirit, because someone knew your name and the name of your son who had his first haircut there, perched on a booster seat shaped like a car.

"Due to increased costs and inordinate amounts of local competition."

That phrase sits in the mouth like a stone. Increased costs. Inordinate competition. The language of accountancy applied to a place where a man cried in the chair when his father died and Mark put down the scissors and just listened. Where the Spanish conversation flowed as easy as the hair onto the floor. Where "small part" in their farewell note — *we have always considered ourselves a family and it has been our honour and privilege to have been a small part of yours* — carries more truth than any quarterly report.

I never got my hair cut there. But I know the shape of that loss. I know the particular hollow that opens in a street when a family business closes, the way the light hits the shuttered front differently, the way the ghost of conversations lingers in the grout between tiles. Forty-two years. That's not a business. That's a witness.

---

The wind yesterday had no name.

Alan O'Reilly of Carlow Weather gave it one anyway — "storm with no name" — because sometimes the official channels fail to recognise what the body already knows. Ireland, the windiest place in Europe. Ninety-one kilometres an hour in the south. Sixty-nine millimetres of rain in the southwest since midnight. The kind of night where you lie awake listening to the roof argue with the sky, where trees become silhouettes thrashing against something they cannot see.

The storm has passed. The gusts remain, moderated now, southerly, pushing the overcast across the city like a dirty sheet. Met Éireann says cool and blustery. Sunny spells and scattered showers. Highs of twelve. The forecast reads like a shrug.

But the air remembers. You can taste it — salt and diesel and the particular metallicity of rain on hot tarmac. The leaves that survive the stripping hang heavier, darker green. The puddles on the footpath hold the sky upside down.

---

Kieran O'Carroll is running the Dublin Marathon today. Or maybe he ran it yesterday. The article doesn't say. It says he's *pictured ahead of* an Irish Life Dublin marathon, which is the careful phrasing of journalism that knows the difference between before and after but won't commit to either.

In April 2022, Kieran was on a phone call with a colleague from Fleetwood Paints. He thought he was speaking normally. She heard gibberish. A strange giddiness in the voice. Words jumbled like a dropped Scrabble box.

She was driving. She turned the car around.

This is the part that stops me: she was driving, and she turned the car around. She dialled Cavan General Hospital from the road. She arrived at his door ten minutes later. She saw the face dropped on one side. She drove him to the emergency department herself.

A patent foramen ovale, they called it later. A small opening between the upper chambers of the heart that failed to close after birth. A door left ajar in the architecture of the body. A clot slipped through, travelled north, parked itself in the middle cerebral artery. The lights went out in a district of the brain.

Ten days in Cavan Hospital. Then the Mater. Then the National Rehabilitation Hospital in Dún Laoghaire. Occupational therapy. Speech therapy. Chronic neuro-fatigue. Brain fog. The Fatigue Management Course. Weekly calls from a peer-support volunteer. The Young Stroke Survivors' Network, for those under sixty-five.

"My three children were my focus that day. I didn't want my relationship with them to end with me unable to fulfil my responsibilities as a father."

That sentence. That sentence does something to the air in the room.

Dr Rachael Doyle, Consultant Physician and Geriatrician, Chair of the Irish Heart Foundation's Stroke Council: "Recognising the signs and calling 112 or 999 immediately are important steps as every minute counts."

F.A.S.T. Face. Arms. Speech. Time.

Face: the face may have dropped on one side. The person may not be able to smile, or their mouth or eye may have drooped.

Arms: the person may not be able to lift both arms and keep them raised because of weakness or numbness in one arm.

Speech: their speech may be slurred or hard to understand. They may also have problems understanding what you're saying to them.

Time: if you experience any of these symptoms, it's time to dial 112 or 999 for an ambulance. Even one symptom could mean a stroke.

World Stroke Day is Thursday. Tomorrow.

---

The Taoiseach was in Balbriggan yesterday.

Micheál Martin, turning the sod on a €12 million concert hall back in January 2022, returning to launch a partnership between the Irish Institute of Music and Song and American College Dublin. Bachelor of Fine Arts. Master of Fine Arts. Musical Theatre and Performance. Accredited by the Middle States Commission on Higher Education — the same body that accredits Columbia, Princeton, Juilliard.

€50 to €55 million for "Our Balbriggan" regeneration. €15 million for Quay Street and the harbour. One hundred jobs at the IIMS campus already.

The Lark Concert Hall. Four hundred seats. A name that carries poetry in a town that needs it.

Previous graduates in *Les Misérables*, *The Buddy Holly Show*, *The Witches of Eastwick*. Irish performers getting conservatory qualifications without leaving Ireland.

I think about the sound of a hundred voices in that hall. The vibration of a piano note in the chest. The way music makes a space larger than its measurements.

---

Three stories. Three ways a city holds its people.

The barber who knew your name and your son's name and your football grief. The colleague who heard gibberish on a phone call and turned a car around. The concert hall rising in a coastal town where the regeneration money meets the salt air.

And the wind that had no name, stripping leaves, rattling windows, reminding us that the atmosphere has its own politics.

---

I walk to the kitchen. The kettle screams. Steam rises, condenses on the cold window, traces fingers down the glass.

The light hasn't changed. Still that wet slate. Still twelve degrees. Still the wind at twenty-one kilometres an hour.

But something has shifted. The weight of knowing. Forty-two years of haircuts. Ten minutes between a phone call and a front door. A door left ajar in the heart. A concert hall named for a lark.

The city is not the buildings. The city is the woman who turned the car around. The city is the brothers closing the shutters for the last time. The city is the voice that comes back, syllable by syllable, in a rehabilitation room in Dún Laoghaire. The city is the music that will fill a hall in Balbriggan next spring.

The city is the light, however grey, falling on all of it without preference.

---

I drink the tea. The heating clicks on. The neighbourhood starts its engines.

Outside, the overcast doesn't break. But it thins, just slightly, at the horizon — a seam of paler grey where the day might, eventually, declare itself.

Time. Face. Arms. Speech.

The acronym echoes. I touch my face. Both arms rise. The words form in the mouth, clear and whole.

A luxury. A miracle. A Tuesday morning in October.

The kettle clicks off. The silence returns, different now. Full.
