The cold got here before the light did. I noticed it on the backs of my hands when I put the bin out, that first real grip in the air, and I stood a second longer than I needed to because I wanted to know whether autumn had actually turned or whether I was only tired and reading the season into my own blood. Nine point four degrees, the report says. Wind at fourteen kilometres an hour. Mainly clear.

Mainly clear. I keep turning that phrase over like a coin. Not clear. Mainly. As if the sky had a mind and the mind was hedging. There is a particular October light that comes with that kind of weather, low and exact, arriving along the street at an angle so sharp it makes every terrace end look freshly drawn. The railings glinted. A man on a bike went past in a heavy coat with the hood down, as though to prove something to somebody. The Hill of Howth was cut out clean against the pale edge of the sky, and the bay underneath it lay flat as a plate, and the whole thing looked less like a city and more like a photograph of one that had been left in the sun.

Friday. The week has weight on it now, the way a branch has weight on it. I made tea and sat down with the phone and read the morning's business the way I always do, half listening, and today it seemed to me that nearly every story had the same shape. Something getting into a body.

First the salmon. Five batches of SuperValu and Centra smoked salmon, pulled from the shelves on the word of the Food Safety Authority, because listeria monocytogenes had been found in them. Consumers are strongly advised not to eat the affected batches. Do not eat. That is the language of the regulator and it is not a phrase that softens with repetition. Return it to the shop for a full refund. The batches carry a use-by date of the seventeenth of October, which is tomorrow, so somewhere in this city at this moment there is a fridge with a light in it and a sealed packet in the door and a date printed on the packet that has not yet happened. The sickness that lives in that packet is an old one, common enough, mostly a bad week for the healthy and a serious thing for the pregnant, the elderly, the newly born, the ones whose defences have been borrowed or spent. Listeria does not knock. It goes in with the food.

Second, and I read this one twice. Yesterday afternoon at thirty-five minutes past three, an excess of chlorine got into the pool at the Clayton Hotel in Liffey Valley. Twenty-three people were assessed. Ten children and one adult went to hospital. The Dublin Fire Brigade set up a decontamination zone, which is a sentence you have to read slowly to believe, because a swimming pool is meant to be the safest water in Ireland for a child. And here is the thing my mind won't let go of: the chlorine was there to keep them safe. It was put in on purpose. Somebody added too much of the very substance that exists to make the water fit to breathe near, and ten children spent the afternoon being checked over by doctors instead of going home. Too little poison and you get disease. Too much and you get a ward. There is a narrow band in the middle where you are allowed to live, and most of life is spent walking around inside it, and nobody prints that on a wall anywhere.

I thought about the roof of the mouth. How little of what we take in we choose. Air goes in without asking. That is the whole design. You can hold your breath for a minute, maybe two, and then the body overrules the mind and takes what it needs, whatever is in the room.

Third story. This one I liked. Cycling to school is up almost forty-nine per cent in the schools that have had Safe Routes to School work done, and thirteen more Dublin schools have been brought into the programme, which now has a hundred and eighteen schools in the capital. Walking up eight per cent. Scooting up seventeen and a half. Car use down ten. Somewhere in that arithmetic there are children putting their legs down on pedals at eight in the morning in this cold, hoods up, breath going sideways, and I find I cannot read that number without seeing them. A city that makes a safe road gets cyclists. A city that doesn't gets excuses about the weather. Yesterday's wind was twenty-one kilometres an hour and today's is fourteen, so the weather is improving, and the children were never the problem.

Then the Iveagh Markets. On Francis Street, opened nineteen hundred and six, alive and trading until the nineteen nineties, then shut up and left to the damp. Nine and a half million euro has been put aside for conservation through the Urban Regeneration and Development Fund, work started last December, and the behind-the-scenes photographs now show scaffold rising through the Wet Market and the Dry Market halls while specialist teams take the old steel frame back to bare metal. The most delicate part of the job is removing the historic lead-based paint, which was used happily all through the twentieth century and is now recognised as hazardous and has to come off under strict controls.

A building of a hundred and twenty years, and the thing it needs saving from is its own coating. That paint was progress once. It kept the iron from the wet air of the Liberties and it was put on by men who thought they were doing the building a favour, and every one of them is dead now and the paint has become the danger. Time did that. Nobody meant it. I like the Iveagh Markets because it is the honest version of how this city treats what it loves: ignore it until it becomes a hazard, then spend millions learning to care about it properly. I have known people like that too. I have been one.

Down in Swords they are taking applications for twenty-five cost rental homes at a place called Furzefield. Four one-bedroom duplexes, fifty to sixty-three square metres, at one thousand three hundred and ninety-five euro a month. Seven two-beds at one thousand six hundred and fifty. Fourteen three-beds, the largest of them a hundred and thirty square metres, at one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine. Cost rental means not for profit, rents set at least a quarter below the market, and the bus called the Swords Express will take you in to town. I do the sum in my head the way anyone does, one bed, one thousand three hundred and ninety-five, and I decide not to make a speech about it. But the city has a price now for the right to sit in it and eat your dinner in peace, and the price is printed in a table, and the table is what we hand our children instead of an answer.

And then Waterford, which I have been circling since yesterday. Philip English. His wife Kim. Their sons, Callum, twelve, and Jayden, nine. A petrol bomb through the front door of their house just after five in the morning. The two youngest children were pulled out by emergency services and are still in critical condition, fighting for it, in a hospital bed somewhere while the rest of us get on with our Fridays. The eldest son, John James, has been going through CCTV footage from the weeks before, piecing it together himself, and he has said publicly that he will not be intimidated and that he is not afraid. Not afraid. I have thought about that sentence all morning and I still don't know what it costs a young man to say it out loud. The report for Dublin this morning says mainly clear. Both of those are true at the same time, and I have stopped expecting the world to notice when its own sentences contradict each other.

The tea has gone cold again. It does that. I made it hot and then I read about a family burned in their hallway, and now it is a different drink altogether. The window has fogged at the bottom corner where the heat of the room meets the glass, and through the clear part of it I can see the light moving across the kitchen floor, slower now, the sun getting up properly over the far roofs. Nine point four degrees and mainly clear. A shelf gap in a supermarket with a notice taped above it. Scaffold inside a market hall on Francis Street, lead paint coming off the steel in strips. A girl on a bike at the top of a hill, braking, walking the rest. A fridge with the light off and a packet dated tomorrow. A boy in Waterford looking at footage of an ordinary street in the weeks before it stopped being ordinary.

None of it resolves, and I am not complaining about the news. It's how a Friday is built: mostly clear, cold, the week resting its weight, the light too low to be hopeful and too exact to be ignored. I will go out into it in a while. I will put on the heavier coat and walk as far as the river and back, and the cold will get into my hands again and I will be glad of it, because being able to feel the weather is the cheapest proof there is that a person is still here and still taking things in. Air and food and smoke and paint and other people's memories. Something is always going into a body and something is always leaking out. In all my years of reading about it I have never once worked out how to choose the one or keep hold of the other.

Mostly clear, then. Cold enough for a heavier coat, and I still don't know what will have happened by the time I get back.
