Over the past 48 hours, government bond markets across the United States, United Kingdom, and Europe have experienced a sharp selloff, pushing borrowing costs to multi-year — and in some cases multi-decade — highs. The rout reflects three converging forces: an escalating Middle East conflict driving energy prices higher, swelling government deficits requiring unprecedented borrowing, and central banks signaling rates may stay elevated longer than markets hoped.

## United States: Treasury Intervention Fails to Calm Nerves

The most dramatic action came from the U.S. Treasury. On Wednesday, August 19, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced a doubling of the government's bond buyback program, increasing purchases of longer-dated debt from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation. The move targeted the 10- to 30-year segment, where a "buyers' strike" had pushed yields to levels not seen since 2008.

The initial reaction was exactly what Bessent hoped for: the 10-year Treasury yield fell from 4.74% to 4.65%, and the 30-year yield dropped from 5.34% to 5.20%. Stock futures surged. But the relief lasted less than a day. By Thursday, yields had clawed back all losses — the 10-year closed at 4.70%, the 30-year at 5.25%. On Friday, they pushed higher still, with the 30-year touching 5.28%.

The market's message was clear: investors view the buyback as a band-aid on a structural problem. The United States now carries over $40 trillion in federal debt, with a deficit on track to exceed $2 trillion this year. Corporations — particularly tech giants funding AI infrastructure — have issued nearly $1.7 trillion in bonds so far in 2026, a 27% increase over last year, competing directly with Treasuries for investor dollars.

Complicating matters, Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh has signaled rate hikes remain on the table if inflation doesn't retreat toward 2%. Minutes from the Fed's July meeting revealed many officials believe higher rates will be necessary. The Treasury's attempt to suppress long-term yields risks undermining the Fed's inflation fight by keeping financial conditions looser than intended — a tension critics warn could force the Fed to hike more aggressively.

Adding pressure, the Iran conflict has sent Brent crude above $92 a barrel, reigniting inflation fears. The dollar has weakened sharply, falling to its lowest level in 100 days, as markets bet U.S. intervention will fail to contain yields while eroding currency confidence.

## United Kingdom: Gilts Caught in the Crossfire

UK government bonds have not been spared. The 10-year gilt yield hovered around 5.05% on Friday, while the 30-year gilt traded near 5.85% — its highest since 1998. The selloff reflects both global spillover and domestic pressures. UK inflation accelerated to 2.9% in July, a four-month high driven by energy prices. The Bank of England is now widely expected to raise rates in September. Political uncertainty following Labour Party losses in local elections has raised questions about fiscal discipline. Goldman Sachs analysts note the BoE faces a "material about-turn" in monetary policy, tightening into a weakening economy — a delicate balancing act that has gilt investors demanding higher risk compensation.

## Europe: Bunds and Peripherals at Crisis-Era Levels

Across the Channel, the picture is equally stark. Germany's 10-year Bund yield climbed to 3.275% — its highest since 2011 — before retreating to 3.26%. The 30-year Bund hit 3.79%, a 15-year peak. France's 10-year OAT reached 4.10%, a 17-year high, while the 30-year OAT touched levels last seen in 2008. Italy's 10-year yield rose above 4.1%, pushing the spread over German Bunds to 85 basis points, reflecting growing fiscal sustainability concerns.

The ECB is nearly certain to hike in September (90% probability), with the deposit rate expected to reach 2.76% by March 2027, up from 2.25%. Eurozone inflation ticked up to 2.9% in July, driven by oil and natural gas prices. Supply is no less daunting: eurozone governments issued €38.3 billion in the first post-summer week, the busiest restart on record. Barclays projects €1.54 trillion gross issuance in 2027. Meanwhile, the ECB is allowing bond holdings to mature without reinvestment, forcing private investors to absorb more supply. France faces particular scrutiny: its deficit may stay above 5% of GDP, and political fragmentation makes consolidation uncertain. Italy must refinance debt equivalent to 17% of GDP this year, the highest in the bloc.

## Why This Matters

Rising bond yields translate directly into higher mortgage rates, costlier business loans, and more expensive government borrowing. In Germany, 20-year fixed mortgage rates are expected to rise next week. In Italy, smaller banks have begun repricing mortgages upward. French households have been shielded so far, but sustained high yields will eventually feed through.

For governments, the math is unforgiving. The average coupon on existing debt is well below current market yields. Every maturing bond refinanced at today's rates adds to the interest bill. France's interest costs are projected to double by 2031. Italy already spends the most on debt service as a share of GDP. The U.S. now spends more on interest than any budget item except Social Security and Medicare.

The bond market is sending a unified message: the era of free money is over. Governments borrowed heavily when rates were near zero; now they must service that debt at 4%, 5%, or higher. Central banks are reluctant to step in again. Investors, burned by years of negative real returns, are demanding a proper term premium for holding long-dated debt in an inflationary, geopolitically unstable world.

The Treasury's failed buyback may be remembered as the moment the market called the bluff: artificial yield suppression cannot work when fundamentals — deficits, inflation, war, and corporate supply — all point the same direction. The question is not whether yields will stay elevated, but how high they must go before a new equilibrium emerges.
