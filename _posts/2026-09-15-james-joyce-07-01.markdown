The morning arrives clear. Fifteen and a half degrees, the meteorologists say, mainly clear, wind fifteen point one kilometres per hour from the west. Numbers. I prefer the feel of it — that particular September light, pale gold and thin, the kind that shows the dust on the windowsill and the crack in the teacup you've been meaning to throw out since March. The air has no weight today. It sits on the skin like a promise or a warning, depending on which way you're facing.

I walked past The Grá on Eustace Street yesterday. The sign still hangs, rainbow lettering fading at the edges, but the door was locked. A notice taped to the glass in someone's careful hand: *Our last day will be 30 September. The costs of doing business in Dublin is high and increasing. We couldn't make the maths work.*

The maths. That word stays with me. As if a city could be balanced like a ledger. As if the queer creative hub that sold sustainable t-shirts and gourmet ice cream and gave the local dogs a biscuit when they padded in — as if that could be reduced to columns of euro and cent. The Grá. The Irish word for love. They signed it *With grá from The Grá.* Love from Love. And the maths won.

I stood there longer than I needed to. A woman came up behind me, read the notice, made a sound in her throat — not quite a sigh, not quite a tut. *Such a lovely business,* she said to no one. *We need to do better at supporting small businesses.* Then she turned and walked toward Temple Bar proper, where the tour groups swell and the pints cost seven euro and the maths works perfectly for someone, just not for the people who make the place worth visiting.

The Liberties. Tailors' Hall. Today the Fringe Festival opens *Pretty Vacant* there — Tom Swift's musical about the housing crisis, about vacancy and dereliction and who the city is for. *What is a city for and who is it for?* The question hangs in the programme notes like smoke. They did a walking tour of The Liberties with Anthony Freeman, a local community worker. He showed them where the aparthotels rise, where the tourist accommodation blooms like mould on damp brick, where the most densely populated area in the country has no proper football pitch. *Corporate greed placed above community needs.* Swift's words. He said they realised it was a microcosm of everything. They also found the humour, the craic, the energy. *If you took it too seriously, you'd be crying.* So they made it a musical. Euro-pop. Satire. A former Eurovision contestant playing a property developer's friendly face. Ruth McGill as Carmilla. The audience enters a fake community consultation and the play starts before they're through the door — characters on the street outside Tailors' Hall, indistinguishable from the real people arguing about bins and planning permission and the price of milk.

Liz O'Connor's voice is in the soundtrack. An activist, a mother, a woman from Oliver Bond flats. Her interview clips made it into the songs. *Even stories she told us made it into some of the songs that we wrote.* I think about that. A woman's voice, recorded in a kitchen maybe, or a community centre, threaded through a musical that will play for six nights in a hall built in 1703. The Liberties. The name itself — liberties granted, liberties taken. The Palatine weavers, the Huguenots, the tanners and brewers. Now aparthotels. Now the maths.

The light shifts. A cloud passes, brief and high. The temperature doesn't change but the quality does — the gold goes grey, the shadows sharpen. I think of Michael Owen sitting in a Dublin Live studio, telling a journalist about Pakistan. St Patrick's Athletic. Brian Kerr, Johnny McDonnell, Ger O'Brien, Jamie Moore. They went to Lahore, or maybe Karachi, trying to build a pipeline. A talent pathway from Pakistan to Europe. *It died a death,* Owen says. *Finances, infighting, a league already there and a new super league trying to take over, a little bit political.* He shrugs in the quote. *You probably know as much as me.*

A football dream. Dublin men in a Pakistani boardroom. The former Liverpool striker, the former Real Madrid Galactico, the manager who took Ireland to a World Cup quarter-final. They wanted to drive something great. *Sadly, I mean it was at the other end, the Pakistan end, that it sadly fizzled out.* The phrase *fizzled out* does a lot of work. No explosion. No scandal. Just the slow leak of air from a tyre. The maths again. The politics. The existing league, the new super league, the infighting. *I was sort of waiting to see, but I haven't heard any news since.*

And then Owen pivots — the journalist's question or his own instinct — to Troy Parrott. The Dubliner. Twenty-four. Scored a dramatic winner against Real Madrid, Owen's old club. *Nothing better than early on in your career at a new club, scoring a big winner or doing something that grabs the headlines.* Owen knows. He was eighteen when he announced himself against Argentina. He knows the weight of a moment. *He'll be absolutely thrilled. Spanish players are taught to play in a certain way, and he has a different way of playing. Powerful. Physical. Scores goals. A real crowd favourite.*

The Bernabéu noise. *Real Madrid expects every single game. If you lose, it's a crisis. Newspapers dedicated to their teams, filling it just on training sessions on a Monday. One little thing goes wrong, magnified a hundred times.* I've never been to Madrid. But I know that noise. I've heard it in Croke Park on an All-Ireland final day. I've heard it in the Aviva when the anthem plays and the crowd holds its breath. The magnification. The crisis. The newspapers.

Liverpool under Iraola. *A lingering hangover from last season's poor performances. A sinking level.* Owen's phrase. *A rising tide lifts all boats.* He repeats it. *When everything's sinking, it's bloody hard to be the standout.* Henderson, Milner, Robertson — *thinking are these good enough to be here? And all of a sudden now we're saying these are some of the most important players Liverpool have had in the last ten years.* Time. Patience. The tide.

Oasis. Slane Castle. September 2027. Two nights. Richard Ashcroft supporting. Tickets from one hundred eighty-one euro to five hundred one euro. Registration closes Thursday, four PM. *More than ten million fans from one hundred fifty-eight countries queued last time.* The banks of the Boyne. The grass. The stone. The Gallagher brothers, older now, the feud settled or suspended or monetised — who knows, who cares. *Start the celebrations.* They're already here. The anticipation is its own weather system. I'll be forty-one then. Or forty-two. The age where you stop counting forward and start counting back.

The cloud passes. The gold returns. My shadow stretches long on the pavement.

I keep walking. Capel Street. The Grafton. The quays. The river holds the light, throws it back doubled. A heron stands motionless at the water's edge, one leg folded, neck retracted — a feathered question mark. The Luas clatters past, red and green, the Doppler shift of its bell. Tourists with wheeled suitcases navigate the cobbles. A busker on Henry Street plays *Wonderwall* badly on an acoustic guitar. Nobody sings along. The song belongs to 1995 and to next September and to no time at all.

*The costs of doing business in Dublin is high and increasing.*

The sentence echoes. The Grá. The aparthotels. The Pakistan pipeline. The Liverpool hangover. The Oasis tickets at five hundred euro. The Fringe Festival ticket prices I haven't checked but assume. The rent I pay for a room with a window that looks at another window. The pint price. The bus fare. The calculation we all run in the background, constant as heartbeat: *can I stay?*

Swift said it: *We're all guilty of sitting back. Things change in the city all the time, and we complain — why did they tear that building down? Why are there so many hotels and not enough housing? But ultimately it's about being more vocal, more conscious.*

More vocal. More conscious. The words land soft and heavy.

The musical asks the audience to decide as the curtain closes. *How to proceed.* Not what to think — how to proceed. The difference matters. Thinking is private. Proceeding is public. Proceeding means showing up at the planning meeting, the residents' association, the picket line, the ballot box. Proceeding means using the word *grá* and meaning it, not as branding but as practice.

I pass the Project Arts Centre. A poster for *Pretty Vacant* on the hoarding. Tailors' Hall. September 15th to 20th. Tuesday to Friday at half seven, weekend at half two and half seven. *The cast we have, we're incredibly lucky to have.* Swift's voice again. *It kind of goes to a mad place quite quickly. I hope we haven't gone over the top.*

Over the top. The phrase of someone who's looked at the edge and wondered if the leap was too far. But the edge is where the light catches. The edge is where the city shows its true shape — not in the centre where the money pools, but at the margins where the vacants sit and the communities hold on and the artists make musicals about it and the queer shops close and the football dreams fizzle and the heron stands on one leg in the Liffey's reflection waiting for a fish that may or may not come.

The wind lifts. Fifteen point one kilometres per hour. It finds the gap in my collar, the frayed cuff. Mainly clear. The meteorologists were right. But they didn't mention the quality of the light on Eustace Street at 7:01 AM, or the way the notice paper curls at the corners where the rain got to it last week, or the woman's throat-sound, or the busker's bad chords, or the heron's stillness, or the weight of a word like *grá* when the maths says no.

They never mention that.

The light holds. I walk on.
