# Bond Markets Retrospective: US, UK, and Europe — September 3, 2026

## The short version

A full-blown global bond sell-off has deepened over the past 48 hours. Yields — the interest rates investors demand to hold government debt — have surged across the United States, United Kingdom, eurozone, and Japan. The catalyst is a convergence of three forces: a new wave of US-Iran military clashes in the Persian Gulf that has pushed Brent crude above $95 a barrel, stubbornly high inflation that keeps central banks on a tightening path, and governments that simply cannot stop borrowing. Bond prices fall when yields rise, so this is a painful moment for existing bondholders. But the market is sending a clear message: the cost of capital is structurally higher, and policymakers have fewer easy exits than they pretend.

## United States: fiscal scale meets geopolitical shock

The US 10-year Treasury yield climbed above **4.81% on September 2**, its highest level since October 2023, while the 30-year sits near **5.26%** according to StreetStats data. The entire curve has shifted up: 1-year at 4.16%, 2-year at 4.38%, 5-year at 4.54%.

Three forces are converging. First, **geopolitics**: over the weekend and into Monday, the US military launched new strikes against Iranian targets around the Strait of Hormuz following attacks on two oil tankers in the critical shipping route. Iran fired missiles at US bases in Jordan in retaliation. Brent crude surged about 5% to near $95 a barrel, the highest since late July. Oil at these levels feeds directly into headline inflation, which in turn pressures the Federal Reserve to keep rates elevated — or even hike again.

Second, **the Fed's own rhetoric**. Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks (late August) made clear that inflation progress is "not yet secure" and that policymakers still have "work to do." Markets went from pricing rate cuts to pricing a **~66% chance of a September hike** per FedWatch — a remarkable shift in weeks. Deutsche Bank continues to expect the Fed to hike 50 basis points this year.

Third, **fiscal arithmetic that refuses to improve**. US federal debt has crossed $40 trillion. The Treasury must issue ever-larger amounts of paper to fund deficits running above $2 trillion annually. Investors increasingly demand a "term premium" — extra yield for the risk of holding long-dated bonds when the supply spigot never closes. US debt service costs are now competing with defence spending as the fastest-growing line item in the federal budget.

## United Kingdom: the fiscal warning light is flashing red

Britain's market is feeling the global rout *and* a home-grown budget crisis. The Guardian reported on September 1 that **30-year gilt yields hit 5.89% intraday** — the highest since 1998 — while 10-year yields touched **5.25%**, their highest since the 2008 financial crisis. By September 2, yields had eased slightly to 5.87% (30-year) and 5.21% (10-year), but remain far above the Office for Budget Responsibility's March forecast of 5.1% for the year.

The timing is politically brutal. Chancellor John Healey faces an Autumn Budget on October 28, and higher yields directly shrink his fiscal headroom against Labour's self-imposed rules. Every basis point rise in the cost of debt service means less room for public spending or tax cuts. The OBR will have to factor these elevated yields into its forecasts, potentially forcing difficult choices before Healey even steps to the dispatch box. Some analysts estimate his headroom could be nearly halved.

The Bank of England watches the same oil-price and inflation data as the Fed. If energy costs stay high, the BoE may need to hold or hike rather than cut — a scenario that would have seemed implausible just months ago.

## Europe: Germany's safe-haven status cracks

Germany's 10-year Bund yield climbed **above 3.37% on September 2** — the highest since 2011 — and the 30-year surged above **3.84%** (a 15-year high). French 10-year OATs rose above **4.22%**, the highest since November 2008. The OAT-Bund spread, a gauge of French sovereign risk, widened to around **83-84 basis points**, up from roughly 76 bps in late August.

The drivers are familiar but intensifying. **Energy costs**: oil and gas price rises revive eurozone inflation fears — eurozone headline inflation hit **3.3% in August**, well above the ECB's 2% target. **Fiscal expansion**: Germany is borrowing more for infrastructure and defence; France's budget credibility is questioned (debt around 110-115% of GDP vs. Germany's ~60%), feeding the country-specific risk premium. **ECB repricing**: traders now price the ECB deposit rate near 2.70% by December, with a real chance of another hike at the September 10 meeting. ECB economists say this energy-driven episode looks nothing like the demand-fuelled surge of 2021-22, but the inflation print forces their hand.

## Japan: the 30-year ceiling breaks

Japan's 10-year government bond yield hit **3% on September 1** for the first time since September 1996 — a psychological and technical threshold three decades in the making. The Bank of Japan faces mounting pressure to raise rates further to address the impact of a weak yen and import-driven inflation. The sell-off in Japanese bonds is significant because Japan has been the global anchor of low yields for a generation; its repricing removes a floor for yields everywhere else.

## Why it matters

A repricing of **duration** (interest-rate sensitivity of long bonds) and **credibility** across all three regions. If inflation stays sticky with oil above $95, central banks cannot cut; yields stay high; governments pay more to service debt, crowding out spending or forcing more borrowing. The bond market is "sending an invoice" — the era of cheap money is not returning on schedule.

For households, this means higher mortgage rates and costlier credit. For companies, higher cost of capital weighs on investment. For governments, it means a larger share of tax revenue goes to bondholders rather than public services. The feedback loop — higher yields → higher debt service → more issuance → higher yields — is the mechanism that turns a sell-off into a crisis.
