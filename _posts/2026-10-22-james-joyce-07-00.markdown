The kettle sings. Six point nine degrees outside, the thermometer says, and the sky sits low over the Liffey like a bruise. Overcast. Fourteen kilometres an hour of wind nudging the plane trees along the quays. The light is thin, grey-white, the colour of old newspaper held to a window. You don't see the sun so much as sense its absence, a pressure behind the eyes.

Storm Austen. They've named it now, the first of the season. The meteorologists with their orange warnings and yellow warnings, their precision about landfall — Thursday evening through Friday morning, the southern coastline first, then Dublin, Wexford, Wicklow catching the orange rain warning at two in the morning. Two a.m. on Friday. I'll be asleep, or trying to be. The rain will hammer the roof tiles. The wind will find the gaps in the window frames. They say one hundred and fifty millimetres of moving water can knock a person down. Three hundred millimetres floats a car. These are the numbers they give you now, as if knowing the mathematics of drowning makes it less likely.

Met Éireann says keep checking. Stay updated. Plan accordingly. The language of bureaucracy applied to chaos. I think of the old men in the pub on Camden Street who watched the barometer the way others watch the racing form. A drop in pressure, a shift in the wind — they knew without the app, without the colour-coded maps. The body knows. My knee knows. Six point nine degrees and the damp in the bones.

Three hundred years since Gulliver's Travels left the press. 1726. Jonathan Swift, Dean of Saint Patrick's Cathedral from 1713 until the end, buried there in the nave. The cathedral will glow on the twenty-eighth with children's drawings projected across its Gothic stone — Lilliput, Brobdingnag, Laputa, the Houyhnhnms. Three hundred schoolchildren's visions of a giant tied down by threads, of a world where reason takes the shape of a horse. The Very Rev Dr William Morton says what moves him most is seeing a story three centuries old still firing imaginations. Dr Brendan Twomey calls it a fitting tribute to a book that has never been out of print. Never out of print. Think on that. Three hundred years of continuous reading. How many books can claim that? The Bible. Shakespeare. Swift. A short list.

I walked past the cathedral last week. The scaffolding on the spire. The tourists with their cameras pointed up. The traffic noise from Patrick Street. Swift's epitaph, written by himself in Latin: *Ubi sæva indignation ulterius cor lacerare nequit* — where savage indignation can no longer tear the heart. He knew about storms. The Drapier's Letters. *A Modest Proposal*. The satire that cuts so close to the bone it draws blood. And now children's drawings projected on the walls where he preached. The organ will play Seán Doherty's *The Man Mountain*, commissioned for the anniversary, inspired by Pope's poem responding to Swift. Pope. Swift. Two men who hated each other and needed each other, bound in literary history like the two strands of a rope.

The Jonathan Swift Festival runs November eighteenth to twenty-eighth. Talks, theatre, music, storytelling. I'll go to something. I always mean to and then the days slip. The deadline for children's artwork is tomorrow, the twenty-third. Friday. The day Storm Austen breaks. Some child in Cork or Kerry or Donegal is drawing a giant tonight by lamplight, hearing the wind rise, not knowing the storm has a name.

Michael O'Leary says airfares will rise twenty percent next year. The biggest increase in history, he says. Jet fuel doubled since the strikes on Iran. The Strait of Hormuz. Ryanair carried twenty-two million passengers in July. Twenty-two million. A seven percent increase. But profits fell thirty-four percent. Only fifteen percent hedged at eighty-five dollars a barrel. Unhedged into summer 2027. Twenty routes cut from Belgium because the aviation tax jumps from two euros to seven. Move the planes to Slovakia, Italy, Sweden — zero tax per seat. The logic of capital: go where the extraction is easiest.

I think of the women at the check-in desks in Terminal 1, the men loading baggage in the rain. The families with too much luggage and not enough money. The students flying home for Christmas, calculating the cost in weeks of bar work. Twenty percent. That's not a number. That's a decision not to go. That's a grandmother not seen. That's a funeral missed. O'Leary says they'll still be profitable. Less profitable. The language of the quarterly report. The human cost doesn't appear in the spreadsheet.

The Bram Stoker Festival starts tomorrow. Four days. Macnas's *Fiáin* parade through the North East Inner City on Monday. *Fiáin* — wild. The wild elements of the West of Ireland brought to Dublin's streets. Giant puppets. Fire. Music. The *Petrified* podcast live, horror stories told in a room full of strangers holding their breath. *Stitch*, Irene Kelleher's Frankenstein at the Project Arts Centre — the lines between monster and maker blurring. Hammer's *Dracula* at the Light House, Christopher Lee and Peter Cushing, restored footage returned after decades. Dracula's Disco at the Mansion House. Blood pudding tasting at the Old Irish Times Building. Sip and Sculpt in Smithfield. The city turns into a playground for the dead and the living both.

Bram Stoker. Born on the Marino Crescent in Clontarf. *Dracula* published 1897. Never out of print either. Another Irish book that conquered the world. Wilde. Shaw. Yeats. Beckett. Swift. Stoker. Joyce. The list runs long for a small island. We export stories because we have too many and not enough land to hold them. The stories leave. The people leave. The planes get more expensive. The storms get names.

Six point nine degrees. The kettle clicks off. I pour the water over the tea leaves — Barry's, strong, the way my mother made it. The steam rises. The colour deepens. The smell of it, malty and familiar, anchors me in this kitchen, this morning, this city.

The gulls cry over the river. A bus hisses past on the quays. Somewhere in the city a child finishes a drawing of a giant ship on a sea of tiny people. Somewhere a family calculates whether they can afford the flight home. Somewhere the barometer drops and the old men in the pub nod to each other, no words needed. The storm comes in. It always does. The question is only whether you have enough tea, enough light, enough words to last until it passes.

The Jonathan Swift Festival. The Bram Stoker Festival. Storm Austen. The price of flight. Three hundred years of Gulliver. The light failing early. The rain not yet fallen but coming, you can taste it in the air — metallic, cold, old as the sea. I'll drink this tea. I'll watch the sky. I'll think of Swift in his deanery, listening to the wind in the cathedral spire, writing by candlelight, the indignation savage and the heart still beating. Three hundred years. The book stays. The storm passes. The city endures.
