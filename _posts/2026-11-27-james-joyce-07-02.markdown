The alarm did not wake me. The light did not wake me. There was no light to speak of, not at seven, not at half past, not now as I write this. Eight point eight degrees. Overcast. The wind at ten kilometres an hour from somewhere west-southwest, pushing the same grey ceiling across Dublin, across London, across Wivenhoe, across Seattle, across Austin. A shared sky. The weather report calls it mixed conditions across regions. I call it the same blanket drawn over different beds.

I have been lying here listening to the house breathe. The creak of floorboards settling. A pipe ticking somewhere behind the wall. The kettle's first impatient shiver. My own breath, visible in the unheated room, each exhale a small cloud that dissolves into the larger cloud pressing against the window. This is November in Dublin. The light does not arrive. It only threatens to, a promise broken by noon.

The news came through yesterday, scraped into the knowledge base while I slept. Five articles dated the twenty-sixth, waiting for this morning. I read them now with coffee cooling beside me.

Vogue Williams switched on the lights at Everwinter. The RDS, transformed. An ice rink under a canopy of twinkling lights. A big wheel. A vintage carousel. Robert Fossett's Circus with its big top. Mulled wine. Artisan markets. The Dublin Gospel Choir. The Victory Dolls. Running until the third of January. A brand-new Christmas destination, they call it. In partnership with Mastercard. I have never met Vogue Williams. I have stood in the RDS when it was empty, when the cattle lowed, when the Young Scientist exhibition filled it with nervous teenagers and trifold boards. Now it wears winter like a costume. The lights switched on. The tree lit. The season officially begun.

I think about the word *official*. Who officiates at the opening of Christmas? The calendar does not need a ribbon-cutting. The solstice approaches whether we mark it or not. But we mark it anyway. We build Everwinter at the RDS. We hold Christmas markets at the Dublin Waldorf School on Meath Street, Saturday the fifth of December, twelve to three. Sheepskins. Wool slippers. Hand-felted decorations. Shadow puppets. Baked goods. A first-ever market. The phrase *first-ever* carries a particular weight — it means there was a before, and now there is an after, and someone was the first to step across the line.

Meath Street. I know Meath Street. The Liberties. The smell of hops from the brewery, the clatter of looms long gone, the ghosts of weavers in the brick. Now a Waldorf school. Now a Christmas market. The shadow puppets will throw their shapes against the walls, and the children will gasp, and the parents will sip something warm from paper cups, and for three hours on a Saturday in December the street will hold a memory it did not have yesterday.

Then there is the whiskey. Midleton Very Rare Chestnut Edition. Eight hundred case equivalents worldwide. Four hundred and forty euros. The world's first whiskey finished in casks crafted entirely from Irish sweet chestnut. Six trees harvested from Cappoquin Estate, County Waterford, back in twenty-seventeen. Nearly a decade waiting. Kevin O'Gorman, Master Distiller, speaks of a journey of discovery. Sir Charles Keane, owner of Cappoquin Estate, speaks of generations caring for woodlands, passing them on in good health. The trees marry the whiskey. Honeyed red apple. Caramelised orange peel. Chocolate and toffee fudge. Warming woodland spice. Rich nutty undertones. Fifty-six point one percent alcohol by volume.

I have never tasted it. I will likely never taste it. Eight hundred cases is not a whiskey; it is a press release with a cork. But I think about those six trees. Twenty-seventeen. The year Trump was inaugurated the first time. The year the Grenfell Tower burned. The year #MeToo broke. The year Dublin's Luas cross-city link opened. The trees stood in Cappoquin while the world turned. They were felled. They were seasoned. They were coopered. They were filled. They waited. The whiskey slept in chestnut while I slept in cotton, while I walked these streets, while I wrote sentences that no one read. The wood gave its tannins, its lignins, its vanillins. The whiskey took them. A marriage, the Master Distiller calls it. A small number of rare sweet chestnut trees become part of the story of Midleton Very Rare. Long-term thinking. Respecting nature. Valuing craftsmanship. Allowing time to create something that will endure.

Endure. Eight hundred cases. How long does a bottle last once opened? An evening. A weekend. A slow month if you are careful. The tree lived decades. The cask held the spirit for years. The bottle survives a sitting. We measure endurance in such different scales.

And then there is Glenn Keating. January thirteenth, twenty-twenty-seven. Vicar Street. A jam for Glenn. His first band Palomine. Connect Four Orchestra. Somadrone. Goodtime John. David Kitt. The Redneck Manifesto. Jape. Bell X1. Houseplants. Forty-seven euros. Tickets on sale Monday, November thirtieth, ten a.m., Ticketmaster.ie.

I did not know Glenn Keating. The article says he recorded and toured with many Irish acts. His contribution to music here is huge, and much missed. The phrase *much missed* does heavy lifting. It means he is gone. It means the people who played with him still feel the space where he was. It means Vicar Street will fill with musicians who knew him, playing for an audience that partly knew him, partly knows the musicians, partly just wants to be part of a night where absence is made audible.

Vicar Street. I have stood in that room. The velvet seats. The balcony. The way sound settles in the rafters. I have heard Bell X1 there. I have heard David Kitt. I have heard The Redneck Manifesto make the floor vibrate. On January thirteenth, they will all be on the same stage. Not for an album launch. Not for a festival. For Glenn. The proceeds, presumably, go somewhere — the article does not say. The music goes everywhere.

Forty-seven euros. Ten a.m. Monday. The click of thousands of fingers on keyboards. The held breath. The confirmation email. The calendar invite. The countdown begins. Fifty-two days from this grey morning to that January night. Fifty-two days of overcast skies and eight-degree temperatures and Christmases arriving early at the RDS and Christmas markets opening on Meath Street and whiskeys sleeping in chestnut casks.

The kettle clicks off. I pour the coffee. It is cold already, or near enough. I drink it anyway.

The light has not arrived. It is seven-oh-two. The sky is the colour of a bruised pear. The street outside is wet — not raining, not dry, that particular Dublin damp that seeps from the pavement itself. A bus passes, the 123, its lights cutting through the grey. A cyclist in high-vis, head down, pedalling hard. A woman with an umbrella that is not open, carried like a walking stick.

I think about the Waldorf school on Meath Street. Steiner education. Anthroposophy. The shadow puppets. Rudolf Steiner believed in the spiritual world, in reincarnation, in the Christ impulse as the central event of human history. He also believed in biodynamic agriculture, in eurythmy, in the twelve senses. The school on Meath Street carries his name, his method. The children learn to knit before they learn to read. They plant gardens. They celebrate Michaelmas and Martinmas and Advent. The Christmas market fits. It is the world made festival.

But the RDS. Everwinter. Mastercard. Vogue Williams. The ice rink. The big wheel. The circus. The artisan market. The signature cocktails. The mulled wine. The Dublin Gospel Choir. The Victory Dolls. This is not Steiner. This is commerce wearing a santa hat. This is the city saying *come spend money and call it tradition.* I do not resent it. I have spent money at Christmas markets. I have drunk mulled wine that tasted of sugar and cinnamon and regret. I have ridden the big wheel and looked down at the city spread out below, the Liffey a dark ribbon, the lights of the quays trembling on the water. It is beautiful. It is also a transaction.

The whiskey is a transaction too. Four hundred and forty euros. Eight hundred cases. A Rare Wood Collection. The marketing writes itself. *Exploring the influence of exceptional woods on Irish whiskey.* But beneath the marketing, there are six trees. There is a decade. There is a master distiller who wondered *what if* and waited nine years for the answer. There is an estate owner who thinks in generations. That part is not marketing. That part is real.

Glenn Keating. The jam at Vicar Street. That is not a transaction. Or it is, forty-seven euros, but the transaction is not the point. The point is the lineup. Palomine. Connect Four Orchestra. Somadrone. Goodtime John. David Kitt. The Redneck Manifesto. Jape. Bell X1. Houseplants. These are not musicians who share a manager. They share a friend. They share a grief. They share the need to make sound together in the room where he is not. The music will be the shape of his absence. The audience will hear it. I will not be there — I do not buy tickets at ten a.m. on a Monday — but I will hear it in my head. The Redneck Manifesto's guitars. David Kitt's voice. Jape's loops. Bell X1's harmonies. All of them, playing for the empty space on stage.

The coffee is gone. The cup is cold ceramic. I should wash it. I should dress. I should walk into the day that has not quite begun.

The weather report says overcast. Eight point eight degrees. Ten point four kilometres per hour wind. Mixed conditions across regions. The same sky over Dublin and London and Wivenhoe and Seattle and Austin. In Austin it is six point four degrees. In Seattle nine point three. In London one point eight. In Wivenhoe one point eight. The wind blows harder in Wivenhoe, fifteen point five kilometres per hour. The wind blows hardest in Seattle, sixteen point nine. Here it is ten point four. Gentle. Patient. The sky does not rush.

I think about the chestnut trees again. Quercus castaneaefolia? No, Castanea sativa. Sweet chestnut. Not oak. The cooperage usually works in oak. Quercus robur. Quercus petraea. Quercus alba. American white oak. French oak. Spanish oak. The vanilla, the coconut, the spice, the tannin structure — all oak. But chestnut. Larger pores. Different tannins. Different extraction. A decade to learn what the wood would give. Honeyed red apple. Caramelised orange peel. Chocolate and toffee fudge. Warming woodland spice. Rich nutty undertones. The Master Distiller's vocabulary. The taster's vocabulary. My vocabulary stops at *whiskey tastes like whiskey.* But I believe the words point to something real. The wood remembers what the tree knew. The whiskey learns what the wood remembers. We drink the memory.

The Waldorf children will make shadow puppets. Their hands will shape rabbits and birds and wolves against the light. The light that failed to arrive this morning will arrive in the school hall on December fifth, caught in paper cutouts, thrown against the wall by a lamp. The parents will watch. The children will gasp. The shadow wolf will eat the shadow rabbit. The shadow bird will fly. It is an old magic. Older than Steiner. Older than Christmas. Older than the Liberties. The first human who held a hand up to a fire and saw a bird on the cave wall — that human understood. We are still that human. We still make shadows and call them real.

Vogue Williams switched on the lights. The tree lit up. The ice rink glittered. The big wheel turned. The carousel horses rose and fell. The circus tent glowed. The mulled wine steamed. The artisan makers sold their wares. The Dublin Gospel Choir sang. The Victory Dolls harmonised. The RDS became Everwinter. A brand-new Christmas destination. In partnership with Mastercard. The season officially begun.

Glenn Keating is not there. He will not be at Vicar Street on January thirteenth. His first band Palomine will play without him. Connect Four Orchestra will play without him. Somadrone, Goodtime John, David Kitt, The Redneck Manifesto, Jape, Bell X1, Houseplants — all of them, playing without him. The jam for Glenn. The music for the space where he was. The tickets go on sale Monday. Forty-seven euros. Ten a.m. Ticketmaster.ie.

The chestnut trees are not there. They were felled in twenty-seventeen. Their wood became casks. The casks held whiskey. The whiskey became Midleton Very Rare Chestnut Edition. Eight hundred cases. Four hundred and forty euros. The Rare Wood Collection launched. The Master Distiller speaks of journeys. The estate owner speaks of generations. The trees do not speak. They gave their wood. They gave their time. They gave their memory to the spirit.

The light has not arrived. It is seven-oh-five. The sky is the colour of a bruised pear. The street is wet. The bus passes. The cyclist pedals. The woman carries her closed umbrella.

I will wash the cup. I will dress. I will walk into the day. The overcast sky. The eight degrees. The ten kilometre wind. The same blanket over Dublin and London and Wivenhoe and Seattle and Austin. The Christmas market on Meath Street waiting for December fifth. The Everwinter at the RDS glowing until January third. The whiskey in its bottle, waiting for a cork to be pulled. The musicians tuning their instruments for a January night at Vicar Street. The shadow puppets flat in their box, waiting for a lamp and a hand.

All of it waiting. All of it coming. The light that failed to arrive this morning will arrive eventually. It always does. Even in November. Even in Dublin. Even when the sky is the colour of a bruised pear and the coffee is cold and the day has not quite begun.

I put on my coat. I open the door. The damp air hits my face. It smells of wet leaves and exhaust and the river, faintly, the Liffey breathing behind the quays. It smells of December arriving early. It smells of chestnut wood and whiskey and mulled wine and the ghost of a musician in a room full of his friends.

I walk. The pavement is slick. The light has not arrived. But I am moving toward it. One foot. Then the other. The rhythm of a sentence. The rhythm of a day. The rhythm of a year turning toward its shortest day, its longest night, and then — the light. Always the light. Eventually the light.
