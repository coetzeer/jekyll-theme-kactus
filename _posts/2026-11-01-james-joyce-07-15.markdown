The light drizzle. 10.5 degrees. The kind of morning where the air doesn't so much fall as suspend itself, a fine grey gauze between you and the spires. I woke to it pressing against the window — not rain, exactly, not mist, but something older. The city breathing out.

Abbey Street at five a.m. Three gardaí, one a trainee. A cyclist. A firearm produced, aimed, then discarded into the Liffey like a bad secret. The magazine recovered, sent for forensics. "Realistic imitation," they're saying. But the water doesn't know the difference. The Liffey takes everything — shopping trolleys, bicycles, phones, knives, now this. I think of the river at that hour, black and slow, carrying its new cargo toward the bay. The cyclist fled. The trainee garda will remember the weight of that moment, the way the streetlight caught the barrel. Or didn't. Memory edits. The city keeps its own archive.

I walked past there yesterday. The Luas tracks gleaming. A woman with a red umbrella. The smell of wet concrete and exhaust and something fainter — yeast, maybe, from the bakery on Jervis Street. The same streets. Different hour. The news arrives later, mediated, framed. But the drizzle was there at five a.m. too, falling on Abbey Street, on the Quays, on Temple Bar. Indiscriminate. Democratic.

---

The pitch at Alfie Byrne Road. Sheriff YC. Tyre tracks carved into grass that volunteers spent weekends tending. A bonfire last Halloween put it out for months. Now this. The locks broken. A car driven over it, deliberate as a signature. The club's statement: "Do you know the knock on effect this has on the kids who play football on this pitch?" The question hangs. TD Daniel Ennis: "gutted." "Stupid, senseless behaviour." They've been asking Dublin City Council for fencing since March. Promises made. Nothing done. The East Wall pitch still not right from bonfire damage years ago.

I know that pitch. Not personally — I've never played there — but I know the shape of it. The geometry of a community held together by white lines and goalposts. The volunteers who show up in rain, in wind, in this same drizzle, lining the field, cutting grass, hauling nets. The parents on the sideline with thermoses. The kids who only want to kick a ball. All of it held hostage by someone's boredom, someone's cruelty. The car tracks are still there, hardening in the cold. By spring they'll be faint scars. The grass will grow over. But the trust — that takes longer.

---

November 1st. New rules for learner drivers. Third permit, fourth permit — you need a test in the last two years now. Fifth permit, you start over. Sixty-four percent of drivers have driven unaccompanied on a permit or know someone who has. Fifty-eight percent cite test wait times. Fifty-six percent, rural transport. Thirty-one percent: lack of respect for regulations. €160 fine. Two penalty points. Vehicle impounded.

The numbers settle in the damp air. I think of the girl practising parking on the polygon, instructor watching from outside. The polygon — that word, clinical, geometric. The reality: wet tarmac, steam from the engine, the clutch biting, the heart beating too fast. The instructor's voice. The rain on the windscreen. The new rules are necessary, they say. Close the gap. But the gap isn't in the rules. It's in the waiting. The bus that doesn't come. The test centre booked until spring. The father who works nights and can't sit in the passenger seat. The mother who never learned. The rules change. The waiting stays.

---

Tom Hopkins died. October 26th. Christy Phelan, sixteen years behind the counter of Phelan's corner shop in Carrigstown. Ballyfermot. Pearse Street before that. Husband to Renee. Father of four. Grandfather. The death notice lists them all — Lilly, Lynn, Danielle, Ine, the grandchildren adored. Frank, his best friend, and Marie. The Alzheimer Society of Ireland for donations.

I never watched Fair City religiously. But you don't live in Dublin and not know Christy Phelan. The corner shop. The heart of Carrigstown. The place where stories converged — the gossip, the crises, the quiet transactions of daily life. Tom Hopkins stood there for sixteen years, weighing sugar, dispensing advice, carrying the weight of fictional lives that felt more real than some real ones. He died peacefully, surrounded. The notice says "wonderful care of staff at Carechoice Step Down Facility and St James's Hospital." The words "wonderful care" — you don't write those unless you mean them.

His reposing is Thursday at Staffords, Ballyfermot. The funeral after. A celebration of Thomas's life. Family flowers only. The corner shop will feel different on screen. The actor goes, the character lingers. That's the trade.

---

The Dublin Book Festival starts Tuesday. Twenty years. One hundred and twenty events. Royal Irish Academy of Music. Kevin Barry and Martin Doyle on Saturday. Niamh Campbell and Doireann Ní Ghríofa on Sunday. Oein DeBhairduin and Patricia Fjellgren — Traveller writer, Sámi writer — on Indigenous creation and resistance. DCU St Patrick's Campus: Literature and the Digital Age. Colin Barrett, Rob Doyle, Róisín Kiberd. The PANDEMONIUM comics readings. The multilingual open mic at Teeling Distillery in the Liberties on Sunday.

I'll go to something. I always say I will. The programme sits open on my phone. The buzz of book launches. The smell of paper and coffee and possibility. The Indie Press Celebration with karaoke after — that stays with me. Karaoke after poetry. The absurd, glorious mix. Ireland's literary scene thriving in the margins, in the independent presses, in the journals you have to hunt for. The festival holds space. That phrase — holds space. It means something here. A city that has lost so many spaces — pitches, venues, shops, pubs — still makes room for words.

---

The drizzle thickens. Or maybe it's just my attention settling. Wivenhoe is foggy at 6.7 degrees. London overcast at 9.9. Seattle clear at 6. Austin clear at 19.6. The world turns, each city wearing its weather like a coat. Dublin wears dampness. We know it. We're made of it. The brick absorbs it. The sandstone softens. The mortar remembers every hand that laid it.

I think of the firearm in the Liffey. The tyre tracks on Alfie Byrne Road. The learner driver gripping the wheel on the N11, the rain blurring the lights. Tom Hopkins's corner shop, fictional and eternal. The book festival posters going up on lamp posts already damp. All of it happening at once. The city doesn't pause for significance. It just accumulates.

A magpie on the railing. One for sorrow. Two for joy. Three for a girl. Four for a boy. Five for silver. Six for gold. Seven for a secret never to be told. The old rhyme. I count. Just the one. It tilts its head. The drizzle beads on its feathers. It flies.

The light changes. Not brightens — shifts. The grey lifts a fraction. The spires of the Pro-Cathedral catch something. The Custom House dome. The cranes at the docklands, skeletal against the sky. November. The year folding inward. Samhain past. The dead remembered. The living continuing.

I'll make tea. I'll open the book festival programme properly this time. I'll choose something. The drizzle will continue or it won't. The Liffey will carry its secrets. The pitch at Alfie Byrne will grow over. The learner drivers will book tests or they won't. Tom Hopkins's grandchildren will grow up knowing their grandfather was Christy Phelan, and also more than that.

The city breathes. I breathe with it. That's the whole practice.
