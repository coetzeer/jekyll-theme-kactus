# Bond Markets Retrospective: US, UK, and Europe — September 2, 2026

## The short version

A full-blown global bond sell-off is underway. Yields — the interest rates investors demand to hold government debt — have surged across the United States, United Kingdom, and eurozone over the past 48 hours. The catalyst: a perfect storm of US-Iran military clashes that sent oil prices jumping, sticky inflation that keeps central banks on a tightening path, and governments that simply cannot stop borrowing. Bond prices fall when yields rise, so this is a painful moment for existing bondholders. But the market is sending a clear message: the cost of capital is structurally higher, and policymakers have fewer easy exits than they pretend.

## United States: fiscal scale meets geopolitical shock

The US 10-year Treasury yield rose to **4.80% on September 1**, up 4 basis points from the prior session, according to Trading Economics. The 30-year yield sits near 5.28%. These are levels last seen in 2023, and the trajectory is unambiguously higher.

Three forces are converging. First, **geopolitics**: over the weekend, the US and Iran exchanged missile fire in the Gulf. Brent crude surged above $90 a barrel, the highest since late August. Oil at these levels feeds directly into headline inflation, which in turn pressures the Federal Reserve to keep rates elevated — or even hike again.

Second, **the Fed's own rhetoric**. Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks (late August) made clear that inflation progress is "not yet secure" and that policymakers still have "work to do." Markets went from pricing rate cuts to pricing a **~66% chance of a September hike** — a remarkable shift in weeks.

Third, **fiscal arithmetic that refuses to improve**. US federal debt has crossed $40 trillion. The Treasury must issue ever-larger amounts of paper to fund deficits running above $2 trillion annually. Investors increasingly demand a "term premium" — extra yield for the risk of holding long-dated bonds when the supply spigot never closes.

## United Kingdom: the fiscal warning light is flashing red

Britain's market is feeling the global rout *and* a home-grown budget crisis. The Guardian reported on September 1 that **30-year gilt yields hit 5.89%**, the highest since 1998, while 10-year yields touched **5.25%** — their highest since the 2008 financial crisis.

The timing is politically brutal. Chancellor John Healey faces an Autumn Budget on October 28, and higher yields directly shrink his fiscal headroom against Labour's self-imposed rules. Every basis point rise in the cost of debt service means less room for public spending or tax cuts. The Office for Budget Responsibility will have to factor these elevated yields into its forecasts, potentially forcing difficult choices before Healey even steps to the dispatch box.

The Bank of England watches the same oil-price and inflation data as the Fed. If energy costs stay high, the BoE may need to hold or raise rates — even as the UK economy barely grows. Gilts are caught in a pincer: global yield pressure from abroad, fiscal pressure from home.

## Europe: Germany's safe-haven status is cracking

For years, German Bunds were the quiet corner of the global bond market — the asset you bought when things got scary. Not anymore. Euronews and Trading Economics report that **Germany's 10-year yield climbed above 3.3%** on September 1, the highest since May 2011. The 30-year Bund yield surged above 3.84%, also a 15-year high.

French 10-year OAT yields rose above 4.215%, their highest since November 2008. The spread between French and German debt — a barometer of eurozone stress — is widening as markets question France's budget credibility.

The drivers are familiar but compounding: **oil and gas prices rising** (reviving inflation fears), **Germany issuing more debt for infrastructure and defence**, and **ECB rate expectations shifting**. Traders now price the ECB deposit rate near 2.70% by December, implying a real chance of another hike. The old assumption that Europe would simply glide toward easier policy has been retired.

## Why it matters

The common thread across all three regions is a **repricing of duration and credibility**.

"Duration" is market shorthand for sensitivity to interest rates: long bonds get hit hardest when rates rise. The US has the deepest market and the largest fiscal hole. The UK has the sharpest political spotlight with a Budget looming. Europe has new spending plans (defence, green transition) and uneven national budgets that make the eurozone's monetary union harder to manage.

If inflation stays sticky — and oil above $90 makes that likely — central banks cannot cut. If they cannot cut, yields stay high. If yields stay high, governments pay more to service debt, which either crowds out spending or forces more borrowing. The bond market is doing the job it has done for centuries: sending an invoice. This one comes in a surprisingly large font.
