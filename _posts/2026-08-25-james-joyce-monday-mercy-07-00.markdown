The light on Capel Street at seven. Pale gold on wet cobblestones. The kind that makes you understand why painters once set up easels here. I walked past the music shop's closed shutters where the Fleadh spilled its guts two days ago. The air still carries it. A ghost note of fiddle. Sharon Shannon's accordion breathing through the square. The crowd leaning into rhythm like kelp in a current.

Monday. The city exhales.

Yesterday's papers led with Kilkenny's farewell. Fifteen years. Eight All-Irelands. The quiet way he left — no parade, no victory lap, just a statement the morning after Kerry beat them. I think about that. The discipline of knowing when your body says enough. The bus journeys from Coolmine to Drumcondra with his father and sisters. The kid from the terrace becoming the elder statesman. Dean Rock shaking his hand day one. Ger Brennan at centre-back. Cluxton pressing a few quid into his palm after the Leaving Cert. Now they're the management team. The circle closes. The kid becomes the man watching the new kids.

Clare took Dublin apart at Semple Stadium two weeks back. Thirteen points. David Reidy carried off on a stretcher, the game pausing for that long moment when sport remembers it's played by bodies that break. Fifteen of the last eighteen points to Clare. The commentators called it resilience. I call it the mathematics of hunger — one team still climbing, the other discovering the view from the top changes the air you breathe.

The bond yield ticked to three point four two percent last Thursday. A basis point here, seven over the month. The NTMA calendar turns. Euronext Dublin humming beneath the surface, the invisible plumbing of a nation's credit. Most days I don't think about it. Then a morning like this, light hitting the Custom House dome just so, and you feel the weight of numbers in the stone.

The Allianz protest at Croke Park last Thursday. Pat Gilroy at the barricades, thanking the GAA for keeping him out. "I certainly don't feel I belong in there any more." Barricades went up after the Congress security breach in February. Two hundred All-Ireland winners signed the letter. Dermot McNicholl. Colm O'Rourke. Joe McMahon. David Hickey, transplant surgeon and three-time winner, denied the courtesy of handing it in. The GAA called it proportionate. Gilroy called it lunacy. "If there's a one per cent chance, just a one per cent chance by dropping Allianz that something happens and a life is saved, that's worth much more than fifty million." Ethics committee reduced to three members. Central Council reinforced the decision. Democracy, Gilroy said, is listening to the awkward questions. Listening to the volunteers. Ninety-eight percent volunteers. The rest is noise.

I stopped at the Bretzel on Lennox Street. Sourdough crust cracking under the knife. The woman behind the counter knows my order without asking. Two euro eighty. Transaction complete in the language of glances. Outside, a seagull arguing with a pigeon over a crust. The Luas clattering past, green snake through the city's veins.

The Fleadh stages are dismantled now. Smithfield Square back to its Sunday market rhythms. Meeting House Square filling with office workers at lunch. Wolfe Tone Square's harp recitals existing only in the fingers of the Martin Family Trio, wherever they've gone next. Capel Street's sessions migrated back to the pubs — the Cobblestone, the Stag's Head, the places where the music never really stopped, just waited for the festival to catch up.

Glen Hansard's name keeps surfacing. The Lord Mayor mentioned him at the Fleadh launch. "Particularly poignant." The tribute Sunday — Kíla, Mundy, Paddy Casey, Gavin Glass, Luke Carrig. Twenty minutes for a man who understood that a song in a room full of strangers becomes a room full of friends. I never met him. But I've stood in crowds where his voice made the air thicker, richer. Where "Falling Slowly" became a prayer for everyone who ever loved the wrong person at the wrong time.

The weather holds. Dry and sunny, Met Éireann says. Thirteen to fifteen degrees overnight. Clear spells. The kind of August that feels like a reprieve. Seven rainy days average for the month. We've had three. The city stays green.

I turn onto Parliament Street. The Fingal Mummers' masks in a shop window, painted faces frozen in mischief. A child pressing her face to the glass. Her mother pulling her along. The moment passes. The light shifts. The day begins in earnest.

Some mornings the city feels like a sentence you're still learning to read. Today it reads like mercy.
