Over the past two days, government bond markets across the United States, United Kingdom, and Europe have been caught in a violent tug-of-war. Yields — the interest rate investors demand to lend money to governments — surged to multi-year and in some cases multi-decade highs, then pulled back sharply when the U.S. Treasury stepped in with emergency liquidity support. The episode laid bare how fragile sentiment has become as investors grapple with three simultaneous pressures: a widening conflict in the Middle East that threatens to keep oil prices elevated, government borrowing that shows no sign of slowing, and central banks that may be forced to keep interest rates higher for longer.

## United States: Treasuries Test 20-Year Highs, Then Stabilize

The benchmark 10-year U.S. Treasury yield climbed to 4.74% on August 21, while the 30-year yield briefly pierced 5.34% — its highest level since 2007. The selloff was driven by a toxic mix of ballooning federal deficits, a surge in corporate debt issuance, and uncertainty about the policy direction under Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh. Long-dated bonds, which are most sensitive to inflation and fiscal expectations, bore the brunt of the selling.

The turning point came on August 19, when the U.S. Treasury announced it would at least double the size of its buyback operations for longer-dated securities, injecting at least $4 billion per operation. This effectively acts as a backstop for the market, assuring investors there will be a buyer for the flood of long-term debt the government needs to issue. Yields dropped by as much as 10 basis points (0.10 percentage points) in response, and the ripple effect crossed the Atlantic, halting the selloff in European bonds too.

Why it matters: Higher long-term yields feed directly into mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and the government's own interest bill. The Congressional Budget Office projects net interest costs will exceed $1 trillion annually within the decade. If yields stay elevated, every sector of the economy feels the drag.

## United Kingdom: Gilts Hit 19-Year Highs Ahead of October Budget

Britain's bond market has been the most dramatic. On August 19, the government sold a new 10-year gilt at a yield of 5.155% — the highest rate since August 2007. The same maturity was yielding just 5.04% a month earlier. Thirty-year gilt yields touched 5.86%, kissing the post-1998 peak reached during the early days of the U.S.–Iran conflict.

The backdrop is a "perfect storm" for UK debt. Chancellor John Healey faces an October 28 Budget while Prime Minister Andy Burnham's spending plans — council house building, social care reform, defence increases — collide with the highest borrowing costs in the G7. July inflation came in at 2.9% (up from 2.6%), though services inflation eased to 3.4%, giving the Bank of England cover to hold rates at 3.75%. Societe Generale and MUFG both see the BoE staying on hold unless the Middle East conflict escalates further and drives energy prices higher.

Why it matters: Every 0.1 percentage point rise in gilt yields adds billions to the UK's annual debt servicing costs. With the debt-to-GDP ratio near 100%, the fiscal arithmetic for the Autumn Budget just got significantly harder.

## Europe: Bunds, OATs, and BTPs All Under Pressure

Europe's bond market mirrored the U.S. move, with the German 10-year Bund yield hitting 3.275% — its highest since 2011 — before retreating to 3.26%. France's 10-year OAT yield reached 4.13%, a 17-year high, while Italy's 10-year BTP yield climbed above 4.1% for the first time since March. The spread between French and German 10-year yields widened to 85 basis points, reflecting growing concern about France's 2027 budget negotiations and next year's presidential election.

The European Central Bank is now expected to raise its deposit rate to 2.76% by March 2027 (from 2.25% currently), with money markets pricing a 90% chance of a September hike. Germany plans record bond issuance — Commerzbank sees €400 billion in gross supply for 2027 — while Italy must refinance debt equivalent to 17% of GDP this year, the highest in the eurozone.

Why it matters: The eurozone's fragmented fiscal rules mean countries with high debt (Italy, France) face a vicious cycle: higher yields increase borrowing costs, which worsen debt dynamics, which push yields higher still. The ECB's transmission protection instrument (TPI) remains untested at these levels.

## The Common Thread: Oil, War, and Fiscal Credibility

The common catalyst across all three regions is the U.S.–Iran conflict. With the Strait of Hormuz effectively constrained, Brent crude has hovered near $91 a barrel. Oil at these levels feeds directly into headline inflation, forcing central banks to keep policy restrictive even as domestic economies slow. At the same time, governments are issuing record amounts of debt — the U.S. Treasury, UK DMO, and German Finanzagentur are all tapping markets aggressively — and investors are demanding a higher "term premium" to absorb it.

The Treasury's buyback announcement provided a temporary circuit breaker, but it doesn't solve the underlying imbalance: too much supply, not enough natural demand, and a geopolitical wildcard that could reignite inflation at any moment.
