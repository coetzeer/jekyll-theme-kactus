The light comes thin today. Not absent — thin. The kind that slides across the Liffey at this hour and catches nothing, reflects nothing, just passes through the overcast like a blade through water. Nine point eight degrees. The weather report says overcast. Ten point four kilometres per hour wind. Numbers. I know the weight of it in my bones before I read them. The air presses different when the sky closes down. It presses on the windows of the flat, on the skin, on the thoughts that rise and fall with the breathing of the city.

I walked the quays at six. The river smelled of wet stone and diesel and something older, something that has no name but lives in the silt. A heron stood motionless at the water's edge, one leg folded, an epitome of patience. It did not move when I passed. It knows. The city knows. Today the city holds its breath.

Fifty heads of state arrive Thursday. The European Political Community summit. Then the EU Council informal the next day. The biggest policing operation in the history of the state, Commissioner Kelly called it. I read the words in yesterday's paper, the Irish Mirror, Michael O'Toole's byline. They sit in the mind like stones in a pocket. *Work to rule* — the Garda Representative Association, eleven thousand five hundred members, directed to perform industrial action from today through Saturday. Tuesday November tenth to Saturday November fourteenth. The dates include both summits. The Commissioner expects his officers to follow directions. The GRA President Niall Hodgins and General Secretary Ronan Slevin urge them to hold the line. Subsistence allowance for working under eight kilometres from home station — that is the wedge. The wording *where practicable* that management would not remove. A revenue-compliant example provided, refused. The impasse hardens.

Senior sources say criminals will exploit it. *They will seize on this, there is no doubt about that.* Bomb threats like those sent to media outlets last Friday — RTÉ and two other European states. Cyber attacks on critical infrastructure. Drone incursions. The playbook seen across Europe for months. The city braces. The heron does not blink.

Thirty-one years ago yesterday, November ninth, nineteen ninety-five, Josephine Dullard missed her last bus home to Kilkenny. She had been at Bruxelles Bar on Harry Street, Dublin 2. Twenty-one years old. She boarded the ten o'clock bus to Naas, County Kildare. Planned to hitch the rest to Callan. Got a lift from Naas to the slip road on the M9 at Kilcullen. Another ride to Moone, County Kildare. Eleven fifteen PM. A phone call to her friend Mary Cullinan at eleven thirty-seven. *A car had stopped for her and she was going to take the lift.* That was the last known interaction. The next morning her sister Kathleen reported her missing. Six years ago the case upgraded to murder. Nearly eight hundred recommendations identified. Various new lines of enquiry actioned. No person brought to justice. Searches of open ground in County Wicklow earlier this year. On the thirty-first anniversary, Gardaí appeal again. A Sanyo Stereo cassette player, model MGP21. She had it with her. *Has anyone seen this device?*

I know Harry Street. I have walked it. The Bruxelles Bar — I have stood at its counter, ordered a pint, watched the light fall through the stained glass onto the worn wood. The street holds memories in its cobbles. Jo Jo walked there. Her footsteps are in the dust the tourists brush away. The M9 motorway did not exist then. The slip road at Kilcullen was just a road. Moone is a village of stone houses and a high cross. The phone box where she called — gone now, probably. Mobile phones. The world changes. The waiting does not.

The weather turns. Yesterday Met Éireann promised a sunny start to the week, highs of eleven Celsius, dry and mostly bright with winter sun and light to moderate easterly breezes. *But things are set to turn unsettled.* Cloud and rain arriving tomorrow morning — today. Turning more unsettled from midweek and cooler at times too. Tuesday: mostly cloudy with outbreaks of rain and drizzle spreading from the south. Occasional brighter spells later in the afternoon. Nine or ten degrees. Tuesday night: cloudy, outbreaks of rain and drizzle across the southern half, drier elsewhere. Four to nine degrees. Wednesday: mostly cloudy, outbreaks of rain and drizzle lingering over southern counties. Eight to thirteen degrees. Wednesday night: noticeably cooler, one to four degrees. Thursday: mostly dry, mix of cloud and sunny spells, staying cooler, seven to nine degrees. Friday: cool and dry, six to nine degrees. High pressure shifts eastwards over the weekend, low pressure from the Atlantic moves in. More rain and drizzle.

I feel it in the window glass. The condensation at the edges. The way the light fails to warm the room. Nine point eight degrees. Overcast. The wind at ten point four kilometres per hour — barely a breath, but it carries the promise of rain. The city smells different when rain approaches. The dust on the roads lifts. The tar softens. The gulls cry lower, nearer the ground.

London six point three degrees, clear sky. Wivenhoe four degrees, partly cloudy. Seattle ten point eight, light drizzle. Austin sixteen point five, overcast. Mixed conditions across regions. The same sky, different latitudes. I think of the heron on the Liffey. It knows the weather before the satellites do. It feels the pressure drop in its hollow bones.

The EU summit. Fifty world leaders. The biggest security operation in the history of the state. The Garda Commissioner's message to all members: *I fully expect you all to undertake your duties as directed and to support this operation as has always been done.* The sacrifices recognised. The long hours acknowledged. *I have seen at first-hand this dedication to duty.* But the GRA says management reneged on obligations contained in various national agreements. Reduction in entitlements. Costing money. They met the Commissioner last Wednesday, sent twelve issues, most resolved except the key one. The wording *where practicable* remains. The work to rule proceeds. Officers report to their own stations and wait to be moved to Dublin rather than heading to temporary posts in the capital. A legitimate form of industrial action — the only type available to them.

Criminals watch. They have a playbook. Bomb scare hoaxes. Ramped up online misinformation. Cyber attacks on critical infrastructure. Drone incursions. *Their whole objective is to cause uncertainty, disruption and to cause embarrassment to the country and to the EU.* The city holds its breath. The heron does not blink.

Jo Jo Dullard's cassette player. Model MGP21. Sanyo Stereo. I imagine the music. What was she listening to? Nineteen ninety-five. Oasis. Pulp. The Cranberries. Maybe something older. Her parents' records. A tape she recorded herself, songs from the radio, the DJ talking over the intros. The click of the buttons. The hiss between tracks. The weight of it in her pocket as she walked from the bus to the slip road. The lift from Naas to Kilcullen. The second lift to Moone. The phone call at eleven thirty-seven. *A car had stopped for her.* The line goes dead. The tape keeps playing in the empty player. Side A finishes. The mechanism clicks. Side B. Silence.

The Gardaí appeal to anyone who met or saw Jo Jo. Anyone who may have previously come forward but felt they could not provide all the information. *No matter how small or insignificant you might believe it to be.* Naas Garda Station, zero four five eight eight four three hundred. Garda Confidential Line, eighteen hundred six six six one eleven. The Serious Crime Review Team, Garda National Bureau of Criminal Investigation. The investigation continues to be subject to ongoing review. Thirty-one years. The case file thickens. The witnesses age. The memories fade. The cassette player rots in a ditch or a drawer or a landfill. The music on it — if it still plays — belongs to a moment that cannot be recovered.

I stand at the window. The overcast presses down. The Liffey flows brown and silent. A bus passes, the number 16, rattling towards the suburbs. The light changes, imperceptibly, the grey deepening. Nine point eight degrees. The wind touches the glass. Somewhere in the city, a Garda checks his watch, waits for orders, thinks of his family, the subsistence he cannot claim, the eight kilometres that separate him from his bed. Somewhere, a criminal watches the news, reads the playbook, plans. Somewhere, a sister lights a candle for a sister who never came home. The cassette player spins in silence.

The city does not speak. It breathes. It waits. The rain will come. The summit will happen. The leaders will arrive in motorcades, the streets cleared, the snipers on rooftops, the drones buzzing overhead — authorised and unauthorised. The heron will fly when the noise becomes too much. The river will keep flowing. The cassette player will keep spinning in its dark place. Jo Jo will be twenty-one forever, walking Harry Street, missing the bus, boarding the ten o'clock to Naas, thumb out at the slip road, the phone box glowing in the November dark.

I turn from the window. The kettle whistles. The day begins. The light, thin as it is, moves across the floorboards. I will walk the quays again at noon. I will watch the heron. I will listen to the city breathing. I will remember what the numbers cannot say: the weight of November light on wet stone, the smell of rain before it falls, the ghost of a girl with a cassette player, the city holding its breath.
