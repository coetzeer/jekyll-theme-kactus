The light this morning has no edge to it. Seventeen degrees and overcast, the sky a single sheet of grey paper held at arm's length over the city. No shadows. No glare. Just this diffuse glow that makes everything look like it's been dipped in milk — the brick of the Georgian doors, the wet gleam on the cobblestones near Christ Church, the face of the woman on the 16 bus who keeps checking her phone like it might deliver news she's not ready for.

I know this light. It's the light that sits on Dublin in late September, the light that remembers summer but won't admit it's gone. The meteorologists called it 17.7°C and overcast, 5 km/h wind from the southwest. They don't say how it feels in the joints. They don't say how it makes the Liffey look like beaten steel, or how the gulls cry differently when the air hangs this heavy.

***

A boy walks into the Criminal Courts of Justice on Parkgate Street today. Nineteen, maybe twenty. His face will be in the papers tomorrow, or maybe it won't — the courts have their own logic about what the public needs to know. He's charged in connection with the death of Siminic Rostas, twenty-four, who died in Beaumont Hospital on September 1st after a confrontation at an abandoned premises in South County Business Park, Leopardstown. Eight arrests now. Poles, sticks, shovels. CCTV captured what the witnesses couldn't articulate. The court heard that Rostas was struck with a shovel, struck again after he was unconscious. Self-defence, they both claimed. The shovel doesn't care about claims.

I've walked past that business park. Grey units, boarded windows, the kind of place that exists in the peripheral vision of the city — where people live in the walls because the city has no other room for them. Fifteen people, the gardaí said, had been living there for months. Fifteen lives in the margins. A group arrived to collect personal property, the dispute escalated, and now a mother somewhere has a son who won't come home, and another mother has a son who walks into a courtroom at 10:30 on a Monday morning.

The light doesn't distinguish. It falls on the courthouse steps the same way it falls on the wet grass in Saggart Cemetery.

***

Harry Strumble. Nineteen, maybe twenty. A hurler for Commercials in Rathcoole, a footballer for St Mary's in Saggart. "An amazing smile that lit up a room" — the club's words, not mine. He died on Wednesday, September 9th, after what they call a brave illness battle. His funeral mass was Saturday in the Church of the Nativity of the Blessed Virgin Mary. Saggart Cemetery. The same grey light on the headstones.

I didn't know him. But I know the rhythm of a GAA club in September — the training sessions thinning out, the championship winding down, the way a parish holds its breath when one of its own goes quiet. "He never passed you without saying hello and always with that gorgeous smile." That's the detail that stays. Not the medals. Not the underage titles. The hello. The smile. The acknowledgement that you exist, that you're seen.

His mother Deborah. His father David. Sophie and Davina, his sisters. The club statement reads like a litany: *heartbroken, deeply saddened, deepest sympathy, love and support, privilege of knowing him, never be forgotten.* Words stretched thin by repetition, trying to carry a weight no words can carry. *Ar dheis Dé go raibh a anam, ní bheidh a leithéid ann arís.* On the right hand of God may his soul be, his like will not be again.

The Irish has a music English loses. The rhythm of it holds the grief differently.

***

Someone in Dublin woke up a millionaire on Sunday. The first Lotto Plus 2 winner at the new €1 million top prize since the game changed. Numbers: 05, 13, 19, 21, 23, 42, bonus 18. Quick Pick ticket, bought the day of the draw. The retail location hasn't been announced yet. Could be the Centra on the corner. Could be the Applegreen on the N11. Could be the Tesco on Pearse Street where another Dublin player won €21,756 matching five and the bonus in the main draw. Numbers there: 1, 3, 9, 14, 16, 19, bonus 27.

I think about the weight of that ticket in a pocket. The ordinary morning that preceded it — coffee, toast, the same bus, the same grey light. The moment the numbers aligned and the ordinary cracked open. Emma Monaghan from the National Lottery called it a "truly special milestone." Milestones are for roads. This is a life. One moment you're checking tickets in the kitchen, the next you're signing the back of a piece of paper and calling 1800 666 222.

The winners in Dublin and Sligo — €21,756 each. Not life-changing in the millionaire sense. But a roof repaired. A car replaced. A breath held for months, released. The Sligo winner bought their ticket at Milo's on Main Street in Enniscrone. I've never been to Enniscrone. But I know the feeling of a small shop in a coastal town, the salt in the air, the lottery terminal humming in the corner like a small altar.

***

The weather experts — Exacta Weather, Met Éireann — they're calling for a shift. The 23-degree highs of the past days, "unseasonably warm" they keep saying, as if seasons have contracts they're bound to honour. A cooler front moving down from the north. Heavy, thundery showers. Monday evening into Tuesday. Then high pressure building again, warmer, longer, maybe into the third week of September. The models agree. The GFS runs, the ECM. They speak in pressure systems and temperature anomalies. They don't speak in the smell of rain on hot tarmac. They don't speak in the way a city exhales when the heat breaks.

Today, though. Today is the pause. 17.7 degrees. Overcast. The breath held.

***

I walked past the South County Business Park once. Late afternoon, winter maybe. The units stood empty, windows dark, a FOR LEASE sign bleaching in the sun that wasn't really sun. I thought: people sleep here. People wake here. People argue about property and pride and whatever else makes men pick up shovels. The Luas rattled past on the green line, full of people going home to dinners and televisions and the ordinary violence of family life — the sharp word, the slammed door, the silence that settles like dust.

Now a boy walks into a courtroom. Now a mother in Rathcoole visits a grave in Saggart. Now someone in Dublin — or maybe Sligo — holds a ticket and wonders if the light will ever feel different on their face.

The gulls cry. The 16 bus pulls away. The woman with the phone puts it in her bag, unresolved.

This grey light. It doesn't judge. It doesn't explain. It just falls — on the courthouse, the cemetery, the corner shop, the abandoned units in Leopardstown, the wet cobblestones, the back of my hand resting on the windowsill.

Seventeen degrees. Overcast. The city breathing.

*Ar dheis Dé go raibh a anam.*
