# Bond Markets Retrospective: US, UK, and Europe — September 4, 2026

## The short version

A full-blown global bond sell-off deepened over the past 48 hours before a partial reprieve arrived Thursday. Yields — the interest rates investors demand to hold government debt — surged across the United States, United Kingdom, eurozone, and Japan, then eased slightly as markets digested dovish Fed signals and awaited Friday's US payrolls report. The catalyst was a convergence of three forces: a new wave of US-Iran military clashes in the Persian Gulf that pushed Brent crude above $95 a barrel, stubbornly high inflation that keeps central banks on a tightening path, and governments that simply cannot stop borrowing. Bond prices fall when yields rise, so this has been a painful stretch for existing bondholders. The message from markets is clear: the cost of capital is structurally higher, and policymakers have fewer easy exits than they pretend.

## United States: fiscal scale meets geopolitical shock

The US 10-year Treasury yield climbed above **4.81% on September 2**, its highest level since October 2023, while the 30-year touched a post-2008 peak. The selloff was fueled by a mix of heavy government debt issuance, an oil-price shock that reignited inflation concerns, and expectations that the Federal Reserve may keep monetary policy tighter for longer. On Wednesday, Deutsche Bank estimated that 10-year yields could climb to roughly **5.5% over the next year** before coupon income outweighs capital losses.

Thursday brought a pause. Treasury yields moved lower across the curve after **Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said he was "leaning toward keeping interest rates unchanged"** at the September meeting — a shift from the recent hawkish tone that had markets pricing a 66% chance of a rate hike. The 10-year yield fell more than 2 basis points to **4.77%**, and the 30-year dropped to **5.25%**. The decline came as **initial jobless claims rose slightly to 206,000** for the week ended August 29, pointing to a stable but not overheating labor market. All eyes now turn to Friday's August nonfarm payrolls report, which could tip the Fed's hand.

## United Kingdom: budget headroom under siege

The UK felt the selloff acutely. On Tuesday (September 2), London markets — closed Monday for a bank holiday — caught up with the global rout. The **30-year gilt yield hit 5.89%**, its highest level since early 1998. The **10-year gilt yield reached ~5.25%**, the highest since the 2008 global financial crisis.

This isn't just a market move — it's a fiscal crisis in the making. **Chancellor John Healey's headroom against Labour's fiscal rules could be almost halved** at his October 28 budget if yields persist. Deutsche Bank's Sanjay Raja estimated headroom would fall from £26 billion (projected in the spring forecast) to **£13.8 billion** before any new spending plans. Almost all of the deterioration comes from higher government interest costs — the Office for Budget Responsibility had assumed gilt yields at 5.1% for this year, but they are running well above that. Yields eased back slightly later Tuesday to **5.85% (30-year) and 5.21% (10-year)**, and on Thursday the 10-year eased **9.5 basis points** as a global bond rally lifted sentiment. But they remain far above the levels baked into fiscal forecasts.

## Europe: Bunds at 15-year highs, periphery spreads compressed

Germany's 10-year Bund yield surged to **3.38–3.40% on September 2–3**, the highest since **April 2011** — a 15-year high. The move was driven by rising oil prices, eurozone inflation data showing price growth at its highest in nearly three years, and money markets pricing nearly a 100% chance of a **25-basis-point ECB rate hike to 2.5% next week**, with further increases expected by mid-2027. ECB policymakers Olli Rehn and Martin Kocher warned that prolonged conflict and rising inflation risks could warrant further tightening.

By Thursday, Bund yields eased to **~3.36%** as Brent crude retreated from six-week highs and natural gas prices fell. The Eurozone bond losing streak snapped after six days.

Notably, **peripheral spreads have compressed dramatically**. The spread between French 10-year OATs and German Bunds narrowed to around **5–10 basis points** (French yield ~3.60%, Italian BTP ~3.71%). In September 2022, the Italy-Germany spread was 251 basis points; by January 2026 it had dropped to 59 bps, and it has compressed further during this selloff. This unusual convergence suggests investors are treating core and peripheral eurozone debt similarly — perhaps reflecting a broader repricing of all sovereign risk rather than credit differentiation.

## Japan: breaking the 3% barrier

Japan's 10-year JGB yield held **above 3%** for the first time since 1996 — a symbolic and practical milestone. The Bank of Japan's yield curve control has effectively ended, and Japanese yields are now tracking global peers higher. This matters because Japanese investors have been massive buyers of foreign bonds; as domestic yields rise, the carry trade unwinds, adding to selling pressure globally.

## What it means for you

- **Governments**: Refinancing maturing debt at higher rates will progressively increase interest costs and strain public finances. France stands out among developed markets for combining large deficits, elevated debt, and political uncertainty. Emerging markets with twin deficits face a dual hit from higher borrowing costs and funding risks.
- **Companies**: Leveraged firms face rising debt service costs. Rate-sensitive sectors (real estate, utilities) are most exposed.
- **Consumers**: Mortgage rates, auto loans, and credit card debt are benchmarked to government yields. Higher yields transmit directly to household budgets.
- **Investors**: The silver lining — new bond buyers now get meaningful coupon income. Yields around 4.8–5.3% on 10-year paper provide a cushion against further price declines that didn't exist in the low-yield era. But equities face a growing competitor: "At some point, higher yields are a painful experience for equities," said Natalia Lojevsky of CIFC Asset Management.
