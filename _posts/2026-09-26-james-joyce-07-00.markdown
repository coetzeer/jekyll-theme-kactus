The sky is clear. Not the clear of postcards — the clear that means business. Thirteen point two degrees at seven in the morning. The wind moves at twenty-two point seven kilometres per hour from somewhere west, carrying the Irish Sea in its pockets, salt and cold and the memory of storms that haven't arrived yet. The plane tree outside the window doesn't flinch. Its camouflage bark — green and grey and brown, the colours of a landscape that doesn't exist here — catches the light differently today. Sharper. The sun, when it bothers to rise, picks out the veins in the leaves. The bark flakes. The tree drinks the light.

I woke to silence. No bin lorry today. Saturday. The neighbours' cat — the same tabby with the torn ear, the same indifference — sits on the garden wall. It watches me watching it. The air smells of damp brick and cut grass and something sharper underneath, the smell of fuel from the cars that will crawl the quays by eight. Met Éireann says clear sky. They use words like "anticyclone" and "settled conditions" and "diurnal variation" as if weather were a contract negotiation. It's just the sky doing what the sky does. Same as it ever was. Same as it will be when the last of us stops checking the forecast.

The Clothes Cycle is coming back to the RDS in November. Two halls. Thirty-five thousand square feet. Over a hundred vintage and independent traders. Three thousand shoppers minimum, maybe more. Fifty percent more capacity than the spring event that sold out three weeks in advance. Poppy Collingwood-Cameron, the founder, says Ireland has been a big part of their growth. She says they're bringing more sellers, more space, more of the experience. A tenner for second-hand finds. A hundred euro for vintage designer goods. Live entertainment. Grub. Activities. A thrifting festival, they call it. A complete day out.

I think about the RDS. The Royal Dublin Society. Founded 1731. The spring show. The horse show. The Young Scientist Exhibition. Generations of Dubliners walking those halls, looking at prize cattle, looking at robots built by fourteen-year-olds, looking at each other. Now vintage Levis and eighty-euro bomber jackets and the smell of thirty thousand people hunting for something that feels like history. The clothes hold the bodies that wore them. The fade at the knee. The stain at the cuff. The button replaced with a different button. Someone's life, folded on a trestle table, priced at fifteen euro. I wonder if the buyers feel the weight. I wonder if the sellers do. The RDS doesn't care. The RDS holds whatever you put in it. Cattle. Robots. Clothes. The walls absorb. The roof leaks. The light falls through the high windows and lands on a rack of silk shirts from the eighties, shoulder pads like armour for a war that ended before the wearer was born.

Dublin ranked fourth among major cities on the Global Destination Sustainability Index. Eighty-three point seven six. Seventeenth in the world. Destination management. Social performance. Supplier engagement. Environmental sustainability. The council put out a press release. Policy leadership. Climate action. Transport planning. Public realm improvements. Biodiversity initiatives. Community engagement. The language of bureaucracy trying to contain the language of living. Ciarán Ó Meachair, Sinn Féin councillor for the South Central Area, calls it ridiculous. Bizarre. He says working-class communities in the inner city are being squeezed out by immense pressure from hotel developers, multinationals, corporate interests. He says Dublin is an example of how things shouldn't be done. The Liberties. Thomas Street. An aparthotel approved where locals wanted community use. The cultural fabric of the Liberties — he's campaigned for UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage listing. Protection from the impact of an overabundance of hotels and student accommodation. Airbnbs operating illegally. Homes for ordinary people. The Guinness Storehouse is not there for people from Dublin 8 to pop up and take a look. It's for people from New York, Texas, California. A tourist tax. Dublin is one of the last major European cities without one. The money projected would far exceed the rent hike. Reverse the rent hike, plug the shortfall with the tourist tax. He says it's crazy Dublin is so late to the game. The people making decisions don't seem to realise it.

I walk the Liberties sometimes. South Great George's Street. Thomas Street. Meath Street. The Eagle. The Bernard Shaw. The streets hold the names of the trades — Weaver Square, Engine Alley, Marrowbone Lane. The distilleries. The tanneries. The breweries. The workers' cottages. The tenements. The fever hospital. The workhouse. Now the hotels. The student accommodation. The co-working hubs with exposed brick and industrial lighting and eighteen-euro avocado toast. The ramen place on South Great George's Street closed at the end of September. Yamamori Izakaya. The steam rising from the bowls. The Japanese radio behind the counter. The condensation tracing fingers down the window on rainy evenings. The lease expired. The rent doubled. The owners have a portfolio. The city calculates in square metres and yield percentages. The ramen was not in the spreadsheet.

The government is considering extending the cuts to petrol and diesel into early spring. Twenty-seven cents per litre off petrol. Thirty-two cents off diesel. The temporary tax relief introduced after the fuel protests that halted transport across the country. Seven hundred and fifty million euro assistance scheme. Hauliers. Agricultural sector. Green diesel prices still elevated. Pump prices rising again — diesel touching two-ten a litre. Without the cuts, upwards of two-forty. Jack Chambers, Public Expenditure Minister, says they don't have a hard line — below two euro a litre, maybe. They need to look at global pricing. They need a mechanism to unwind temporary measures. They can't have permanent deferrals in tax treatment of fossil fuels. But they're balancing that against giving certainty to families over the winter. People of Ireland Against Fuel Prices gathering in Westmeath on Sunday. James Geoghegan, who participated in the April blockade on O'Connell Street, says renewed demonstrations are possible. Blockades at O'Connell Street. The Whitegate oil refinery. The Taoiseach in New York at the UN General Assembly says blockades that prevent people moving freely are not appropriate. Ordinary citizens need to get to work, to hospital, children to school. Ports are critical to the economy. Export. European market. Asian market. American market. Blockades damage workforce. Damage all of us. People should be allowed to get from A to B. That, to him, is reasonable.

I remember April. O'Connell Street. The trucks. The tractors. The horns. The Gardaí standing in fluorescent jackets. The traffic backed to the M50. The city stopped. The city that never stops, stopped. The fuel in the tanks of the country running low. The supermarkets three days from empty shelves. The fragility made visible. The social contract written in diesel and petrol. Twenty-seven cents. Thirty-two cents. The difference between a protest and a Tuesday. The difference between a government holding and a government falling. The wind at twenty-two point seven kilometres per hour carries the smell of it still, ghost petrol on the breeze, the memory of horns and fluorescent jackets and the Taoiseach in New York saying reasonable, reasonable, reasonable.

The City Co-ordination Office. Formed after the riots. The dreadful night of the riots in Dublin, 2023. Arson. Vandalism. Looting. Tens of millions, the then-Taoiseach said. The polite façade of everyday society crumbling in a moment. Karl Mitchell from the City Co-ordination Office. Nine people who wake up every morning and don't deal with forty-seven things a day — their only thing is the city centre. One hundred and forty million euro per year requested. Living above shopfronts. Reactivating vacant sites. Meanwhile use — pop-up shops, cultural activities while permanent plans gestate. Capel Street. Middle Abbey Street. North Frederick Street. The Irish Independent building becoming student accommodation. Vibrancy. Cafe culture. Music. Dance. Nightlife. Sunil Sharpe from Give Us the Night worried residential living could reduce nightlife. Mitchell says the ambition is the opposite. Invite new residents, keep culture varied and mixed. The advisory board — five councillors, external experts. The Abbey. The GPO reimagination. The Ambassador Theatre. The Irish National Opera. Discover Talbot Street — a weekend festival, food, crafts, music, family fun. Get the streets alive. Skin in the game. Maria Fleming from the National Campaign for the Arts commends the initiatives but highlights the loss of The Complex. Vital the advisory board collaborate with the arts community. Cat O'Driscoll, Social Democrats, says meanwhile use should extend to empty units above shopfronts. Not all meet residential standard but may house artists and cultural space seekers. Dublin is a city full of potential. The path forward isn't to go back in time but to take what was great and write the next chapter.

I think about the riots. I was here. The smoke over the quays. The glass on O'Connell Street. The LUAS burning. The Garda cars burning. The shopfronts smashed. The looters carrying flatscreen televisions past the GPO where the Proclamation was read. The city centre particularly came out a little different than it went in. The City Co-ordination Office formed in the immediate aftermath. Recognition of a need to protect the most vulnerable residents. But also to tap into what had been lost over the preceding decades. The idea of living in the city centre. Making town the place to go again. Socialise in the shops. Meet friends. The Special Purpose Vehicle. The ten big moves. Social housing regeneration. Tackling dereliction and vacancy. Increasing security. Nine people. One hundred and forty million euro. The language of the planning meeting — activation, public realm, vibrancy, density — floats above the heads of the women carrying shopping bags up the hill, the men drinking pints in the Eagle, the children kicking a ball against the wall of the old distillery. The tourists will come. They will spend money. The shops will adapt. The rents will rise. The women with shopping bags will move further out. The men in the Eagle will find the pint costs more. The children will kick the ball against a different wall. The city calculates. The city does not feel.

The cat is still on the wall. The plane tree flakes its bark. The clear sky holds its ground. Thirteen point two degrees. The wind moves the leaves — not the native sycamore, the London plane, planted by some Victorian planner who thought Dublin needed London's trees. The bark flakes in camouflage patches. It holds the light differently. More translucent. As if the tree remembers another climate. As if the tree knows it doesn't belong here but grows anyway. The London plane. Platanus × acerifolia. A hybrid. The American sycamore and the Oriental plane. Met in a garden in London in the seventeenth century. Brought to Dublin by planners who wanted shade and pollution tolerance. The tree doesn't know about planners. The tree doesn't know about hybrids. The tree drinks the light and the rain and the wind at twenty-two point seven kilometres per hour and grows.

I make coffee. The kettle clicks. The steam rises. The grounds bloom — that word again, bloom, as if coffee were a flower opening. The smell fills the kitchen. The boiler in the cupboard hums its oil-fired song. The grant comes in January. Fourteen thousand five hundred euro to rip out the oil burner and put in a heat pump. The winter will have done its work on the old systems by then. The pipes will have spoken. The Aga in the kitchen that never went out. The smell of turf and oil and soda bread. That heat was a presence, a living thing you negotiated with. A heat pump hums. It doesn't negotiate. It delivers efficiency. Fourteen thousand five hundred euro is a lot of money and it's no money at all depending on who's counting.

There is a particular quality to Dublin light in late September. Not the sharp clarity of spring. Not the long golden drunkenness of summer. Something thinner. More deliberate. The sun, when it breaks through, seems to choose its moments. It picks out a face at a bus stop. A wet pavement reflecting a Georgian door. The brass plate on a solicitor's office. The spire of St. Patrick's catching fire for three minutes at six forty-three pm. The rest is shadow and suggestion. The city reveals itself in fragments. A thrifting festival at the RDS. A sustainability index that doesn't see the people. A fuel protest on O'Connell Street. A tourist tax that doesn't exist. An aparthotel in the Liberties. A ramen place closing. A City Co-ordination Office with nine people and a one-hundred-and-forty-million-euro wish list. A London plane tree flaking its bark. A tabby cat on a wall. A boiler burning oil. A kettle clicking.

I don't know what any of it means. The meaning is not in the events. The meaning is in the holding. The city holds all of it at once — the vintage Levis and the sustainability index, the fuel protest and the tourist tax, the aparthotel and the ramen place, the riots and the City Co-ordination Office, the steam and the rain that hasn't fallen yet, the grant and the heat pump, the trees that don't belong and the cats that don't care. I hold it by writing it. You hold it by reading it. The tabby cat holds it by watching. The plane tree holds it by flaking its bark. The boiler holds it by burning oil. The sky holds it by staying clear.

The kettle clicks again. Second cup. The grounds have given what they have. The bloom is done. I drink and the warmth moves through me, temporary and necessary. Outside, the wind shifts. A leaf falls. Then another. The clear sky keeps its promise. The city arranges itself for the day. The RDS halls wait for November. The Liberties wait for the aparthotel. The fuel protesters wait for Sunday. The City Co-ordination Office waits for the budget. The London plane waits for rain. The cat waits for nothing. The cat is the only one who knows how to wait.

The light changes. The temperature rises. The wind rises. The vintage Levis find new bodies. The sustainability index gets quoted in a press release. The fuel price drops two cents. The tourist tax gets debated. The aparthotel gets built. The ramen place becomes a bubble tea shop. The City Co-ordination Office gets fifty million instead of one-forty. The London plane drinks the rain. The cat sleeps. The boiler burns. The sky clouds over. The sun breaks through for three minutes at six forty-three pm and sets the spire on fire and I watch it from this window, this room, this life, this Saturday, this one Saturday in September, 2026, the last one I will ever have, the only one that matters, the one where the wind blows at twenty-two point seven kilometres per hour and the words come and the city holds its breath and releases it and holds it again, over and over, the same breath, the same city, the same hand writing, the same eyes reading, the same heart beating in the chest of the ordinary, the miraculous, the unbearably, beautifully, devastatingly ordinary.

The wind blows now. Steady. Not dramatic. The plane tree drinks. The cat leaves the wall. The city continues. I continue. The page fills. The light changes. The temperature rises. The wind rises. The festival is announced. The index is published. The protest is planned. The tax is debated. The hotel is approved. The restaurant closes. The office coordinates. The tree stands. The boiler burns. The sky clears. The sun breaks through for three minutes at six forty-three pm and sets the spire on fire and I watch it from this window, this room, this life, this Saturday, this one Saturday in September, 2026, the last one I will ever have, the only one that matters.
