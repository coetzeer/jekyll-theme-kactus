Eleven degrees. Clear sky. The kind of morning that feels like a promise the afternoon might not keep.

I woke to light already pooling on the floorboards, pale and sharp, the way it only gets in late October when the sun stays low and the air turns crisp enough to taste. Eleven point nine degrees, the weather report says. Seven kilometres an hour of wind. Numbers that mean nothing until you step outside and feel them — the cool bite at the wrists, the way your breath doesn't plume but simply disappears, swallowed by air that holds no moisture.

High pressure building. One thousand forty-three hectopascals by Monday, they say. Close to the October record set in 2018 at Sherkin Island, one hundred forty-three point eight. I think about pressure the way I think about gravity — invisible, constant, shaping everything without announcing itself. The barometer rises and the city settles. Windows stay shut. Coats stay buttoned. The storm debris in the gutters dries and waits.

The swans have been dying at the Samuel Beckett Bridge. Seventeen in four years. Eight this year alone. The bridge opened in 2009, designed like a harp, its cables the strings of the national instrument. But swans' eyes sit on the sides of their heads — they cannot see what lies directly ahead. They fly at eighty-eight kilometres per hour, fifteen kilos of muscle and feather, and hit steel cables they never knew existed. Catastrophic, the wildlife responder said. Eight have died in 2026. High tide takes the vertical space beneath the bridge. The swans don't go under. They try to go through.

I think about this as I walk toward the river. The Samuel Beckett Bridge catches the morning light on its spine, white cables fanning against blue. Beautiful. Lethal. The swans don't know the bridge is a monument. They know only the sky, the water, the instinct that pulls them along ancient flight paths. The tide rises. The gap closes. They choose the only way they know.

Andrea Porter, the wildlife responder, says they're trying to gather proper data. Light, flight paths, tide times. Variables. She wants to approach engineers. But engineers built the harp. Engineers calculated the tensions, the loads, the wind shear. They did not calculate swans.

The Liffey runs slate-grey beneath the bridge, reflecting nothing recognizable. A few gulls. The smell of wet stone and diesel and something older, tidal, that no development will ever wash away. I stop at the rail. The cables hum — barely — with traffic vibration. A low note. The harp plays itself.

This weekend the DART+ West works begin. Clonsilla to M3 Parkway suspended. Saturday the thirty-first, Sunday the first. Replacement buses. One hundred twenty Iarnród Éireann staff working around the clock, lowering track at two bridges between Hansfield and M3 Parkway. Excavation. Overhead lines to come later. They're doubling capacity, trebling electrification. The map shows green lines spreading like veins. A team of one hundred twenty people, through the night, while the city sleeps or drinks or argues or dreams. The rails come up. The bed goes down. The future gets laid in the dark.

I think of my father taking the train to Maynooth in the eighties. Diesel carriages, smoke, the rhythm of joints in the track. He'd fall asleep and wake at his stop, never sure how. The electrification changes the sound. Changes the smell. Changes the time. The green lines on the map are someone's calculation of where people will want to go. But people go where life takes them. The map follows.

At Liffey Valley, they've built a Pumpkin Playland. Red Rotunda, near Marks and Spencer and Next. Twelve to four daily. Creepy crafts, ghoulish games, face painting. A Boo-gie Bash Disco on Saturday the thirty-first. Spin the wheel for a treat from Insomnia. Hocus Pocus in the cinema. Scream's thirtieth anniversary for the grown-ups. Free admission. The mid-term break filled with manufactured wonder.

I passed there Tuesday. The car park was full. Children in polyester capes. Parents with coffee cups, checking phones. The pumpkins were foam. The cobwebs came in plastic bags. But the laughter was real. The sugar rush was real. The tired tantrum at three forty-five was real. We build these moments now. We schedule them. We brand them. The season arrives in a shopping centre newsletter.

The high pressure holds. Clear sky. Eleven degrees. The kind of morning where you can see the Wicklow Mountains from the top of Killiney Hill — if you knew where to look, if the haze didn't blur the edge where land becomes suggestion. I once walked there with a woman who pointed at the Sugar Loaf and said it looked like a breast. I laughed. She didn't. We walked back in silence. The mountain kept its shape. The silence kept its weight.

Pressure. The word comes from the Latin *premere* — to press. The air presses down. Fourteen pounds per square inch at sea level. We don't feel it because we're built of it. We are pressure vessels. Blood pushes out. Air pushes in. Equilibrium. Until it shifts.

The record at Sherkin Island. 2018. I was in Dublin then, living in a flat above a bakery on Camden Street. The smell of soda bread at six AM. The sound of the Luas clanging past. The pressure that October was 1043.8. I didn't know. Nobody knows the pressure until it's measured. We feel the results — the headache, the joint ache, the strange clarity, the way sound carries differently. The swans feel it in their bones. The rails feel it in their expansion. The pumpkins in the shop window feel it in their slow rot.

Met Éireann says Thursday — today — mainly dry apart from isolated patches of light rain or drizzle. A mix of cloud and sunny spells. Breezy. Moderate to fresh southerly wind developing. Fourteen or fifteen degrees. Thursday night: cloudy and breezy with persistent rain or drizzle moving into the southwest and west early in the night, spreading eastwards across the western half of the country. Ten to thirteen degrees. Fresh to strong and gusty southerly winds.

Friday uncertain. Cloud and rain gradually making its way east. Persistent rain lingering. Best of the drier weather towards the east coast. Thirteen to fifteen degrees. Saturday cloudy and wet in the north and west, rain moving southeastwards, becoming lighter and patchier. Twelve to fourteen degrees. Light variable winds.

Sunday the high pressure builds. Mostly dry and calm. Sunny spells developing, although cloudier over the northern half. Cooler. Nine to thirteen degrees. Light northerly wind.

The forecast reads like a poem written by committee. Each line qualified. Each certainty hedged. *Uncertainty in the forecast from Friday.* *Latest guidance suggests.* *Best of any drier and brighter weather.* The atmosphere refuses to be pinned. We measure it. We model it. We name the storms — Chloe, they're saying, possible date spotted by Met Éireann as woeful winter looms. We give them names so we can speak to them. So we can say *Chloe took the roof off the shed* rather than *the wind did it.*

The swans don't name the wind. They fly or they don't. The tide doesn't name the moon. It rises or it falls. The rails don't name the train. They carry it or they break.

I walk back through streets waking slowly. A delivery van reverses into a loading bay. A woman opens a café, flips chairs onto tables, wipes a surface that doesn't need wiping. The smell of coffee. The sound of a radio playing something from the nineties. The light sharpens. Shadows shorten. The pressure builds, invisible, inexorable, writing its record in hectopascals on instruments at Sherkin Island and Valentia Observatory and Dublin Airport.

One thousand forty-three. Close to the record. Not warm. Not sunny. Just settled. The word *settled* does heavy lifting in Irish weather talk. It means the fighting's done. The storms have passed. The air sits still enough for you to hear your own thoughts — if you want to. If you dare.

I don't always want to.

The swans. The bridge. The rails being lowered in the dark by one hundred twenty workers. The foam pumpkins. The pressure rising. The light on the water. The woman who saw a breast in a mountain. The flat above the bakery. The soda bread. The Luas. The forecast hedging its bets. The names we give storms. The names we don't give the dead swans.

Seventeen. Eight this year. No names. Just feathers and bone and the harp's steel strings singing in a wind they cannot see.

The day opens. The city breathes. Eleven degrees. Clear sky. High pressure building. I walk on.
