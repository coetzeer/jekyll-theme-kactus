The light comes through different now. Not the golden spill of September, not the long reluctant evenings of June. This is November light — pale, thin, the colour of weak tea held up to a window. It arrives at 7:02 by the clock on the wall, though my body knew the hour before my eyes opened. The cold has a way of announcing itself through the bones first.

Two point eight degrees. The weather app says clear sky, eight kilometres per hour wind. But the app doesn't tell you how the air tastes — metallic, sharp, like you've been sucking on a coin. It doesn't tell you how the breath hangs in the room, a small cloud that says *I am here, I am still here*. Dublin at this hour is a city of ghosts and heating systems clicking on.

Last night they said it would drop to minus seven. The weather people, with their models and their satellites, their Arctic air pushing down from the north. High pressure moving close. The language of meteorology is strangely poetic when you listen to it: *sharp frost*, *icy patches*, *cold few days coming up*. A mini-freeze, they called it. As if the city were a meal left too long in the freezer, edges hardening, centre still soft.

I think about the emergency preparedness booklet that was supposed to arrive through every letterbox in Ireland. Minister Helen McEntee's voice on the radio a few months back: *storms, flooding, power outages and other emergencies that remind us of the importance of being prepared*. The checklist reads like a liturgy: two to three litres of water per person per day, stored in a cool dark place. Torch with spare batteries. Matches or lighter in a safe place. Know your gas and electricity shutoff points. Your MPRN and GPRN numbers from the bill. Battery or wind-up radio. Power banks for phones. Prescription medicines. First aid kit. Non-perishable food, rotated so nothing expires. Baby formula if you need it. Sanitary products. Documents in a waterproof bag — birth certificates, passports, insurance details, emergency contacts. Spare keys. Warm clothes. Cash and cards. A road map in case GPS fails.

A road map. When did I last hold a paper map? The very idea feels archaic now, like keeping a flint and steel beside the gas hob. But I understand the logic. When the grid goes dark, the satellites still orbit but the phones don't talk to them. We've built a world that requires electricity to navigate itself.

The burglar on Dawson Street at 4:30 a.m. wasn't thinking about emergency preparedness. Eugene Duff, forty-five, 117 previous convictions, a butcher by trade when he's not breaking into Eddie Rockets on South Anne Street. The gardaí spotted him on Dawson Street carrying two large packets of ketchup with Eddie Rockets written on them. Two packets of ketchup. Not the till. Not the safe. Ketchup.

They found €143 in cash on him, two battery packs from an iPad or iPhone, a cold can of Red Bull. The restaurant's fridge was open, Red Bull gone, till on the ground. He was too drunk to question for six hours. The judge called him a nuisance, said he seemed incapable of not being drunk, incapable of not getting into fights when drunk. Gave him a belated chance — sixteen months suspended. *We'll give you a chance*, the judge said, though the court was doubtful.

I keep turning the ketchup packets over in my mind. Why ketchup? Hunger, certainly. But why not the Red Bull too? He took a cold can of that. Maybe ketchup is comfort. Maybe it's the only thing that made sense in the cold dark at 4:30 a.m. — a condiment, familiar, harmless, something a child might pocket. The particularity of it haunts me. Two large packets. Not one. Not a bottle. Packets. The kind you tear open with your teeth.

The city is full of such particularities. At the RDS today, Art Source opens its doors. Ten Tracey Emin works, combined value €143,000 — the same number as the cash in Duff's pocket, a coincidence that means nothing and everything. *I Need Tomorrow*, the 2026 series, six blue and black lithographs of the artist as a reclining figure, body loosely rendered and dissolving. *Because It's You*. *I Kept Dreaming of You*. *I Need Tomorrow*. Editions of fifty. Three on display. Works starting at five thousand euro. Fifteen thousand art lovers expected over the weekend. Banksy and Warhol too, and a hundred original pieces for a hundred euro each on opening morning, first come first served.

Gerard Gormley of Gormleys curated the Emin pieces. *While best known for her installations and paintings, Emin's prints have become highly sought after for their immediacy, intimacy and unmistakable hand-drawn quality*. Her work draws on themes of love, longing, vulnerability and memory. The raw emotion and honesty. Accessibility, he calls it. Five thousand euro for accessibility.

I think about the burglar and the artist. Duff with his ketchup packets and his alcoholism since childhood, his brother's letter and his former employer's letter, his job offer waiting, his hostel on Dame Street. Emin with her dissolving bodies and her blue-black lithographs, her forty years of turning pain into something people queue to buy. Both of them, in their different ways, trying to survive the night. Both of them known by their marks — his 117 convictions, her Turner Prize nomination for *My Bed*, the unmade sheets and the vodka bottles and the cigarette butts, the life laid bare.

The fuel protesters were supposed to arrive by noon today. A convoy from Ennis, Banner Plaza on the M18 at Junction 12, 8:45 a.m. departure. *People of Ireland Against Fuel Prices*. Truck drivers, farmers, hauliers, recovery operators, small business owners, ordinary workers. They downgraded it to a Dublin-only protest. The Gardaí said zero tolerance for road blockages, rolling protests, any disruption to the EU summit. The European Political Community meeting. Heads of state. One of the largest security operations in the history of the state. The protest group said they weren't prepared to put people's driving licences, jobs, businesses, livelihoods at risk. *People are already struggling enough without potentially losing their ability to earn a living simply because they are trying to make their voices heard*.

The voices. Always the voices. The city holds them all — the judge's doubtful mercy, the minister's preparedness, the artist's longing, the burglar's hunger, the protesters' restraint, the weather's indifference. Two point eight degrees. Clear sky. The frost will clear to leave a mostly bright and cool day, just the odd shower. Highest temperatures seven to nine degrees. Light variable breezes.

Tonight it freezes again. Minus one to plus four. Cloud increasing from the west. Outbreaks of rain and drizzle along the west coast. Saturday cloudier, milder, eight to eleven. Sunday wet, rain in all areas. Monday mainly dry with sunny spells. Tuesday wet again. The cycle continues, indifferent to ketchup packets and lithographs and convoys that don't arrive.

I should make tea. The kettle clicks, the water boils, the steam rises and condenses on the cold window — a small weather system of my own making. The light strengthens, pale grey becoming something closer to white. A siren somewhere, distant. A bus braking. The city waking, not all at once but in patches, like the frost clearing.

The emergency booklet is somewhere in a drawer, unread. I know where the gas shutoff is. I don't know my MPRN. I have no wind-up radio. I have matches, somewhere. Candles too. The power banks are charged because I charged them yesterday, not because of the booklet. The water in the tap runs clear and cold and I trust it, though the booklet says store bottled water in a cool dark place.

Trust. That's the word. We trust the grid until it fails. We trust the judges to be doubtful but merciful. We trust the artists to make beauty from dissolution. We trust the weather to do what weather does — freeze, thaw, rain, clear. We trust the city to hold us, with its ghosts and its heating systems and its particular light, thin as weak tea, that says *here is another day, make what you can of it*.

The tea steeps. The clock ticks. The light spreads across the floorboards, touching the dust motes, the edge of a book, the cold handle of the mug. Two point eight degrees. Clear sky. The city wakes cold, and I am in it, and that is enough for now.
