The street throbs at noon. Cars hiss on the tarmac, engines cough in the heat, and the tram rattles past the General Post Office, its brass nameplate catching the weak sun. The crowd moves like a river of umbrellas turned inside out, each passerby a droplet caught in the flow. I note the way the shopkeepers display their wares—silks that whisper of the East, copper pots that gleam like the sun through a dirty window. An old woman haggles over a bunch of lilies, her voice rising over the din, a small aria of commerce. Somewhere a child laughs, sharp and bright, cutting through the built‑in noise of the city. The air smells of exhaust and freshly baked bread from the bakery on the corner, a paradox that makes the senses sharpen. I think of the old days when the street was quieter, when the only advertisements were painted signs and the occasional hoarding of newspaper headlines. Today everything is lit up, electric, and the glare is almost blinding. Yet there is a rhythm, a pulse that you can feel if you stand still for a moment at the center of the intersection, near the statue of a long‑dead nationalist, his stone eyes watching the world go by. I breathe in the mixture of exhaust and fresh rain that has just started to fall, a summer storm that promises to wash the streets clean. The water pools in the gutters, reflecting the neon signs of cafés, the blinking of smartphones, the endless scrolling of headlines that no one reads. In that moment, the city is both ancient and brand‑new, a manuscript being written in real time, each word a footstep on the cobblestones of history.
