The light through the kitchen window at seven a.m. is thin, watery, the yellow of old letters. August ending. The air smells of wet pavement and cut grass, the river's breath rising from the Liffey where it curves past the quays.

I walked the dog earlier. The streets were empty the way Dublin mornings empty — not deserted, suspended. A bin lorry rumbled past, hydraulics sighing. A fox crossed the road near the park entrance, unhurried. It knew something I didn't.

Yesterday afternoon a car went over the bridge at Ballymount. Junction 10. An elderly couple, the news said, in their eighties. The vehicle broke through the barrier and fell onto the M50 below. I think about that. Not the traffic snarl, the helicopters, the garda tape. The moment before: hands on a steering wheel, a conversation perhaps, or silence, the radio playing something from forty years ago. Then the world tilted. The bridge railing gave way like cardboard.

They say the driver lost control. A medical episode, maybe. Or a body that served faithfully for eight decades simply stopping, without warning.

The M50 carries thousands of commutes every hour. Arguments, podcasts, the silent negotiation of merge lanes. Yesterday it caught a falling car and two lives suddenly unmoored. Today the lanes run clear. The barrier will be repaired. Grass will grow over the scar on the embankment.

At one o'clock today, Verdi at Wood Quay. The last of the Opera in the Open series. Twenty-seven years of free lunchtime opera in the amphitheatre behind the Civic Offices. I've never gone. Always meant to. Next Thursday, I'd think. Next year. Now it's the final Thursday of August, a gala celebrating Giuseppe Verdi. *La forza del destino*. *Rigoletto*. *Aida*. The great chorus of Hebrew slaves rising from stone steps where civil servants eat sandwiches on Tuesdays.

The fiscal council warned yesterday that government spending will feed inflation. "Repeated overruns." The budget looms in October. Households worse off, the ESRI said last year. Two percent. Low-income households faring worse. The words float like pollen — fiscal drag, indexation, corporation tax windfall, rainy-day funds. Abstract nouns for the price of milk, the rent climbing, the decision not to turn the heating on until November.

A man on the radio this morning said the economy is strong. Strong for whom. The fox didn't care. The river didn't care. The elderly couple in hospital beds probably didn't either.

I make coffee. The kettle whistles. Steam rises, catches the light.

Verdi had a particular honesty. He wrote about fathers and daughters, curses and love that kills, the force of destiny catching you ready or not. He knew railings give way. He knew cars fall. He wrote music for the moment after, when the chorus sings anyway.

The dog nudges my hand. Time for the second walk. The day waits, ordinary and enormous, full of collisions and arias, budgets and foxes, light falling just so across wet pavement.

I'll go to Wood Quay today. Stand at the back of the amphitheatre. Let Verdi wash over me. The Hebrew slaves will sing of their homeland, and I'll think of a car falling from a bridge, a fox crossing the road, the price of milk, how the light looks at seven a.m. on the last Thursday of August — thin, watery, yellow as old letters, and completely sufficient.
