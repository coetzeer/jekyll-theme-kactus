The kettle sighs. Steam rises in a thin column, catching the pale rectangle of window-light — such as it is at seven in November. Six point nine degrees, the report said, back in October. Overcast. Fourteen kilometres an hour of wind finding its way through the sash cords. I remember that morning. The light failed differently then, a grey wash over the city that made everything look borrowed, temporary. Today the temperature reads much the same on the phone screen, but the body knows. The body keeps its own calendar.

I walked past IMMA on the twenty-third of October. The posters were already up: *Eltiqa: How to Work Together?* Sixty works by six artists from Gaza City. Mohamed Abusal. Abdel Raouf Al-Ajouri. Mohammed Al-Hawajri. Raed Issa. Dina Matar. Sohail Salem. The names settle in the mouth like stones. Twenty years of a collective practice, Eltiqa — *encounter* in Arabic — sustained through siege, through displacement, through the kind of erasure that turns a life into a footnote. Some works smuggled out. Some carried by artists who managed to leave. Some left behind in institutions because the route home no longer existed. The Palestinian Ambassador, Dr Jilan Wahba Abdalmajid, stood in the gallery on the twenty-second and said she hoped the exhibition would amplify voices, convey resilience. Hope. The word hangs in the cold air. I think of notebook sketches made with found materials. I think of a timeline on the wall stretching from 1948 to 2026, each year a wound, each year a stitch. The light in that room — curated, deliberate — falling on colour that refuses to be extinguished. Dina Matar's celebration. Raed Issa's sombre. Both true. Both necessary.

The wind shifts. A bin lid clatters somewhere down the lane.

Bill Murray was in Cobh that same week. Seventy-six years old, 98% Irish by the narrator's reckoning, handing out ice creams in Kinsale. *The first time I came to Ireland I felt like I was in a family wake — it looked like everyone was my cousin.* He said that. On camera. For a BBC series called *A Course Called Ireland*, golf as the vehicle, heritage as the destination. His great-grandmother born on the boat crossing the Atlantic. One hundred and fifty years. He stood at the water's edge in Cork harbour and said: *To live like this... it's so beautiful and so lovely and to have had such pain. You can see the water, the sky, the clouds and the sun and realise all that's given to you by god and that what you get from your fellow man can be so wicked.* The words arrive in my kitchen with the steam. Wicked. Incomprehensible. Seed. Survival. Pride. He uses the word *pride* without irony. An American actor on Irish soil, finding the ghost of a great-grandmother in the chop of the Celtic Sea. I think of my own great-grandparents. The boat. The green river in Chicago. The dyed water, the parade, the performance of belonging. We carry the leaving inside us. We carry the arriving. The two never quite reconcile.

In Ballyfermot, councillors argued over saturation. One hundred and fourteen additional units at Stonemount Park, Block F. Four hundred and eighteen homes already there, managed by Iveagh Trust. Two thousand two hundred across the city. Vincent Jackson asked *at what stage do we reach saturation point* — his phrase, his fear. *Mistakes of the past being replicated.* Nine hundred plus social units without robust estate management. Darragh Moriarty called it disappointing to hear a councillor call for less social housing when lists are bursting. Hazel De Nortúin spoke of tenants evicted within six months by approved housing bodies because they can't sustain the tenancy. Philip Sutcliffe: *There's no use putting people in an area if they don't have proper bus routes, hospital services, and schools.* The words circle. Saturation. Management. Mix. Sustain. Community. Flourish. I walk Ballyfermot Road sometimes. The brick. The windows. The lives stacked vertically, horizontally, each door a universe. Saturation is a word for chemistry, for sponges, for markets. Not for people. But the fear is real. The towers that came down. The complexes demolished because management failed. The wisdom of hindsight. The ceiling of what we should purchase. I think of the residents' committee meeting with housing officers. Social connections fostered. Steps put in place. The language of bureaucracy trying to hold the shape of human need.

The kettle clicks off. Silence rushes back in.

November light. It does not illuminate so much as reveal. The dust motes. The scratch on the tabletop. The ring left by yesterday's mug. The face in the window-reflection — older than I remember, younger than I feel. Six point nine degrees. Overcast. The wind fourteen kilometres an hour. The same numbers, different month. The body knows.

I think of the Gaza artists' notebook sketches. Found materials. Paper scarce. Pigment improvised. The line drawn anyway. The encounter — *eltiqa* — happening in the space between what is possible and what is necessary. I think of Bill Murray in Kinsale, ice cream melting on his fingers, the craic easy, the history heavy. I think of Ballyfermot, the saturation debate, the ceiling, the floor, the walls between.

The light fails differently in November. It fails gently. It fails completely. It leaves you with the outline of things, the essence stripped of distraction. The kettle. The steam. The names. The boat. The brick. The line drawn anyway.

Outside, a blackbird sings. Just one. Just now. The day begins.
