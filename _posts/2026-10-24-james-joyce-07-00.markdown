The light fails over St Andrew's Street at 7 a.m. on a Friday in October. Not fails exactly — arrives grudgingly, the way a reluctant guest arrives at a wake. Eight point four degrees. The wind eleven and a half kilometres an hour from somewhere west-southwest, pushing a ceiling of grey so low it brushes the tops of the plane trees. Overcast. The meteorologists call it overcast. I call it the sky pressing its palm flat against the city's face.

I walked the length of Grafton Street yesterday evening, the Christmas lights already strung in their dormant coils along the lampposts, waiting. The buskers were out — a fiddler near the Gaiety, a guitarist with a loop pedal outside Brown Thomas, a woman singing something in Irish near the St Stephen's Green gate. Her voice rose and fell in the damp air. *Mo ghrá, mo ghrá*. My love, my love. Or maybe it was *mo ghrádh*. The vowels blur in Dublin rain.

Forty years. The Cedar Tree on St Andrew's Street. Ireland's first Lebanese restaurant, they say. Since 1986. Before I knew this city properly, before the Celtic Tiger roared and whimpered, before the cranes became a permanent skyline feature. The cedar of Lebanon, *Cedrus libani*, grows on mountains. Here it grew in a basement dining room with arched doorways and brass lanterns, feeding generations of Dubliners lamb kibbeh and hummus that remembered the hand that mashed it. Bank Holiday Monday, October 26th. Two days from now. Special offers all day. *Come and dine with us this Bank Holiday Monday for our last horah!* The exclamation mark in their Instagram post feels like a small defiance. *It's been a pleasure bringing you a taste of Lebanon since 1986 and an honour to be Ireland's first and oldest Lebanese restaurant.*

Forty years. My father would have remembered 1986. He died in 2001, but 1986 he lived through. He would have been fifty-three. I think of him walking this same street, younger than I am now, the city different but the light the same — this particular grey that makes the red brick glow like embers. The Cedar Tree's doors closing. Another door. Another light going out. The city eats its own history and calls it progress. A boutique hotel will probably take the space. Or a co-working hub with exposed brick and oat milk lattes. *Exposed brick* — the phrase makes me tired. The brick was never meant to be exposed. It was meant to hold the building up.

Donal Lunny died yesterday. Seventy-nine. Cancer. St James's Hospital. The bouzouki player. The architect of sound. Planxty. The Bothy Band. Moving Hearts. Usher's Island. He designed his own bouzouki, did you know? Took a Greek instrument and made it Irish, the way the Irish make everything Irish eventually — the language, the faith, the sorrow, the song. Christy Moore knew him sixty-six years. *I met him first in 1959 in school, and what I remember most from those years were his lack of homework, that he was more interested in being out on the Liffey trying to tickle trout, or climbing trees to gaze into the wild birds' nests.*

Tickle trout. The phrase catches me. Tickling trout — *guddling*, they call it in Scotland. Running your fingers under the rock ledge where the trout rests, stroking its belly until it goes limp with trust, then lifting it out. A strange intimacy. A boy on the Liffey in 1959, trousers rolled up, water cold around his calves, learning the patience that later became music. The bodhran. The guitar. The bass. The mandolin. The keyboard. But the bouzouki — that was the one. Eight strings in four courses. A sound like light on water. Like the Liffey at dawn when the sun actually breaks through.

The National Concert Hall called him *one of the most influential and innovative figures in Irish traditional music*. *Pioneering work*. *Redefine*. *Enduring contribution*. The language of institutions. But Christy Moore's memory — the homework not done, the trees climbed, the birds' nests peered into — that is the language of life. The boy who became the man who built the instrument that carried the tradition forward. He played the NCH in 2023. *Drawing from the Well: Tommy Peoples*. The well. The deep well. *Uisce beatha*. Water of life.

And the Christmas busk goes on. Glen Hansard gone now — earlier this year, the articles say *tragic passing* — but Mundy confirmed it. The Christmas Eve busk on Grafton Street. Fifteen years. Over two million euro for the Dublin Simon Community. U2. Dermot Kennedy. Imelda May. Liam Ó Maonlaí. *I'll do some of the lifting but not all of it*, Mundy said. *Glen was amazing, he was driven and could really rally the troops, and get artists to leave their families and come out on Christmas Eve.*

Leave their families. Christmas Eve. The darkest evening of the year. Standing on Grafton Street with a guitar case open, singing *Fairytale of New York* or *The Auld Triangle* or something newer, something raw, while the shoppers rush past with bags from Brown Thomas and Arnotts, the red buses hissing, the Luas clanging its warning bell. And Glen Hansard there, stage manager, MC, the man who called up a seventeen-year-old Dermot Kennedy and watched him *blow us all away*. *Glen could see Dermot's talent coming up, he could see talent no matter how young the singer.*

Donal Lunny helped too, the article says. *There have been many other people who helped Glen over the years like Danny (O'Reilly) from The Coronas and Donal Lunny.* The circle tightens. The bouzouki player and the busk organiser. The tradition and the spontaneous generosity. Two million euro in a woolly hat passed along the pavement. Noreen O'Donnell, backing singer with The Frames, walking the line in 2010. *We made three or four grand for the homeless, which was more money than the gig made that night.*

The gig made less than the busk. That tells you something about this city. About where its heart actually beats. Not in the venues with the ticket prices and the rider requirements. On the pavement. In the cold. With a woolly hat.

A storm is coming. Boelo. Named by the Spanish meteorological service, adopted by Met Éireann. Status Orange for Donegal, Galway, Leitrim, Mayo, Sligo — wind, two p.m. Saturday to nine a.m. Sunday. Status Orange for Kerry — rain, seven a.m. to two p.m. Saturday. Yellow for the rest. Clare, Cork, Limerick. Northern Ireland too, the UK Met Office weighing in. *Very difficult travelling conditions. Power outages. Large coastal waves. Fallen trees. Flooding.*

The forecast reads like poetry written by a pessimist. *Rather wet and windy tomorrow morning with widespread rain and fresh to strong and gusty southerly winds, reaching near gale force or gale force near coasts. The rain will be heavy at times, especially in the west and southwest with spot flooding possible. A clearance to blustery showers will follow through the afternoon, with isolated thunderstorms possible in the northwest, as winds veer southwest to west and increase strong to near gale force and gusty, reaching gale force or strong gale force near west and northwest coasts as Storm Boelo tracks to the north of the country.*

Tracks to the north. The language of shipping forecasts. *Veer. Back. Increase. Decrease. Good. Moderate. Poor. Fog. Rain. Showers. Thunder.* I used to listen to the shipping forecast on RTÉ Radio 1 late at night, the names rolling out — Viking, North Utsire, South Utsire, Forties, Cromarty, Forth, Tyne, Dogger, Fisher, German Bight, Humber, Thames, Dover, Wight, Portland, Plymouth, Biscay, Trafalgar, FitzRoy, Sole, Lundy, Fastnet, Irish Sea, Shannon, Rockall, Malin, Hebrides, Bailey, Fair Isle. A litany. A rosary. The sea does not care about your counties. The sea does not care about your warnings.

But we care. We watch the trees. We check the gutters. We tape the windows in exposed corners. We think of the cedar of Lebanon on the mountain, roots gripping limestone, surviving centuries of wind. The Cedar Tree on St Andrew's Street had no roots in limestone. It had roots in community. *It has been wonderful to be part of one big family with you all.* Family. The word does heavy lifting in Ireland. It covers blood and choice and neighbourhood and the customers who came every Friday for thirty years and the staff who stayed and the children who grew up on kibbeh and now bring their own children.

The robins. The article about robins in October gardens. *Robins will keep coming back if you add 1 food to your garden in October.* Mealworms. Sunflower hearts. Suet. *Birds need high-energy, high-fat foods during cold weather.* The RSPB. SongBird Survival. The British Trust for Ornithology. *Wild food sources like berries, nuts, and fallen fruits are at their peak in autumn, meaning birds don't have to rely as heavily on supplementary feeding.*

But they come anyway. The robin — *Erithacus rubecula*. The gardener's companion. The Christmas card bird. Territory holders. They sing in winter. Both sexes sing. *Tsee-tsee-tsee-tsee*, thin and high, falling like the temperature. They follow the spade. They know the turned earth yields. I have watched them on the allotment behind the house in Phibsborough. Hopping onto the fork handle. Waiting. The eye bright as a bead of jet. No fear. Or a different calculus of fear — the worm matters more than the giant.

*Mealworms are a key for robins, they absolutely love them.* Sue Morgan, CEO of SongBird Survival. *But I think if you went with sunflower hearts and mealworms... robins would really like those.* The ellipsis in the quote. The hesitation. The human voice breaking through the press release language. *Really like those.* Not *prefer*. Not *select for*. *Really like*. The bird has preferences. The bird has a small life that matters to it.

The moult. *After the breeding season concludes, adult birds are depleted. Their plumage, crucial for flight and insulation, becomes thoroughly worn and deteriorated.* *Moulting is a physically demanding time for birds. They need to conserve energy, so they become much quieter and spend more time hidden amongst vegetation, making them less conspicuous.*

Quiet. Hidden. Conspicuous. The words apply to more than birds. The city in October. The light failing at 7 a.m. The Cedar Tree closing. Donal Lunny gone. Glen Hansard gone. The storm rolling in from the Atlantic. We moult too. We shed the year's feathers. We grow new ones in the dark. We are depleted. We conserve energy. We become quieter. Less conspicuous. But the robin sings in winter. Both sexes. Territory holders. *Tsee-tsee-tsee-tsee*. A claim. *I am here. This is mine. I survive.*

The wind picks up. Eleven and a half kilometres an hour. The plane trees shiver. A leaf detaches, spirals, lands on the wet pavement. Eight point four degrees. My breath visible. The Luas clangs somewhere near the College Green stop. A bus hisses past, the number 46A to Phoenix Park. The driver's face illuminated by the dashboard. A woman in a red coat hurries past, umbrella raised against rain that hasn't started yet. Anticipation. The Irish skill. Expecting the worst. Being ready.

Forty years of lamb kibbeh. Sixty-six years of friendship. Fifteen years of Christmas Eve busking. Two million euro in a woolly hat. Seventy-nine years of a life that changed the sound of a nation. One food — mealworms — to keep the robin returning. One storm named Boelo. One morning, overcast, eight degrees, the light failing over St Andrew's Street.

I turn the corner onto Dawson Street. The Duke pub. The Mansion House. The roundabout where the horses clop for the tourists. The Shelbourne's doormen in their topcoats. The green copper dome of the National Gallery. The city wakes slowly. The bakers. The cleaners. The first coffee cups steaming in hands that know the weight of the day coming.

The Cedar Tree's last horah. Monday. Bank Holiday. I should go. I should eat one last plate of kibbeh, dip bread in hummus that remembers the hand that mashed it. I should say thank you to the family who fed this city a taste of Lebanon for forty years. *Love, The Cedar Tree family.* The sign-off on the Instagram post. Love. Not sincerely. Not regards. Love.

The robin lands on the railing outside my window. *Tsee-tsee-tsee-tsee*. I have no mealworms. I have sunflower hearts in a paper bag in the kitchen. I will go down. I will scatter them on the bird table. The robin will come. It will tilt its head. It will take what is offered. It will sing. The storm is coming. The light fails. The city endures.

*Tsee-tsee-tsee-tsee.*

I am here. This is mine. I survive.
