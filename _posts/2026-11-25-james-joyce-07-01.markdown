The light falls different on Phibsborough at seven in the morning. Not better. Not worse. Different — the way a bruise changes colour overnight, the way the same song sounds different when you hear it in a different room. Grey-white, thin as cigarette paper, it slides between the curtains and lands on the duvet cover, on the stack of unopened mail, on the rim of yesterday's tea cup where the dregs have gone cold and dark.

I lie there listening to the radiator tick. The pipes remember heat the way the body remembers touch — belatedly, grudgingly, in fits and starts. Eleven and a half degrees yesterday, the weather report said. Light drizzle. Sixteen point nine kilometres per hour wind. Numbers that mean nothing until you step outside and feel the air bite the wrists where your sleeves don't quite meet your gloves. Dublin in November. The city breathes damp.

---

The mail on the floor: a bill, a flyer for a pizza place that closed two years ago, a letter from the RTB. Residential Tenancies Board. The acronym lands heavier than the envelope. Nine point one percent. That was the number in the paper — rental inflation for new tenancies, first quarter. Nine point one. It sits in the chest like a stone. Two thousand three hundred thirty-five euro. The standardised average rent in Dublin, Q1 2026. In 2016 it was thirteen thirty-seven. I did the math once. Forty-three percent of my income. Forty-three. The number doesn't move. It doesn't negotiate. It just is.

Councillor Feljin Jose said it costs him a euro a day extra to get into town from Phibsborough. Twice a day. Three hundred euro a year. He didn't get an excise cut. He didn't get a VAT cut on his restaurant. I think of the Luas rattling past the window at 6:47, 7:02, 7:17 — each carriage a metal lung breathing commuters in and out. The 90-minute fare went from two euro to two-thirty. Fifteen percent average increase. Up to thirty percent on some routes. The Green Party called it irresponsible. People Before Profit called it outrageous. Sinn Féin called it regressive. The Minister said supply is the fundamental challenge. Supply. Always supply. The word that solves everything and nothing.

I read once that Dublin is the second most expensive city in Europe for remote workers. Twenty-eight hundred four euro a month before coworking. Three thousand one twenty-four with a desk somewhere. London is the only one higher. The bunq index. Joe Wilson said remote working changed the relationship between where people live and where they work. Professionals increasingly have the freedom to choose. That word — professionals. As if the rest of us are amateurs at living. As if the nurse on the night shift at the Mater, the delivery cyclist braking in the rain on Capel Street, the woman cleaning offices at 5 a.m. in the IFSC — as if they're not professionals too. They just don't get to choose. They get chosen by the rent.

---

The kettle clicks. Steam rises, catches the thin light, makes it visible for a second — the particles of air, the dust motes, the invisible made visible. Tea. The ritual. The spoon against the mug. The first sip, scalding, honest.

Outside, the city wakes in increments. The bin lorry on the North Circular, the clatter of glass. A fox — or maybe a cat — scuttles across the neighbour's roof tiles. The church bells at St. Peter's, seven o'clock, seven strokes, each one falling into the damp air and dying there. No echo. The air is too thick for echoes.

Yesterday's weather: eleven point five. Light drizzle. Today will be similar. The Met Éireann pinpoints when wet and blustery conditions will hit amid changeable — that's the phrase they use. Changeable. As if the weather has a personality. As if it decides. The rain doesn't decide. It falls. The wind doesn't choose. It pushes. We're the ones who decide — whether to carry the umbrella, whether to take the bus or walk, whether to stay or go.

---

Katie Taylor fights Flora Pili at Croke Park on Saturday. Eighty thousand plus. Westlife performing — Shane, Nicky, Kian. The band said they were honoured. She personally invited them. The Bray fighter. Three-time undisputed. Final professional fight. The words arrange themselves in the headlines like a liturgy. Farewell. Poignant. Momentous. Record-breaking thirteen-night residency at the 3Arena starting September 10.

I won't be there. Eighty thousand people and I won't be one of them. But I'll hear it. The roar that carries on the wind, depending on the direction. The fireworks after, if there are fireworks. The cars gridlocked on the North Circular, on Drumcondra Road, on the quays. The city holding its breath for three minutes, then twelve, then fifteen — however long it lasts. A collective inhale. A collective exhale.

There's something ancient about it. The crowd. The ring. The two bodies and the space between them where violence becomes art, or art becomes violence, or both at once. Croke Park has held hurling finals, football finals, concerts, the Pope, the Queen. Now it holds a woman from Bray who learned to fight in a shed and carried a nation's weight on shoulders that look, in photographs, too slender for the burden. Flora Pili. Unbeaten. French. The opponent. The necessary other. Without her, there's no fight. Just a woman in a ring, shadowboxing history.

I think of the Vikings spin-off filming near Dublin. Major evacuation at Berlin Brandenburg airport. Paddy Power closing a hundred betting shops, four hundred jobs. The burglar case — Crossguns Snooker Club. The Mi Casa concert. The office rents. The retail park sale. The city accumulates. Layer upon layer. The palimpsest. We write over what came before but the ink shows through. The snooker club where my father took me once, the smell of chalk and stale smoke and pints. The retail park where I bought my first microwave. The office building where I had an interview in 2019 and didn't get the job. All of it still there, ghosted into the present.

---

The tea is gone. The mug sits in the sink, a brown ring at the water line. I should wash it. I should shower. I should dress. The day waits, patient as a cat.

The letter from the RTB — I haven't opened it. Fixed payment notice powers. Two hundred euro fines for landlords over non-compliance with rent-setting rules from September 14. New powers. New rules. The machinery of regulation grinding forward, slower than the market, slower than the need. Four thousand thirty-one eviction notices between April and June. Four thousand. Not a metaphor. Four thousand doors closing. Four thousand sets of keys turning in locks for the last time. Four thousand people wondering where the boxes go, where the furniture fits, where the next rent comes from.

Rory Hearne called it shocking. Worse than expected. Thrown to the wolves. Eoin Ó Broin called for an immediate ban on rent increases and evictions. The Taoiseach said supply is the fundamental challenge. Making progress. More units constructed. Change had to occur. The Housing Commission recommended it. Uncertainty. Investment. Future. The words that justify the present by mortgaging it to a future that never quite arrives.

I think of the woman missing after Nepal floods. The Irish teenager killed in a car crash in Iceland. The couple who died in a hot tub in Spain. The murder of Raonaid Murray — fresh appeal, you may be unknowingly shielding a killer. The gardaí hunting thugs who rammed a patrol car in Kildare. The two young women arrested in a major drug bust. The two men arrested in connection with the South Dublin brawl that led to a death. The pilot who reported a large unidentified black drone off the Irish coast. The city holds all of it. The joy and the grief and the mundane and the inexplicable. It doesn't choose. It holds.

---

The light has shifted. Thicker now. The grey-white has warmed to something closer to pearl. The drizzle, if it comes, will catch it — each drop a brief lens, a momentary magnification of the world inverted. I used to hate this weather. The damp in the bones. The moss on the window frames. The smell of wet wool and diesel and frying onions from the chipper on the corner. Now I find myself noticing — the way the streetlights bloom in the wet, halos of sodium and LED. The way the Liffey turns slate, then silver, then black depending on the cloud cover. The way the seagulls — inland now, city birds — cry like they're still at sea, like they haven't forgotten.

Maybe that's what it is. Not forgetting. The city doesn't forget. The rent increases, the fare hikes, the fight at Croke Park, the Westlife residency, the Viking ships on the screen and the longships under the streets — it all accretes. We are the sediment. We are the layer currently being laid down. Our worries, our bills, our small rituals — the tea, the radiator tick, the seven strokes of St. Peter's — they will be the ghost layer for whoever comes next. The ones who'll pay three thousand for a desk in a coworking space. The ones who'll watch a fight in a stadium that hasn't been built yet. The ones who'll read about us in the KB, if the KB still exists, if anyone still reads.

---

I shower. The water pressure is good today — a small mercy. The steam fills the room, writes on the mirror. I write nothing. Just breathe.

Dress. Coat. The green one, worn at the cuffs. Scarf. The grey one, a gift from someone whose name has softened at the edges. Keys. Phone. Wallet. The letter from the RTB stays on the counter. I'll open it tonight. Or tomorrow. Or the day after. The machinery grinds. The city breathes. The light falls.

Outside, Phibsborough is waking properly now. The bakery on the corner — the smell of soda bread, real soda bread, not the supermarket stuff. The newsagent folding papers. The early Luas, two-thirds full. A woman with a pram, rain cover deployed. A man in a suit checking his watch. A teenager with headphones, shoulders hunched against a cold that hasn't fully arrived yet. The seagull on the lamppost, one eye on the chipper window.

I walk. The pavement is wet from overnight rain I didn't hear. My boots know the cracks, the lifted slab outside the pharmacy, the dip where the tree roots push. The body remembers. The city remembers. We remember each other.

At the Luas stop, I wait. The electronic board: 3 minutes. 8 minutes. 14 minutes. The countdown. The anticipation. The small contract between the system and the passenger — we trust you'll come, you trust we'll board. Sometimes it breaks. Today it holds.

The tram arrives. The doors hiss open. I step on. The city moves through me, and I move through the city, and the light falls different on Phibsborough at seven in the morning, and tomorrow it will fall different again, and the day after that, and the day after that — until the day it doesn't, and someone else stands at this window, with this tea, watching the grey-white slide between the curtains, thinking: so this is how it looks. So this is how it feels. So this is Dublin.

*The radiator ticks. The tea steams. The city waits. We wait with it.*
