The rain does not fall so much as arrive. It has been here all along, suspended in the grey qualification of a September morning, waiting for the hour when the city exhales. Seventeen point two degrees. The thermometer reads it like a verdict. One point four kilometres per hour of wind — barely a breath, barely enough to move the droplets from their vertical insistence into something approaching a slant.

I have been awake since before the clocks turned six. The flat is quiet except for the kettle's growing restlessness and the occasional car on the quays, tyres hissing on wet asphalt. That sound — *hiss* — is the city's true morning prayer. Not bells. Not birds. The tyre on wet tarmac, each rotation a small confession.

Yesterday eighty thousand people packed into Croke Park to watch a woman from Bray fight her final fight. Katie Taylor. The name has been in the air for weeks, thickening like broth. Eighty thousand. The same number that will gather at Slane Castle next summer, two nights, one hundred sixty thousand total, when Fontaines DC bring their noise to the Meath countryside. One euro from each ticket to Focus Ireland. Homelessness gets a euro. The rest goes to the machine that turns music into money, money into venue, venue into memory.

I did not go to Croke Park. I watched the light change on the walls of this room instead. The fight happened in rounds, in combinations, in the geometry of two bodies negotiating the same space with different intentions. She won. Three-time undisputed champion across two weight classes. Only the second fighter to headline that ground since Muhammad Ali in nineteen seventy-two. The number settles in the chest: two. In fifty-four years, two.

The rain knows none of this. The rain falls on the champion and the challenger, on the eighty thousand and the one hundred sixty thousand yet to come, on the boy in Mayo who will not see his thirteenth birthday.

Eoin Doyle. Twelve years old. Twin sister Saoirse. Gurrane, Ballyhaunis. The level crossing at Kiltybo. The six-fifteen from Heuston to Westport. Eight forty-five PM on a Thursday. A man in his late forties in hospital with injuries that are not life-threatening — that phrase, *not life-threatening*, doing so much work, carrying so much weight. The train passengers safe. The line closed overnight. Reopened by four PM Friday. Efficiency restored. The investigation continues. The Coroner notified. The Gardaí appealing for dash-cam footage between eight-fifteen and nine-fifteen.

A twelve-year-old boy who recently saw the Sam Maguire Cup when Jack Coyne brought it to Ballyhaunis GAA club. Joy and grief passing each other in the same narrow lane, neither acknowledging the other.

I think about the fare hikes. Thirty percent. The number floats in the steam above my tea. Councillor Conor Reddy, Ballymun-Finglas, People Before Profit, petition circulating: reverse the increases, make it free. Luxembourg did it. Nationwide, free, residents and commuters and tourists alike. The word *free* catches in the throat. Nothing is free. Someone pays. The question is who, and when, and whether they get a vote.

The Taoiseach says the government is not in a position to fund everything forever. *Sustainable funding model.* Pressure points. The NTA chief executive says it's the first general increase in eight years. Important step. Careful balance. Almost one and a half million people still get free travel or concessions. Children under nine. People sixty-six and over. The fifty percent young adult and student discount maintained.

Words. Numbers. Percentages. The rain does not negotiate.

Seventeen point two degrees. Slight rain. The pavement darkens. The gutters fill. Somewhere a bus arrives, brakes sighing, doors folding open. Someone taps a Leap card. The beep. The deduction. The transaction complete.

I remember being twenty-two, standing at a bus stop on O'Connell Street, the rain exactly like this — not heavy, not light, just *there* — and a woman next to me saying *Grand stretch in the evenings* though it was November and the evenings were nothing but stretch. Dark by four. She laughed at her own joke. I did not know her name. I still do not know her name. The bus came. We got on. She sat at the back. I sat near the front. We did not speak again. The city is full of conversations that never happened, words that hovered at the edge of a throat and fell back down.

The kettle clicks off. I pour. The tea steams, a small cloud rising into the larger cloud pressing against the window.

Fontaines DC at Slane. Grian Chatten's voice carrying across eighty thousand bodies, two nights, June twenty-sixth and twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-seven. Nearly a year away. The tickets on sale now. I wonder how many of the eighty thousand at Croke Park last night will be in that field next summer. How many of the one hundred sixty thousand will remember the rain on this morning, this specific Sunday, the seventeenth degree air, the one point four kilometre wind?

The particular contains the universal. This is what I keep returning to. Not as theory. As practice. The rain on this window. The tea in this cup. The boy in Mayo. The woman in the ring. The band in the castle. The councillor with his petition. The Taoiseach with his sustainable model. The NTA chief with her careful balance. All of it happening at once, all of it happening now, all of it wet with the same rain.

The light is changing. Not brighter exactly — greyer, deeper, the colour of a bruise healing. The streetlamps still on, their glow diffused into haloes. Soon they will click off, one by one, or in groups, or all at once depending on whatever timer or sensor or human hand governs them.

I should walk. The day will not wait for me to be ready. The rain will not pause while I finish this thought. The city turns on its axis, indifferent and intimate, carrying its eighty thousands and its twelves and its one hundred sixty thousands, its fare hikes and its free euros, its champions and its corpses, all of it moving toward whatever the next hour brings.

The tea is drinkable now. I will drink it. I will put on a coat. I will open the door. The rain will meet me. It has been waiting.
