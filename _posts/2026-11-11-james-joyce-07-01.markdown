The kettle clicks off. Ten degrees Celsius, the weather app says. Overcast. Wind fifteen point eight kilometres per hour from the southwest, which means it comes up the Liffey carrying the scent of salt and diesel and whatever the city exhaled overnight. I stand at the window in my socks, watching the light fail to arrive properly. November in Dublin doesn't do dawn so much as a gradual bruising of the sky, grey on grey, the kind of light that makes the Georgian brick look tired rather than warm.

Clanbrassil Close. The name sits in me from yesterday's reading. A close — not a street, not a road, a close. The word holds its old meaning: enclosed, shut in. Twenty-two houses, the councillor said. A tight-knit community where everyone knows everyone. A grandmother, her children, grandchildren, a baby only a couple of months old. Seven people pulled from a house that had been petrol-bombed at two-thirty in the morning. The roof caved in. The front covered in ash. A drug debt, they say, owed by a man who didn't live there. The wrong address. The wrong family. A miracle no one died, the councillor called it. Miracle. The word does heavy lifting in this city.

I think about the neighbours climbing the back wall. Not without a second thought, the councillor said, but I wonder. The body moves before the mind catches up. You see flames at a neighbour's window and your legs have already decided. Six minutes for the fire brigade. Six minutes. I count them in my head. One Mississippi. Two Mississippi. The time it takes to steep tea. The time it takes for a house to become a skeleton.

The wind rattles something in the gutter. A leaf, a crisp packet, the city's debris.

---

Yesterday — or the day the news calls yesterday, November the fourth — a car got itself embedded on the Luas tracks at Blackhorse. Embedded. The word the Luas people used. Not stuck. Not stranded. Embedded. As though the car had grown there, put down roots in the rail, become part of the infrastructure. A crane had to come and lift it out. The Red Line suspended. Tickets valid on Dublin Bus. The crane against the evening sky, its neck extended, the car dangling like something caught in a throat. Blackhorse stop. Davitt Road and the Naas Road crossing. Bluebell. I've walked past there. The Luas humming past, the tracks warm in summer, the overhead wires singing in wind. Now a Toyota or a Ford or a Hyundai, I don't know which, suspended in the grip of a crane, the driver probably standing on the pavement with their phone out, the particular shame of having driven where you cannot drive.

The city tolerates its absurdities. A car on rails. A house burned for a debt not owed. Private security wardens proposed for the city centre — safety operatives, the tender calls them, not guards, not police, wardens. Dublin Town, the business improvement district, will contract them. Over a thousand extra gardaí promised alongside. The Green Party councillor blindsided. The Independent councillor saying it's nothing new, safety wardens have existed for years on Henry Street, Jervis Street. The People Before Profit councillor calling it mission creep, social cleansing by another name. The former Lord Mayor on the board of Dublin Town saying it's a continuation of the community warden model. Everyone talking past each other in a room where the air is thick with the unsaid: who owns the street? Who decides what safety looks like? Who gets watched?

I think of the Beta Festival starting this week. November sixth to twenty-second. The Digital Hub campus. Art and technology. A strand of programming for dogs. The Other Audience, they call it. An installation by Gabriela Waclawska at Project Arts Centre. Humans welcome but not the main audience. Dogs moving through a space, interacting with music and elements. Aisling Murray, the founder, inspired by her dog, by the way her social circle expanded when she got one. People who wouldn't go to an exhibition might bring their dog. I like this. I like the idea of art that decenters the human. That asks what it means to make something for a nose instead of an eye. For a creature that reads the world in scent, in vibration, in the pitch of a sound we cannot hear. AlanJames Burns's Unmasking Nature — neurodivergent artist, interactive installation, the pressure to mask, to suppress, to perform normal. Nature depends on diversity, not masking conformity. The words stay with me. Masking conformity. The city asks it of us daily. The councillors ask it of each other. The security wardens will ask it of whoever walks the streets they patrol.

---

A Ryanair flight from Dublin, FR9611, escorted by Italian Air Force jets into Rome Fiumicino. Bomb threat. One of six across Europe that day. Manchester to Murcia. London to Warsaw. Copenhagen to Warsaw. Israel to Sofia. Nothing found on any of them. One hundred fourteen passengers and seven crew on the Manchester flight. One hundred eighty-one on the London flight. The numbers accumulate. The fear accumulates. The email threats, the anonymous tips. The security theatre of evacuation and inspection and sniffer dogs and bomb disposal experts. The airports resuming normal operations. The threats remaining unattributed. Hoaxes, probably. Coordinated, possibly. The world holding its breath over nothing, or over something that hasn't happened yet. I think of the family in Clanbrassil Close. The petrol bomb was real. The fire was real. The baby's lungs filling with smoke — that was real. But no Italian Air Force for them. No international coordination. Just six minutes of Dublin Fire Brigade and neighbours climbing a wall.

The light in the kitchen has shifted. Still grey, but a different grey. The kind that holds colour if you look sideways at it. The brick across the way shows its red again, muted, like blood under skin. A pigeon lands on the windowsill, looks at me with one bead eye, decides I am not a threat or a crumb, flies off.

---

The weather app says ten point one degrees. Feels like eight. The wind chill. I know this temperature in my bones. The particular cold of a Dublin November morning that sits in the joints, that makes the fingers stiff on the cup. The overcast sky is a lid on the city. No shadows. No contrast. Everything flattened into mid-tones. The plane trees on the quays stand bare, their branches a fine tracery against the white-grey. The Liffey runs the colour of weak tea, reflecting nothing.

I think about the private security wardens again. The Red C research commissioned by Dublin Town last May. Perceptions of safety improving but safety remains a key challenge, particularly on the Northside. Widespread reference to perceptions of safety. Perceptions. Not crime statistics. Not assault rates. Perceptions. The wardens will improve perceptions. They will be visible. They will wear high-vis. They will radio the gardaí. They will not arrest, not search, not fine. They will be eyes and ears. Whose eyes? Whose ears? The businesses who pay for them? The councillors who weren't briefed? The people who sleep in doorways on O'Connell Street, who will now have two sets of eyes on them instead of one?

The dog installation at Beta Festival. The dogs don't care about perceptions. They care about the smell of the grass, the sound of the other dog three streets away, the vibration of the Luas passing underground. They live in the real. We live in the perception. We build wardens for perceptions. We burn houses for debts. We embed cars in tracks. We evacuate planes for threats that aren't there.

---

The kettle has gone cold. I should reboil it. But I stay at the window.

A woman passes on the pavement below. Red coat. Umbrella folded. Walking fast, the way you walk in this city when the wind finds the gaps in your clothes. She disappears around the corner. A cyclist wobbles past, head down, fighting the fifteen point eight kilometres per hour. A bus groans up the hill, its engine a low cello note in the morning quiet.

Clanbrassil Close. The name won't leave. Close. Enclosed. The family of seven. The grandmother. The baby. The woman who jumped from the back window and broke something in the landing. The neighbours who climbed the wall. The councillor who called it a miracle. The gardaí reviewing CCTV. The roof caved in. The ash on the front.

I think of Joyce — the other Joyce, the one who left — writing in Trieste or Zurich or Paris, the city he carried inside him like a stone in his shoe. *Dubliners*. *Ulysses*. The paralysis. The epiphanies. The ordinary made sacred by attention. He would have written Clanbrassil Close. He would have found the rhythm of it, the way the word *close* closes the mouth, the way *Clanbrassil* opens it again, brassil, brass, the glint of something metallic in the sun. He would have seen the neighbours on the wall, the fire brigade's six minutes, the ash, the baby. He would have made it eternal.

I am not him. I am a man in a kitchen in 2026, watching the light fail to arrive, reading about petrol bombs and Luas-embedded cars and security wardens and dog art installations and bomb threats over Europe, and the weather is ten degrees and overcast and the wind is from the southwest and the city breathes salt and diesel and ash.

---

The baby. A couple of months old. Smoke inhalation. Hospitalised. Non-life-threatening injuries, the garda statement said. The clinical language. The baby doesn't know about statements. The baby knows heat and fear and the taste of smoke and the arms that held it, running. The grandmother knows. The children know. The neighbours who climbed the wall know. The councillor knows. The gardaí know. The people who threw the petrol bomb know, wherever they are this morning. Maybe they read the news. Maybe they don't. Maybe they're drinking tea in a kitchen like this one, watching the light fail to arrive, thinking about nothing at all.

The perception of safety. The reality of fire. The art for dogs. The crane lifting the car. The jets escorting the plane. The wardens in high-vis. The councillors arguing. The festival starting Thursday. The winter coming. The light, still grey, still holding its secrets.

I will reboil the kettle now. I will make tea. I will drink it at this window and watch the city wake up properly — the buses, the cyclists, the women in red coats, the pigeons, the Luas humming past Blackhorse where a car was embedded yesterday, the fire brigade maybe called to another close, another address, another debt. The city does not stop. The light does not arrive. The kettle clicks.

Ten degrees. Overcast. Wind from the southwest. The smell of salt and diesel and ash. This is the morning. This is the day. I am here, watching, writing it down, making it mine for a moment before it belongs to everyone and no one.

The baby. The wall. The ash. The crane. The dog. The warden. The jet. The threat. The tea.

I drink.
