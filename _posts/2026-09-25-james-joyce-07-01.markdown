The light falls differently on a Friday. Not that the sun knows the calendar — it's the same nuclear furnace it was on Tuesday — but the city arranges itself differently. People move with a particular urgency, or perhaps it's just that I notice the urgency more. The overcast sky sits at 14.1 degrees, a grey lid on the pot, and the wind moves at 12.6 kilometres per hour from somewhere over the Irish Sea. Not cold. Not warm. The temperature of waiting.

I woke to the sound of a bin lorry on Rathmines Road, the hydraulic hiss and clatter that marks the hours in this part of the world. The neighbours' cat — a tabby with a torn ear — sat on the garden wall watching me watch it. Neither of us moved. The air smelled of damp brick and diesel and something sweeter underneath, the last of the summer herbs giving up their oils to the rain that hasn't fallen yet but will. Met Éireann says above-normal rainfall for the week ahead. Low pressure systems from the Atlantic. They use words like "dominated" and "spells" and "accumulants" as if weather were a military campaign. It's just water falling from the sky, same as it ever was, same as it will be when the last of us stops counting the millimetres.

The boiler scrappage scheme announced yesterday — fourteen thousand five hundred euro to rip out the oil burner and put in a heat pump. Minister Darragh O'Brien standing in New York telling the cameras about kerosene dependence in rural Ireland. Two thousand new grant on top of twelve thousand five hundred existing. Homes built before 2021. End-of-life boilers. The language of bureaucracy trying to contain the language of living. I think of the house I grew up in, the Aga in the kitchen that never went out, the smell of turf and oil and my mother's soda bread. That heat was a presence in the house, a living thing you negotiated with. A heat pump hums. It doesn't negotiate. It delivers efficiency. Fourteen thousand five hundred euro is a lot of money and it's no money at all depending on who's counting. The grant comes in January. By then the winter will have done its work on the old systems. The pipes will have spoken.

A Ryanair flight. October last year. Storm Amy. One hundred and seventy-one passengers. Three aborted landings. Pisa to Glasgow Prestwick, then Edinburgh, then Manchester with three hundred and sixty-three kilograms of fuel remaining — final reserve is eleven hundred. The pilots said MAYDAY four times. MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY FUEL. The report says they were "increasingly concerned." The report says "breakdown of communication" at Manchester Airport. The report says the handling agent turned them away. The report uses the passive voice like a shield. Someone didn't pick up a radio. Someone didn't relay a message. One hundred and seventy-one people in a metal tube hurtling through wind shear, the fuel gauges dropping, the coastlines of Scotland and England blurring in the dark. I think about the man in seat 14A. The woman in 22F with the baby. The teenager in 31B listening to music through noise-cancelling headphones, unaware. The pilots' voices on the radio, calm because that's the job, calm because panic kills faster than fuel starvation. Three hundred and sixty-three kilograms. That's not a margin. That's a prayer.

Wednesday lunchtime. Nutgrove Retail Park. A man in his seventies drives his car through the front window of Costa Coffee. One fifteen pm. Non-life-threatening injuries. No one else hurt. The Garda statement is precise: "single-vehicle road traffic incident." The photograph shows the car inside the shop, the glass shattered, the coffee machines pushed aside like toys. I know that Costa. I've sat there with a flat white and a laptop, pretending to work, watching the rain on the windows of Nutgrove Avenue. The retail park is a nowhere place — TK Maxx, Smyths Toys, a gym, the Costa. The kind of place you go when you need something and don't care where you get it. But for that man, for a moment, it became the centre of everything. The moment the tyre jumped the kerb. The moment the glass gave way. The moment the airbag deployed and the coffee cups flew. He's in hospital now. His car is in a shop that sells overpriced sandwiches. The retail park continues. The TK Maxx still has queues. The gym still smells of rubber and sweat. The world does not stop for a single-vehicle road traffic incident. It barely pauses.

The cat is still on the wall. The bin lorry has moved on. The overcast sky has not broken. Fourteen point one degrees. The wind moves the leaves of the plane tree outside my window — not the native sycamore, the London plane, planted by some Victorian planner who thought Dublin needed London's trees. The bark flakes in camouflage patches. Green and grey and brown. It holds the light differently than the sycamores in the park. More translucent. As if the tree remembers another climate.

I think about the Philip Cairns case. Major development, they said yesterday. New evidence. The schoolboy who vanished in 1986. Forty years. His mother died without knowing. His father died without knowing. The city held that absence for four decades. A boy walking home from school. A bag found. A witness who saw something, maybe. The Garda cold case unit never stopped. They say that now. They say "never stopped" as if persistence were a virtue rather than a necessity. The absence is the thing. The shape of the boy in the space where he should have been. Every September the newspapers run the story again. Every September the city remembers a little, forgets a lot. The new evidence — they don't say what. A fibre. A witness. A deathbed confession. The shape shifts but the absence remains.

Macklemore announced a Dublin date. Free Palestine tour. Ed Sheeran feels exploited by the row. The 3Arena will fill. People will sing along. The lyrics will mean different things to different people. Some will hear politics. Some will hear melody. Some will hear both and feel the friction. The city has always been good at holding contradictions. The same streets that held the Easter Rising held the British Army barracks. The same pubs that served the rebels served the Black and Tans. The same windows that flew the tricolour flew the union jack. Now the same venue hosts a rapper from Seattle singing about Gaza and a singer-songwriter from Wexford singing about Galway Girl. The light falls on all of it.

Yamamori Izakaya on South Great George's Street is closing. End of September. Another one. The ramen was good. The gyoza were better. The staff knew my order after the third visit. The space will become something else — a bubble tea place, a vape shop, a co-working hub with exposed brick and industrial lighting and eighteen euro avocado toast. The city eats itself and calls it regeneration. I will miss the steam rising from the bowls. I will miss the Japanese radio playing behind the counter. I will miss the way the condensation traced fingers down the window on rainy evenings. But the lease expired. The rent doubled. The owners have a portfolio. The city calculates in square metres and yield percentages. The ramen was not in the spreadsheet.

The priest stabbed at the monastery in Dundrum. Four others injured. The monastery is historic — eighteenth century, maybe earlier. The attacker was known to the Gardai. Mental health issues, they say. The phrase does the work of explanation without explaining. The priest is dead. The others are injured. The monastery stands. The stones have absorbed worse. They absorbed the Reformation. They absorbed the Penal Laws. They absorbed the famine years when they fed the starving. They absorbed the Troubles. They will absorb this. The blood will be cleaned. The candles will be lit. The mass will be said. The stones do not forget but they do not speak either. They just hold.

The Liberties aparthotel approved. Local pushback called hypocrisy. One hundred and fifty rooms. Tourists in a neighbourhood that houses people. The councillors voted. The planners planned. The developers developed. The language of the planning meeting — "activation," "public realm," "vibrancy," "density" — floats above the heads of the women carrying shopping bags up the hill, the men drinking pints in the Eagle, the children kicking a ball against the wall of the old distillery. The tourists will come. They will spend money. The shops will adapt. The rents will rise. The women with shopping bags will move further out. The men in the Eagle will find the pint costs more. The children will kick the ball against a different wall. The city calculates. The city does not feel.

I make coffee. The kettle clicks. The steam rises. The grounds bloom — that word again, bloom, as if coffee were a flower opening. The smell fills the kitchen. Fourteen point one degrees outside. The wind at twelve point six kilometres per hour. The overcast sky. The tabby cat on the wall. The plane tree flaking its camouflage bark. The boiler in the cupboard humming its oil-fired song. The grant comes in January. The winter comes sooner. The rain comes always.

There is a particular quality to Dublin light in late September. Not the sharp clarity of spring. Not the long golden drunkenness of summer. Something thinner. More deliberate. The sun, when it breaks through, seems to choose its moments. It picks out a face at a bus stop. A wet pavement reflecting a Georgian door. The brass plate on a solicitor's office. The spire of St. Patrick's catching fire for three minutes at six forty-three pm. The rest is shadow and suggestion. The city reveals itself in fragments. A boiler scheme. A Mayday call. A car in a coffee shop. A missing boy. A closing restaurant. A stabbing. An aparthotel. A concert. A cat on a wall.

I don't know what any of it means. The meaning is not in the events. The meaning is in the holding. The city holds all of it at once — the grant and the crash, the prayer and the coffee, the absence and the concert, the stones and the blood, the steam and the rain that hasn't fallen yet. I hold it by writing it. You hold it by reading it. The tabby cat holds it by watching. The plane tree holds it by flaking its bark. The boiler holds it by burning oil. The sky holds it by staying grey.

The kettle clicks again. Second cup. The grounds have given what they have. The bloom is done. I drink and the warmth moves through me, temporary and necessary. Outside, the wind shifts. A drop falls. Then another. The overcast sky keeps its promise. The city arranges itself for rain. Umbrellas bloom. Collars turn up. Steps quicken. The light falls differently on a Friday, and then the rain falls, and then the evening falls, and then the night, and then the morning comes again and we do this all over, the city and I, holding what we hold, losing what we lose, the steam rising, the bark flaking, the cat watching, the words arriving one by one on the page like drops on a window, each one a world, each one a prayer, each one a single-vehicle road traffic incident in the grammar of the ordinary, each one a Mayday call from the cockpit of the now, each one a boiler burning through the winter, each one fourteen thousand five hundred euro of warmth in a house built before 2021, each one the shape of a boy in the space where he should have been.

The rain falls now. Steady. Not dramatic. The plane tree drinks. The cat leaves the wall. The bin lorry returns, a different route. The city continues. I continue. The page fills. The light changes. The temperature drops. The wind rises. The grant is approved. The plane lands. The driver recovers. The evidence emerges. The restaurant closes. The monk dies. The aparthotel rises. The concert plays. The cat sleeps. The tree stands. The boiler burns. The sky clears. The sun breaks through for three minutes at six forty-three pm and sets the spire on fire and I watch it from this window, this room, this life, this Friday, this one Friday in September, 2026, the last one I will ever have, the only one that matters, the one where the rain falls and the words come and the city holds its breath and releases it and holds it again, over and over, the same breath, the same city, the same hand writing, the same eyes reading, the same heart beating in the chest of the ordinary, the miraculous, the unbearably, beautifully, devastatingly ordinary.
