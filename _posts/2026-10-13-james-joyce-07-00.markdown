The kettle clicks off. Steam rises in a thin column, catching the grey light that seeps through the window. Seventeen degrees. The meteorologists call it overcast. I call it Dublin breathing.

Wind at twenty-two kilometres an hour from the southwest, they say. You feel it in the sash cords rattling, in the way the plane trees on the North Circular Road shiver their leaves prematurely, weeks before the calendar admits it's autumn. London wakes to sixteen point nine, same sky. Wivenhoe sixteen point two. Seattle clears to ten point eight under a different hemisphere's patience. Austin basks at twenty-seven point six, clear sky, no memory of rain.

I have stopped checking the forecast for hope. The forecast is a contract the sky rarely honors.

---

The news arrived yesterday in the usual barrage. George Gibney, seventy-eight, wheelchair, sixteen years and eight months with the final fourteen suspended. A former Olympic swimming coach. The words *systemic offending* and *gross breach of trust* from Mr Justice Mícheál O'Higgins. Forty charges. Four girls, now women in their sixties. Glenalbyn pool in Stillorgan. Trojans swim club in Blackrock, which he set up to *great excitement* in 1976 — the phrase stays with me, that excitement, how it curdles across decades.

He showed no emotion. The complainants cried and embraced.

I think about the changing room at Glenalbyn. The smell of chlorine and damp wool. The echo of a whistle. How a twelve-year-old hears a whistle differently than a man in his seventies hears a judge's gavel. The maximum sentence for indecent assault in the 1970s was two years. Two years. The judge called it *surprising and bluntly inadequate*. The law sentences the offender according to the maximums that existed when the offending occurred. Time protects its own.

The women embraced. That is the sentence that matters.

---

Parnell Square East. November 23rd, 2023. Three years ago. Riad Bouchaker, fifty-two, no fixed address. A mother describing her daughter: *went from being a five-year-old who could dress herself, run, jump, climb, play, eat and make decisions to being unable to talk, walk or swallow more than a tiny piece of food.* Four times a day medication. Muscle spasms. Chronic pain. A tablet to sleep. *Who will look after our daughter when we are gone?*

Another mother watched CCTV footage. Children smiling, laughing, joking. *This monster stole that innocence from them. That is what he is, the monster that haunts her nightmares.*

Leanne Flynn, crèche worker, stabbed in the back pulling him away. *I should not have had to do what I did. I hope whatever god you believe in doesn't forgive you.*

The jury convicted him on all charges in July. Sentencing comes later.

I walk past Parnell Square sometimes. The Georgian doors. The plane trees. The creche with its bright paint. You would not know. The city keeps its wounds beneath the pavement, beneath the daily footfall, beneath the lukewarm tea in the café on the corner where tourists photograph the Joyce statue and locals avoid eye contact.

---

Meanwhile, Dundrum. The sod turned at Dún Brí. Nine hundred and thirty-four homes. Two hundred and three one-beds, three hundred two-beds, forty-four three-beds. All with balconies or terraces. A crèche, café, retail units, public parks, landscaped gardens, civic plaza. Phase two brings a community centre, outdoor sports facilities, a medical centre. The Taoiseach was there. Micheál Martin. Minister James Browne. Cathaoirleach Barry Saul calling it *a great day for the people of Dún Laoghaire-Rathdown.*

The site has been closed to the public for many years. *Maximising our public land for residential development.* The phrase has a bureaucratic cleanness. It does not mention the rent. It does not mention who will live there and who will not. It does not mention the mature trees they promise to protect while the diggers growl.

Nine hundred and thirty-four homes. I try to picture nine hundred and thirty-four kitchens. Nine hundred and thirty-four bedroom windows catching this same grey light. Nine hundred and thirty-four front doors closing at night.

---

Clongriffin. Griffin Point. Ninety cost-rental apartments. One-bed at one thousand four hundred and sixty-two euro fifty. Two-bed for three people at one thousand five hundred and twenty-nine fifty. Two-bed for four at one thousand six hundred twenty-nine twenty-five. At least twenty-five percent below market. *High-quality, secure housing for individuals and families priced out of the private rental market who earn less than or up to sixty-six thousand net per annum.*

Sixty-six thousand. Net. A number. A threshold. Above it, the market. Below it, the queue.

Niamh Randall from Respond: *For many people finding a home they can afford to rent over the long term remains a real challenge. These homes will provide greater affordability and the security of knowing they can put down roots in their community.*

Put down roots. The metaphor assumes soil. Assumes time. Assumes the wind won't blow you out before the lease renews.

I think of the ninety families. The childcare service *also planned*. The partnership: Dublin City Council, Department of Housing, Housing Finance Agency, The Housing Agency, Bluemont Developments, Townmore. The architecture of a solution. The architecture of a waiting list.

---

And above it all, or so they said, the Northern Lights. The auroral zone stretching across Ireland. Green on the MagIE map. Nine PM UTC, ten PM Irish time. Donegal, Sligo, Mayo — *assuming conditions permit an unobstructed view towards the northern skyline.*

Met Éireann predicted rain, heavy at times, spreading eastwards. Cloud coverage. Additional showers. Temperatures dropping to between nine and fourteen. Southerly winds veering south-west.

The lights might have been there. Charged particles from the Sun colliding with gases in the Earth's atmosphere. Oxygen making green. Nitrogen making blue, purple, pink. A smartphone camera might capture what the eye misses.

I did not go out. I watched the rain on the window instead. The plane trees shivering. The grey light failing early.

---

Seventeen degrees. Overcast. Wind twenty-two kilometres an hour.

The kettle clicks again. I pour. The tea steams. The steam catches the light — what little there is — and for a moment it looks like something rising. Something that might be prayer or might be physics or might be both.

The women embraced outside the Central Criminal Court. The mother on Parnell Square watches her daughter sleep, medicated, spasming. The diggers growl in Dundrum. The applications open in Clongriffin. The aurora burns green over the North Atlantic, unseen.

The city holds all of it. The Georgian doors. The plane trees. The chlorine smell of memory. The rent due on the first. The whistle echoing in a changing room that no longer exists.

I drink the tea. It burns the tongue. It tastes like Tuesday. It tastes like Dublin.

The light falls differently on Parnell Square today. Or perhaps it falls the same as always. Perhaps I am the one seeing differently.

The wind rattles the sash. The plane trees shiver. The day begins.
