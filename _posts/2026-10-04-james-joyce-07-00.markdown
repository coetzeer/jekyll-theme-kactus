The kettle sings its thin song. Steam curls against the window where morning light arrives pale and washed, the colour of weak tea. Eleven and a half degrees, the weather service says. Clear sky. Three point six kilometres per hour of wind — barely a breath. But the forecasters use words like *mixed* and *cooler* and *patchy drizzle* and I know what that means. The light has already begun its retreat. By half past five the sun will surrender. Another week and the clocks fall back, an hour stolen from evening and gifted to morning. Spring forward, fall back. The old rhyme my mother taught me, as if the seasons were a dance step you could memorise.

I stood at the window earlier, before the kettle, before the first cup. The street was empty. A fox crossed the road near the corner shop, unhurried, belonging to the hour between night and day. The Phoenix Park trees stood as silhouettes against a sky that promised nothing dramatic — just the slow grey creep of cloud from the west, the Atlantic breathing on us. Met Éireann calls it *hazy sunshine turning to patchy drizzle*. I call it Tuesday. Or Sunday. October the fourth. The days blur into each other when you stop marking them by anything but the quality of light.

The radio in the kitchen murmurs about Budget 2027. Four hundred to five hundred euro lump sum for those on disability payments. Eight to ten euro increases to the State Pension, Jobseeker's Allowance, Carer's Allowance. Numbers floating in the air like leaves. Four hundred sixty-five thousand households received their first Fuel Allowance last week — thirty-eight euro a week, or two instalments of five hundred thirty-two. I do the sums in my head. Five hundred thirty-two twice is a thousand sixty-four. A thousand sixty-four euro to keep the dark warm. The Christmas Bonus stays — one hundred percent of your weekly payment, minimum twenty euro. The language of budgets is the language of survival dressed in a suit. *Cost of living supports*. *Targeted measures*. *One-off payments*. As if winter comes once and never returns.

A collision on the R156 at Harlockstown. Car and motorcycle. Just after twelve noon. Between Dunboyne and Summerhill. The radio says *emergency services remain at the scene* and *local traffic diversions are in place* and *no further information is available at this time*. The phrases repeat every time. A garda spokesperson. The passive voice of bureaucracy. Someone's father, someone's son, someone's ordinary Sunday shattered on a stretch of road I've driven a hundred times without thinking. The gorse blooming yellow at the ditches. The hedgerows thick with blackberries in September. Now the berries are gone, eaten by birds or rotted on the stem. The road doesn't care. The road keeps taking.

Heimir Hallgrímsson walked out of a press conference yesterday. An Israeli journalist asked about a plane — Dubai to Tel Aviv, co-pilot stabbing the pilot, trying to crash the aircraft. The FAI press officer said football questions only. Then the same journalist claimed a racial slur against an Israeli player in last week's match. The press officer said *that is not true*. The conference ended. Hallgrímsson grabbed his bag and left. Dara O'Shea misses the game — his wife due to give birth. *A simple problem. We deal with it and move on.* The manager's voice: *surviving this camp together as one would be a massive win for me*. *A bond of brothers*. *All the obstacles we face in the future will be less.* I think of the plane. The co-pilot. The knife. The ordinary trust between two people in a cockpit, shattered. How thin the membrane between order and chaos. A football match. A birth. A stabbing at thirty thousand feet. A press conference that becomes a diplomatic incident. The world keeps folding into itself.

The clocks change on the twenty-fifth. Two in the morning. An extra hour in bed, they say, as if sleep were a currency you could bank. But the darkness comes earlier. Five pm sunsets. The light retreating like a tide. Germany started it in 1916. Ireland followed in May of the same year. The European Parliament voted to end it in 2019. Nothing happened. The Commission has no new proposal. The clocks keep turning. We keep pretending we control time. My grandmother's wall clock in the hall — tick tick tick — never knew about daylight saving. It just measured. Now my phone updates automatically. The decorative clocks on the mantelpiece, the one in the car, the oven clock — those I must change by hand. Small rituals. The oven clock always runs three minutes fast. I never correct it properly. Three minutes ahead of the world. A private time zone.

The fox is gone. The street wakes — a car engine, a distant siren, the clatter of bins. My tea has gone cold. The steam stopped rising minutes ago. Eleven and a half degrees. Clear sky. The forecasters promise *plenty of autumn sunshine* and *mostly dry and clear* and *increasing cloud will feed in patchy drizzle*. They speak in probabilities. I speak in the wet on my face when I step outside later, the chill in my fingers, the smell of wet leaves and exhaust and something sweet from the bakery on the corner. The particular contains the universal, Joyce wrote. Or maybe he didn't. Maybe I'm misremembering. Memory is a palimpsest — you write over it so many times the original disappears.

Budget day approaches. The collision on the R156 will become a statistic. Hallgrímsson's press conference will be analysed by pundits who weren't in the room. The clocks will change whether we vote or not. The fox will cross the road again tomorrow at this hour, or another fox will, and the street will be empty and the light will be this same pale washed colour, eleven and a half degrees, clear sky, three point six kilometres per hour of wind. I will make another cup of tea. I will stand at this same window. The kettle will sing its thin song. The steam will curl against the glass. And I will think: this is what it feels like to be alive on an ordinary Sunday in October, in a city that holds its breath between budgets and collisions, between the turning of clocks and the turning of seasons, between the news and the knowing.

The light changes at half past five. That much is certain.
