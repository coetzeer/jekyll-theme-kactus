## The Big Picture

Over the past 48 hours, government bond markets in the United States, United Kingdom, and continental Europe went through a sharp sell-off followed by a partial rebound. In simple terms: investors demanded higher interest rates to lend money to governments, pushing bond prices down and yields up. Then, on Wednesday, the US Treasury announced it would double the size of its bond buyback program, which calmed markets temporarily.

The trigger for the sell-off was a combination of sticky inflation, massive government borrowing, and the ongoing Iran–Israel conflict pushing oil prices toward $91 a barrel. When oil gets more expensive, everything from transport to food costs more, which keeps inflation high. Central banks then have to keep interest rates higher for longer to fight that inflation. Higher rates mean existing bonds paying lower rates become less attractive, so their prices fall and their yields rise.

---

## United States: Thirty-Year Yield Hits a 19-Year High

On Tuesday, August 18, the yield on the 30-year US Treasury bond climbed to 5.34%, its highest level since 2007. The 10-year yield, which influences mortgage rates and corporate borrowing, sat at 4.68% on August 14 and edged up to 4.74% by August 21.

Why did this happen? Two main forces. First, the US federal debt has now topped $40 trillion. The government needs to borrow heavily to cover its deficit, and investors are demanding more compensation for locking up their money for 10 or 30 years. Second, inflation fears have returned. The Iran conflict has driven up energy prices, and the Federal Reserve may need to keep rates elevated — or even raise them further — to keep inflation in check.

On Wednesday, August 19, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced the department would double the size of its long-dated bond buyback operations, from at least $4 billion to at least $8 billion per operation. The idea is to buy back older, less liquid bonds and replace them with new issuance, improving market functioning. Yields fell as much as 10 basis points (0.10 percentage points) on the news. But by Thursday, August 20, Reuters reported the relief was brief — debt worries persist, and yields began creeping higher again.

## United Kingdom: Gilts Follow the US Lead, Then Bounce

UK government bonds, known as gilts, mirrored the US move. The 30-year gilt yield reached 5.78% on August 19, just shy of the 5.87% peak hit in May — the highest since 1998. The 10-year gilt yield hovered around 5.05% by week’s end, up roughly 2 basis points for the week.

On Wednesday, gilt prices posted their biggest daily gain in two weeks. The catalyst was the same US Treasury buyback announcement that helped American bonds. When US yields fall, UK yields often follow because global investors shift money between the two markets.

But the UK has its own problems. The Bank of England held rates steady in July, and July inflation data came in broadly in line with expectations — some components softer, which suggests the BOE may stay on hold for now. Still, the market prices in roughly 41 basis points of rate hikes by December. The BOE also announced it is backing away from mandatory gilt clearing, a regulatory change that would have forced more trades through central clearinghouses. Instead, it will focus on voluntary clearing and portfolio-based repo haircuts — a different path from the US, where mandatory Treasury clearing takes effect December 31.

## Europe: Record Issuance Meets Inflation Fears

Europe’s bond market faced a perfect storm. Germany, the eurozone’s benchmark issuer, sold 30-year bonds at a yield of 3.79% on August 20 — the highest since 2011. The 10-year Bund yield touched 3.26%, its highest since April 2011. France’s 10-year yield hit 4.10%, a 17-year high. French 30-year yields reached levels not seen since 2008.

The OAT–Bund spread — the extra yield investors demand to hold French debt over German — widened to 85 basis points, testing October 2025 highs. Investors are nervous about France’s 2027 budget negotiations and next year’s presidential election. France’s debt-servicing costs are projected to quadruple this decade, from €30 billion in 2020 to €124 billion by 2030.

Making matters worse, European governments came back from the summer break with a flood of new borrowing. The week of August 19 saw €38.3 billion of bond issuance — the busiest post-summer restart on record. Commerzbank estimates German gross bond supply will hit a record €400 billion in 2027. Barclays sees record eurozone gross supply next year. Italy must refinance maturing debt equal to 17% of GDP in 2026, compared with 12% for France and 7% each for Germany and the UK.

The European Central Bank is expected to keep tightening. Markets price a 90% probability of a September rate hike, with the deposit rate seen reaching 2.75% by March 2027, up from 2.25% today. Higher ECB rates mean higher borrowing costs across the continent.

## Why It Matters for Everyone

Bond yields are the price of money. When they rise, mortgages get more expensive, car loans cost more, and companies pay more to invest. Governments spend more on interest, leaving less for hospitals, schools, defense, or tax cuts.

In Germany, 20-year fixed mortgage rates are expected to rise by about 7 basis points in the coming week. In Italy, some smaller banks have already raised mortgage rates. In France, the pass-through is slower but could add 10–20 basis points if yields stay high. The UK faces a similar squeeze: higher gilt yields feed into mortgage pricing and increase the government’s interest bill at a time when the Budget is being prepared.

The common thread across all three regions is that investors are no longer willing to lend cheaply. Years of near-zero rates conditioned markets to expect free money. That era is over. Governments now compete with each other — and with corporate borrowers, including tech giants issuing hundreds of billions for AI infrastructure — for a finite pool of savings.

---

## What to Watch Next

- **US:** Will the Treasury expand buybacks further? Bessent said they could. Will the Fed signal rate cuts or hold steady at the September meeting?
- **UK:** The BOE’s next rate decision is September 18. Watch July wage data and August inflation for clues.
- **Europe:** The ECB meets September 11. The French budget process and German coalition dynamics will shape fiscal credibility. Italy’s refinancing needs keep the periphery vulnerable.
