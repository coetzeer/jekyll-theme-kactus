The morning is grey. Not the grey of absence but the grey of presence — thick, tactile, the kind that presses against windowpanes and makes the city feel closer, smaller, as if Dublin has drawn its shoulders up against the cold. Sixteen degrees. The weather report says overcast, 8.3 km/h wind from somewhere that doesn't matter. But the body knows what the numbers don't: the damp in the joints, the way breath hangs a moment too long, the particular quality of light that makes the brick of the houses look soft, absorbent, like felt.

I woke at 6:40. The alarm didn't wake me; the light did, or rather the lack of it. That particular October light that doesn't so much arrive as seep. It comes through the curtains the colour of weak tea. You don't notice it changing, not really. One day it's September and the evenings stretch golden past nine, and the next you're blinking at 7pm thinking — when did the dark get so quick? The clocks change this month. Someone wrote about it in the paper three days ago: exact date, exact hour. As if time were a thing you could pin to a calendar and not a thing that lives in the body, in the length of shadows across the floorboards.

The paper. I still call it that though I read it on a screen now. The Dublin news. Three days old by the time it reaches this keyboard — the 5th's harvest, gathered for the 6th's reading. Time's small violence.

A creche in Tallaght. Oakview, on the TU Dublin campus. Forty children. Forty small lives measured in footsteps between building blocks and snack time, forty pairs of boots by the door, forty coats on pegs. The provider exercised a break clause in May. The licence expires December 31st. John Lahart, TD for Dublin South-West, stood in the Dáil and said: "This facility, regardless of who owns it, must remain in place." The words hang in the air like smoke. Must remain. As if saying it loud enough makes it true. The Minister of State, Michael Moynihan, acknowledged the stress. The South Dublin Childcare Committee is "collaborating" on "alternative premises." The language of bureaucracy — collaborating, reviewing, alternative — words designed to soften the blow of a door closing. Forty children. Forty families. The woman at the counter who knows which child takes milk and which takes water. The man who fixes the gate that sticks in November. All of it measured in contract clauses and break options.

I think of the light falling on that playground. The same grey light falling on the brick of TU Dublin, on the slides and the sandpit, on the small hands reaching for each other. It doesn't care about contracts. It falls on the open gate and the locked gate alike.

Then there's the Red Line Book Festival. Fifteenth year. October 12th to 18th. Forty events across South Dublin — The Civic, Rua Red, Rathfarnham Castle, local libraries. Sarah Waters discussing queer history with Mary McAuliffe. Louise O'Neill and Lynn Ruane on a memoir called *A Bigger Life*. Ardal O'Hanlon talking crime fiction with Rick O'Shea. Miriam O'Callaghan reflecting on a life in broadcasting. Emmet Kirwan bringing theatre and spoken word. A hundred years of Agatha Christie celebrated by Sophie Hannah and Catherine Ryan Howard. Justine Stafford on the Irish art of "It'll Be Grand." Reel Flamenco. A Family Fun Day with Vikings and Dungeons & Dragons.

Forty events. Seven days. The words *books, ideas, conversation, culture* — they sound like sustenance. They sound like the things that remain when the creche closes and the contract expires. A librarian curated this. Someone whose job is to remember what books feel like in the hand, what stories do to a room full of strangers. I imagine the light in Rathfarnham Castle — different light, older light, filtered through leaded windows onto stone floors where people sit and listen to Sarah Waters speak of queer history. The past made present in a room. The light doesn't change but the listening does.

Michael Flatley's wife died. Niamh. Fifty-two. Twenty years married. He posted a photograph from their wedding day and one with their son, Michael St. James. "My heart is broken. I have lost my beautiful wife, Niamh, and my best friend." The words raw, unguarded. "Hours away" from a flight to America for specialist treatment. He spent "every waking hour" caring for her. She danced from three years old — Riverdance, Lord of the Dance, Fleet of Flames, Celtic Tiger. Met him on the set of Riverdance. The Eurovision interval in 1994 — I remember that light. The whole country stopped. The kitchen in my childhood house, the television flickering, the adults quiet for once, watching Irish feet move like lightning across a stage in Dublin. Niamh in that line. Twenty years later, cancer. A flight she never boarded. The light in the hospital room. The light on the wedding photograph he held up for the camera to see.

Grief is its own weather system. It doesn't read the forecast.

Daniel Kinahan in court. Video link from Portlaoise. Light grey beard, dark grey suit, glasses. Two minutes. "Yes I am." "Yes I can." "Thank you, your honour, thank you." The government jet — a Dassault Falcon 6X, fifty-three million euros — flew to Dubai and back in twenty-four hours. Senior gardaí, the Armed Support Unit, a bullet-and-bomb-proof prison van. Eighteen murders in the feud. PGP phones, encrypted conversations, Sean McGovern sentenced to twenty-four years. Marvin Herbert outside the court, one-eyed, former gangster, friend of Kinahan, shot five times in Marbella in 2008. "It's a joke," he said. "It will all blow over."

The courtroom light. Fluorescent, harsh. The screen light from the video link. The light on the tarmac at Baldonnell when the plane touched down at 6:30pm. The light on the faces of the small crowd assembled outside the Criminal Courts of Justice. The light in Dubai, different altogether — harsh, flat, the light of a place built on sand and money and the turning of blind eyes. Four months fighting extradition. The light in the prison cell in Al Maktoum. The light in Portlaoise now.

All of this happens under the same sky. The same overcast, 16.3-degree, 8.3 km/h sky. The creche in Tallaght. The festival in Rathfarnham. The hospital room where Niamh Flatley died. The courtroom where Kinahan adjusted his glasses. The government jet cutting through cloud at 40,000 feet. The playground sand. The library shelves. The prison van's slit windows.

I think about how the light doesn't choose. It falls on the Taoiseach and the toddler. On the gangster and the garda. On the married couple dancing at Eurovision and the same couple twenty years later in a hospital corridor. On the librarian cataloguing Sarah Waters and the parent reading the notice on the creche door: *We regret to inform you.*

The temperature will drop tonight. The forecast says 3 to 7 degrees by morning. Colder than previous nights. Clear spells. A few showers in the west and north. Wednesday bright and autumnal, 11 to 14 degrees. Thursday cloud from the west, rain following. Friday wet and breezy, then clearance. Saturday brighter. Sunday wetter.

The forecast doesn't tell you how it feels to walk through St. Stephen's Green at 7am when the mist hangs like gauze over the lake. It doesn't tell you the sound of the 46a's engine turning over in the cold, the particular hiss of wet tyres on the N11, the smell of coal smoke from a house in Rathmines that still burns the old way. It doesn't tell you the weight of knowing that forty children will lose their creche while a festival of books opens seven miles away, while a widow holds a wedding photograph, while a man in a grey suit says "yes I can" into a camera.

The light is democratic. The living is not.

I make coffee. The kettle's steam catches the grey light for a second — white on grey, brief as a sentence. The cup warms my hands. The morning stretches ahead: emails, words, the ordinary architecture of a Tuesday. Somewhere in Tallaght a parent is making coffee too, wondering what December brings. Somewhere in Rathfarnham a librarian is unlocking a door. Somewhere in Portlaoise a prisoner is counting the hours to October 11th. Somewhere a husband is learning the shape of absence.

The light doesn't change. But we move through it. We carry our different weathers inside the same sky.
