The light arrives differently now. Half past seven and it's still hesitating at the window, that particular grey-white of mid-October that belongs to no season properly — summer's ghost, winter's promise. I've been awake for an hour, listening to the house settle, the pipes ticking in the walls like a clock with no face.

Dublin at this hour wears a different face. The Liffey runs dark and slow beneath the quays, reflecting nothing recognisable. The Samuel Beckett Bridge harps its cables against a sky that hasn't decided on a colour. Somewhere in Tallaght, a mother has been up all night. Fifteen-year-old Joel Botambengo, six foot one, slim build, black hair, brown eyes — missing from Jobstown since September. The Garda appeal went out days ago. *Contact Tallaght Garda Station on (01) 6666000.* The number sits in the paper like a prayer. A boy that tall doesn't vanish without tearing a hole in the air around him. His mother knows the exact weight of his footsteps on the stairs. She knows which floorboard creaks at the third step. The city holds its breath in ways the news doesn't say.

I think about this as the kettle sighs. The steam rises and catches the weak light, makes it visible for a moment — the particulate dance of morning. Dust motes. Pollen. The breath of the city made visible.

Last month a pilot saw a drone off the Wicklow coast at six thousand feet. Large, black, unidentified. An Aer Lingus Airbus banking toward Dublin, the co-pilot's voice calm on the radio: *traffic, left, three kilometres.* The drone didn't respond. Didn't squawk. Just *there*, in airspace that belongs to schedules and flight plans, to the thousand flights that day which never left the ground because a system in Swanwick coughed. Josie Donoghue sat three hours on the tarmac at Gatwick. *Like trying to get out of Alcatraz,* she said. The sky has become a contested space. We look up less than we used to. The drones, the satellites, the contrails stitching the heavens — we've ceded the upper air to machines and the machines' masters.

But down here, at street level, the fashion students are stitching something else. Dublin Independent Fashion Week — DIFW, they call it, as if the vowels were expendable. JAK of WAK-O cutting garments from dead stock military surplus at Fade Street Studios. Conflict to Creation, the exhibition named. One-of-a-kind pieces from fabric that once wore camouflage in some theatre we've agreed to forget. The King's Trust Enterprise Award for Northern Ireland sits on a shelf in Belfast Metropolitan College. I like the sound of it: *Conflict to Creation.* As if the arrow between them were straight. As if the making undoes the unmaking. The model walks the runway in a jacket cut from a tent that sheltered soldiers in Helmand. The lining still smells of dust and diesel if you press your face to it. Fashion as archaeology. Fashion as exorcism.

The gas bill arrived yesterday. Bord Gáis raising prices for hundreds of thousands. The electricity too. *Hundreds of thousands of customers* — the phrase renders the particular invisible. Mrs. O'Reilly on the third floor turning her heating off in September. The student in the basement flat cooking on a camping stove. The pensioner in Cabra wrapping newspapers around the hot water tank. The numbers aggregate upward: significant degree of overvaluation in the housing market, renters thrown to the wolves, the Lord Mayor saying transport hikes could be the straw that breaks the camel's back. Metaphors mixing in the Dáil chamber. The camel's back. The wolves. The straw. Meanwhile Paddy Power might close a hundred betting shops, four hundred jobs. The flutter, the accumulator, the Friday night slip — gone from the high street, moved to the phone in your pocket, frictionless and infinite.

I walk to the corner shop. The air tastes of wet leaves and diesel and something sweeter — the bakery on Camden Street venting cinnamon and yeast into the morning. The shopkeeper, Mr. Zhang, doesn't ask how I am. He nods. *The usual.* Two euros thirty for the Barry's Tea, eighty cents for the sliced pan. The transaction completes without words. I've been coming here since 2019. He's watched my hair grey at the temples. I've watched his daughter grow from toddler to schoolgirl, her backpack now nearly as tall as she is. We know each other's rhythms. The city is made of these silent contracts.

At the traffic lights on Rathmines Road, a cyclist waits beside me. High-vis vest, helmet camera, the red light reflected in her glasses. She's mouthing something — a song, a podcast, an argument rehearsed. The green man flashes. We move in different velocities, she on carbon fibre and lithium, I on leather soles and cartilage. The city accommodates both. Barely.

The weather report from late September said fourteen and a half degrees, clear sky, seventeen kilometres per hour wind. Today feels colder. The wind has teeth. It finds the gap at my collar, the space between glove and sleeve. Dublin weather doesn't announce itself; it infiltrates. You feel it in the joints before the forecast confirms it. The meteorologist on the radio last week — *localised flooding warning, persistent rain hitting.* The language of siege. The sky as enemy. But the rain also washes the Guinness stains from the pavement outside the Brazen Head, makes the cobbles glisten like jet, turns the streetlights into haloes. There's no pure phenomenon. The flooding and the beauty arrive on the same front.

I pass the church on Harrington Street. The doors are open. Inside, the smell of wax and old wood and something older — the accumulated breath of generations kneeling, standing, sitting, leaving. A single candle burns at the Lady altar. Someone's intention made visible. I don't believe, not in the way the candle asks. But I believe in the candle. In the striking of the match, the flare, the steadying of the flame. The small defiance of darkness. The woman lighting it — grey coat, blue scarf, hands that have washed dishes and held grandchildren and typed emails and wiped tears — she doesn't look at me. She doesn't need to. We're both just keeping vigil in our ways.

Back at the desk now. The tea has cooled to drinkable. The light has strengthened, shifted toward something resembling gold, thin and watery but gold nonetheless. It falls across the keyboard, illuminates the dust on the bookshelf — Joyce, Beckett, Heaney, Boland, the new voices I keep meaning to read. The city continues its million conversations: the Luas clanging through the green at Stephen's Green, the barcode scanner beeping at Tesco Metro, the shouted Portuguese of the delivery rider calling up to an apartment, the gull screaming over the chipper on Aungier Street, the silent calculation of a thousand spreadsheets in a thousand glass boxes on the quays.

Joel Botambengo is still missing. The drone investigation continues. JAK is cutting fabric somewhere in Belfast. Mrs. O'Reilly is deciding between heat and food. The woman in the church watches her candle burn down. I sit here making sentences from the morning, trying to honour the weight of it all, the particular gravity of each life passing through this latitude and longitude on this Monday in October.

The light at half past seven doesn't last. It moves. It always moves. But for a moment — this moment — it holds the city in a kind of grace. Not the religious kind. The physical kind. The way light rests on things without asking permission. The way it reveals what's already there.

I'll drink the tea. I'll write the next sentence. The day will do what days do: unfold, demand, surprise, disappoint, end. But right now, at this desk, in this light, the city is mine to witness. And witnessing is its own form of prayer.
