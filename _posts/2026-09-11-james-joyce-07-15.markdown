The light at seven a.m. in September is a particular thing. Fourteen point four degrees Celsius, the weather app tells me — partly cloudy, wind thirteen kilometers an hour from the southwest. But the app doesn't tell you how it feels on the skin: that cool kiss of air that carries the sea in it, the way the clouds hang low enough to touch the cranes pooling around the docklands, the smell of wet brick and diesel and something floral pushing up from the Phoenix Park. I stood at the window for a long time watching it. The city waking in that specific grey-green light that only Dublin does properly.

Three nights in a row now, the papers say, a gang of masked youths has been jumping onto ground-floor balconies in Waterside, that development stranded between Swords and Malahide. At least one of them carries a knife. They ride bicycles right up to the railings, check for open doors — and the doors are open, because of the weather, because people want the air. A resident named Marcio Santos, who trained in jiu-jitsu in Brazil, told the press he tracked them himself yesterday evening around six. Four males, sixteen to twenty, dark clothing, balaclavas. They robbed the local Daybreak shop too. Multiple emergency calls to Swords Garda Station. No patrol cars dispatched.

I think about those balconies. The particular vulnerability of a ground-floor flat in this city — your living room opening directly onto the path, the only thing between you and the street a sheet of glass and a rail. People leave them open in September because the air is sweet and the heating hasn't kicked in yet. You're eating your dinner, watching the news, and someone's face appears at the window. Not a ghost. A boy with a knife. The gardaí don't come. The resident says a violent confrontation is inevitable. He says he'll protect his family if they cross the threshold. He says they shouldn't have to.

The government jet touched down in Lanzarote at twelve twenty-four yesterday. A Dassault Falcon 6X, the same one that brought Daniel Kinahan home from Dubai last month. It took off from Casement Aerodrome in Baldonnell at nine oh nine. By evening Jason Hutch — thirty-seven, also known as Jamie Murphy, son of Gerry "The Monk" Hutch — was being flown back to Dublin under armed guard, headed for the District Court and eventually the Special Criminal Court. A European arrest warrant. Organised crime charges. Money laundering allegations in both jurisdictions. His father, the Monk, still has a file with the DPP the height of Mount Everest, or so he told the Irish Mirror at the launch of a play about his life. "I'm no leader of any crime gang," he said. "I was never in a crime gang. The judge said I was the leader of a family and I think I'll leave it at that." The judge also said he was the figurehead, the patriarchal figure, that he had possession of the guns from the Regency, that it was a reasonable possibility nothing was carried out by the Hutch organised crime group without his say-so. David Byrne, thirty-three, shot dead in the foyer of the Regency Hotel by armed gunmen disguised as gardaí carrying AK-47s. February 5, 2016. A Friday afternoon.

In Finglas West, the Drugs Unit seized a loaded semi-automatic firearm and a number of rounds of ammunition yesterday. Targeting local organised criminal activity, the press release says. Investigations ongoing. Gardaí in DMR West are committed to keeping people safe.

Three thousand applications for ninety-eight cost-rental homes at Niven Oaks in Santry. One-bedroom apartments at thirteen hundred fifty euro a month, two-beds at fifteen eighty-five. Passivhaus standard — ultra-low energy, almost no traditional heating needed. The development opened officially on Wednesday. Two hundred sixty-eight homes total: one hundred seventy social, ninety-eight cost rental. More than three thousand people applied for the ninety-eight. The math is brutal. Thirty to one. Minister for Housing James Browne called it a tangible example of government investment. The Mayor of Fingal called it a fabulous development. A resident named Gavin McEneff stood in the photograph between the CEO of Cairn and the Chair of Tuath Housing, smiling. I wonder if he knows how lucky he is. I wonder how many of the other two thousand nine hundred and two people spent the night in a room they can't afford, or at a parent's kitchen table, or in a car.

Darkey Kelly's on Fishamble Street, Temple Bar — closure order issued August eleventh, lifted August seventeenth. Evidence of cockroach activity throughout the bar. Multiple stages of the cockroach life cycle. Live and dead cockroaches in close proximity to open ice, glasses, keg taps. Mini India on Westmoreland Street, closure order August twenty-seventh, lifted August thirty-first. Rodent droppings, gnaw marks. The Chief Executive of the Food Safety Authority said it's concerning to see such serious deficiencies in basic food safety controls. Consumers have a right to safe food.

I walk the city in my head. The balconies in Waterside. The government jet on the tarmac in Lanzarote. The queue for keys in Santry stretching around the block. The cockroaches in the ice at Darkey Kelly's. The semi-automatic in a garda evidence bag in Finglas. All of it happening in the same week, the same city, the same fourteen-degree light.

There's a particular weight to September in Dublin. The tourists are thinning. The students are arriving. The light changes — shorter days, longer shadows, that specific pale gold that hits the Georgian doors around Merrion Square around five in the afternoon and makes the fanlights glow. The air carries rain even when it's not raining. You can taste it in the back of your throat, mineral and cold.

My father used to say the weather in this city is not something that happens to you. It's something you live inside. He'd stand at the window in the morning, same as me, rolling a cigarette, looking at the sky. "Soft day," he'd say, or "There's rain in it," or "Grand stretch in the evenings." He didn't need an app. He knew the weight of the air, the direction of the wind, the promise in the clouds.

I think about the residents in Waterside tonight, checking their locks, maybe closing the balcony doors for the first time in weeks. The heat of the day trapped inside. The sound of bicycles on the path outside, tyres on wet leaves. A shadow passing the window. The knife catching the streetlight.

I think about Jason Hutch on the government jet, the hum of the engines, the Spanish coastline receding, the Irish Sea opening beneath him. What a man thinks about at thirty-seven, being flown home to face the Special Criminal Court, knowing his father's shadow stretches across three decades of this city's history. The Regency. The guns. The file at the DPP the height of Mount Everest.

I think about the two thousand nine hundred and two people who didn't get a key in Santry. The applications they filled out, the hope they carried in a folder or an email attachment. The rent they pay for rooms with mould on the ceiling, landlords who don't answer the phone. The calculation of how much longer they can hold on.

The cockroaches in the ice. The gun in the evidence bag. The knife on the balcony.

Fourteen point four degrees. Partly cloudy. Wind thirteen kilometers an hour from the southwest.

The light shifts. A cloud passes. The cranes at the docklands catch it for a moment, steel bones against grey sky. Somewhere in the city a church bell rings — seven thirty, maybe, or eight. The number 16 bus rattles past, full of people going to work, heads against windows, earbuds in, shoulders tight against the cool.

I should make tea. I should start the day. But I stay at the window a moment longer, watching the city I've lived in for decades reveal itself in the morning light — beautiful and brutal, ordinary and impossible, all of it happening at once, all of it true.

The rain will come later. It always does.
