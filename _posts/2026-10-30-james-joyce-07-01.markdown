The light hits different at this hour. Seven a.m. and the sky is already clear, washed pale by something that isn't quite sun and isn't quite memory. Twelve point three degrees. The wind moves at fourteen point eight kilometres per hour from somewhere east, I think, or maybe it's just the city breathing. Clear sky, the report says. Dublin. London overcast. Wivenhoe clear. Seattle overcast. Austin overcast and twenty degrees. Texas always Texas, even in the data.

I keep the window cracked. The air comes in tasting of wet leaves and diesel and something older, something that might be the Liffey breathing back at us after all these centuries. October thirty. The year sliding toward its close the way a coin slides across a pub counter — inevitable, familiar, the sound of it known before it stops.

Christmas FM launches its song contest today. Twenty-eight days until the station returns to FM, subject to licence, they always say subject to licence, as if the airwaves belong to anyone but the dead and the dreaming. November first for streaming. November twenty-eighth for the dial. The SPAR Christmas FM Song Contest. Musicians, bands, solo artists — send us your festive classic, your original or your cover of a classic, and the winner rubs shoulders with Mariah and Wham! and Michael Bublé. Last year Mark Leen from Tralee took it with *Here On Christmas Day*, the Kerry School of Music Chamber Choir and the Kerry Children's Choir behind him. I think about those children's voices. I think about Tralee, which I have never seen, and the way a song can carry a place you've never been into a kitchen in Rathmines, into a car on the M50, into the small hours when you're the only one awake and the radio is the only voice that answers.

The charity drive runs alongside it. The Magic of Christmas appeal. Barnardos. Barretstown. Make-A-Wish Ireland. Community Foundation Ireland. Names that mean children who need things that money can sometimes buy and sometimes cannot. I wonder if the winning song this year will be about snow. It rarely snows here. The weather report says plenty of dry conditions, bright spells, just patches of rain and drizzle. High pressure building. Friday: dry, mix of cloud and bright spells, thirteen to fifteen degrees. Saturday: rain clearing southeastwards. Sunday: dry and settled, cooler, ten to thirteen. Monday: dry and bright again. The meteorologists speak in probabilities and we listen like it's gospel. Plenty of dry. A phrase that sounds like a promise but means nothing more than the absence of wet.

Two hundred and sixty-eight acres. That's the other number lodged in the morning. The Land Development Agency bought it — Kilmartin in Dublin 15, Calliaghwee in Meath. Eighty-five point seven hectares in Fingal, twenty-two point eight in Meath. Three thousand homes potentially. Commercial units. Community facilities. Near established housing in Kilmartin, employment zones in Blanchardstown, the Dunboyne commuter line. Ten substantial landbanks now, the LDA says. Sixteen thousand homes potentially across the greater Dublin region from privately purchased sites alone. Minister James Browne calls it momentum. Phelim O'Neill calls it a catalyst. The words land and home and community and delivery and pipeline and phased growth — they float in the air like dust motes in this morning light, visible only when the angle is right.

Three thousand homes. I try to picture it. Three thousand kitchens. Three thousand beds. Three thousand front doors with three thousand keys. Three thousand lives unfolding in rooms that don't exist yet on land that has waited, empty, for generations maybe, or maybe it was farmed, maybe children ran across it, maybe lovers walked its boundary, maybe nothing happened at all and the grass just grew and the rain fell and the seasons turned. Now it's hectares and acreage and strategic acquisition. The language of planning is a language that erases. It builds by forgetting.

U2 announced European dates yesterday. The Greatest Show On Earth. Carnaval De Luz, the album, out November thirteenth. Stockholm. Paris. Lisbon. Amsterdam. London, two nights at Tottenham Hotspur Stadium. Berlin. Milan, San Siro. Wolf Alice supporting. South American dates added. North American dates coming. But Ireland — Ireland waits. The Edge said it, quiet but firm: *We'll be wrapping it up in Ireland. There's nowhere else we'd want to be for that party.* Mount Temple Comprehensive. Grafton Street. Fifty years. The band that started in a kitchen in Drumcondra, or was it a classroom, or was it a bedroom with posters peeling, will come home last. The party at the end of the tour. The homecoming that hasn't been dated, hasn't been located, hasn't been ticketed. Irish fans wait. We're good at waiting. We've been waiting for things to be built on this land for a long time.

The light in the room has shifted. Brighter now. The clear sky means the sun actually reaches the floorboards, dust dancing in the beam. I think about the children's choir from Kerry. I think about three thousand homes that are currently nothing but coordinates and planning permission and someone's spreadsheet. I think about Bono's voice, The Edge's guitar, the specific silence of a stadium before the first chord. I think about Christmas FM playing *Fairytale of New York* for the ten thousandth time and someone hearing it for the first time, someone for whom the song will become a memory attached to this year, this flat, this heartbreak or this joy.

The weather doesn't care. Twelve point three degrees. Clear sky. Fourteen point eight kilometres per hour. The Liffey flows. The city wakes. A bus groans past. A cyclist clicks past. Somewhere a radio turns on — maybe Christmas FM already testing, maybe just Morning Ireland, maybe someone's playlist. The day begins the way every day begins: in the middle of a story that started long before we arrived and will continue long after we're gone. The light falls on the floorboards. The dust dances. I make coffee. The steam rises and catches the beam for a moment, visible, beautiful, gone.

Three concrete things lodged in the morning: a song contest, a land purchase, a tour without a home date. Three thousand potential homes. Twenty-eight days until Christmas FM on FM. Fifty years of a band. The numbers accumulate. The stories stack. The city holds them all, the way the Liffey holds the rain and the runoff and the occasional shopping trolley and the reflections of the lights at night — Christmas lights, street lights, the lights of windows where people are living lives that will never make the news but are the only news that matters.

I drink the coffee. It's good. The day is clear. The rest is waiting.
