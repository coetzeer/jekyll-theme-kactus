The light does not so much arrive as seep. Eight point four degrees. Overcast. The wind barely moves — one point one kilometres per hour, the weather report says, as if that precision means anything to the body waiting at a bus stop on the North Circular. The air sits heavy, damp, the particular grey that makes the city's brick breathe. You feel it in the fingers first, that cold that is not cold exactly but the absence of warmth, the way November strips the afternoon back to its skeleton.

I have been walking these streets for thirty years and still the light surprises me. Not the bright suddenness of July, the way it gilds the Liffey and turns the Guinness barges into something deliberate, painted. This light — this November light — it does not gild. It reveals. The soot on the Georgian doorframes. The rust weeping from the railings of Mountjoy Square. The faces of the women pushing prams past the boarded-up betting shop on Parnell Street, their breath small clouds in the stillness.

Three pieces of the city arrived in the feed this morning. Three calculations of what it costs to stay.

The National Transport Authority has decided that the 90-minute fare — two euro, ninety minutes, the small mercy that let you cross the city for the price of a cheap coffee — will become two-thirty. An average fifteen percent increase across the board. The councillors call it outrageous, irresponsible, regressive. Feljin Jose, Green Party, Phibsborough: *it's a fifty cent increase per journey. Twice a day that's a euro a day, it's over three hundred a year. I didn't get an excise cut, I didn't get a VAT cut on my restaurant.* The numbers accumulate in the telling. Three hundred euro. The price of staying mobile. The price of the commute from Phibsborough to the city centre, a journey that takes twenty minutes on the 120 when the traffic holds, forty when it doesn't. Hazel Chu says the increase hits the poorest hardest. Conor Reddy, People Before Profit, says free public transport is the only answer — *the Ireland I want to live in is one where people don't spend hours of their lives sitting in gridlock on congested roads.* Daithí Doolan, Sinn Féin: *every penny counts.* The language of the chamber, the language of the street. The fare rises. The city calculates.

I think of the 120 bus rattling past my window at six-fifteen, the driver's face illuminated in the dashboard glow, the few passengers folded into themselves against the morning. Each one doing their sums. The woman with the Tesco bags. The student with the headphones. The man in the high-vis jacket reading a folded Irish Times. Two euro thirty. Two euro thirty. Two euro thirty. The repetition of it, the small violence of arithmetic.

Then the second piece: Dublin, second most expensive city in Europe for remote workers. Twenty-eight hundred and four euro a month before you even think about a desk. Three thousand one hundred and twenty-four with coworking. London alone sits above us. The average rent: two thousand forty-three. The number sits in the chest like a stone. I think of the flat on the South Circular, the one I looked at in 2019 — fifteen hundred for a box room with a window onto a brick wall. The landlord called it *cozy*. The agent called it *vibrant*. The bunq index calls it a six percent surge year on year. Joe Wilson, Chief Evangelist: *remote working has fundamentally changed the relationship between where people live and where they work.* The evangelist's language. The flexibility to choose. The freedom to leave. But choose what? Choose where? The cities that remain — Lisbon, Valencia, Málaga, Athens, Berlin, Amsterdam — they offer *better value*. Value. As if a life could be valued. As if the sound of the seagulls over the Liffey at dawn, the particular smell of rain on hot tar in July, the way the Guinness smells in a proper pub — the Stag's Head, say, or Mulligan's on Poolbeg Street — as if these could be entered into a spreadsheet.

I walked past a To Let sign on Camden Street yesterday. The agent's number. The promise of *modern living*. The building had been a laundry once. My grandmother took her washing there. The smell of steam and soap and the women's voices in Irish and English, the rhythm of it. Now *modern living*. Two thousand a month for the ghost of a laundry.

The third piece: Katie Taylor. Croke Park. Saturday. Eighty thousand seats. Flora Pili, unbeaten, French. The farewell fight. Westlife — Shane, Nicky, Kian — performing before the final ring walk. *She has inspired the whole country and we couldn't be prouder to perform for her at Croke Park, a stadium that also means so much to us.* The words are polished, rehearsed, the language of the press release. But underneath: a woman from Bray who fought her way into history, who carried the weight of a nation's expectation every time she stepped through those ropes. Eighty thousand people. The roar. The silence before the bell. The way the light falls across the canvas at that hour in September — but this is November, and the fight is Saturday, and the weather will be what it will be. Overcast, probably. Eight degrees. The body knows. The body remembers.

I think of the Crossguns Snooker Club in Drumcondra. Alive alive-o. The memory of it surfaces unbidden: the click of balls, the chalk dust motes dancing in the shaft of light from the high window, the old men in their cardigans framing shots with the slow deliberation of prayer. The club survived the pandemic, survived the developers, survived the city's hunger for flats. *Alive alive-o.* The cry of the fishmongers, Molly Malone, the cockles and mussels — but here it means something else. It means: we are still here. We are still playing. The cue strikes. The red drops. The frame continues.

The wind does not move. One point one kilometres per hour. The trees on the North Circular hold their leaves in a kind of suspense. The plane trees, the limes, the horse chestnuts — they know something we have forgotten. They stand in the grey light and wait. The bus arrives. The doors fold open with a pneumatic sigh. The driver nods. I tap the card. Two euro. Not yet two-thirty. Today, at least, the old price holds.

Inside, the warmth hits like a confession. The smell of wet wool, of rain on skin, of the city's breath collected in this metal box. The woman with the Tesco bags finds a seat. The student keeps the headphones on. The man in high-vis reads his paper. The 120 pulls away, rattling past the Mater, past the Rotunda, past the Garden of Remembrance where the Children of Lir rise from the pool in eternal transformation — swans becoming human, human becoming swan, the myth that refuses to settle. The bus crosses the Liffey at O'Connell Bridge. The river is slate, opaque, reflecting nothing. The gulls wheel above the quay walls, their cries stitching the air.

*Remote working has fundamentally changed the relationship between where people live and where they work.*

The words follow me. I get off at College Green. Walk past Trinity's front gate, the campanile, the tourists posing for photos they will upload to clouds I will never see. The cobblestones are slick. The air tastes of coal smoke and diesel and something older — the river, the sea, the thousand years of breath exhaled into these streets. A seagull drops a crab shell onto the pavement. It shatters. The gull screams. A woman in a red puffer jacket — *gardai hunting woman in red puffer jacket after violent robbery in north Dublin*, the headline from September — hurries past, head down. The colour burns in the grey.

I sit on a bench in the college park. The grass is wet. The trees are bare skeletons against the sky. Eight point four degrees. The cold seeps through my trousers, into the bone. This is the temperature of decision. This is the temperature at which people calculate: stay or go. Pay the rent or leave. Take the bus or walk. Fight the fight or hang the gloves.

Katie Taylor will walk into Croke Park on Saturday. Eighty thousand voices. Westlife singing. The ring walk — the long walk from the tunnel to the canvas, the seconds stretching, the weight of every fight she has ever fought pressing against her shoulders. *Undisputed. Three-time. Final.* The words are heavy. They carry the weight of history. But underneath the words: a body. A body that has absorbed punches, delivered them, trained in the cold dawns of Bray, run the cliffs, skipped rope in a gym that smelled of sweat and liniment and hope. The body remembers what the statistics forget.

The remote workers calculate their spreadsheets. The councillors calculate their votes. The NTA calculates its sustainable model. The landlords calculate their yields. The developers calculate their margins. The city calculates its price.

But the plane trees on the North Circular do not calculate. They hold their leaves. The Liffey does not calculate. It flows. The gulls do not calculate. They scream. The old men at Crossguns chalk their cues, line up the shot, strike — the click, the roll, the pocket. *Alive alive-o.*

I stand. The cold has settled in my knees. The bench holds the imprint of my warmth, a small ghost I leave behind. I walk toward Dame Street, toward the castle, toward the river again. The light has not changed. It will not change. This is November. This is the grey that reveals. The soot on the doorframes. The rust on the railings. The faces of the women with the prams. The price of the bus. The price of the flat. The price of the fight.

Two euro thirty tomorrow. Two euro thirty the day after. Three hundred euro a year. Two thousand forty-three a month. Eighty thousand seats. Three-time undisputed. Eight point four degrees. One point one kilometres per hour.

The numbers accumulate. The city breathes. I walk.

*Alive alive-o.*

The cry follows me down the quays, past the Four Courts, past the Custom House, out toward the sea where the light finally, reluctantly, breaks through the cloud — a thin blade of pale gold on the water, brief, deliberate, gone.
