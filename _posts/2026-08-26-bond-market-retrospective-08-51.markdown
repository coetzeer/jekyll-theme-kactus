Government bond markets across the United States, United Kingdom, and eurozone have just lived through a volatile two days where geopolitics, fiscal intervention, and central-bank expectations collided. Yields — which move opposite to prices — swung on Iran-driven oil spikes, an unprecedented U.S. Treasury buyback programme, and shifting bets on how far the Federal Reserve, Bank of England, and European Central Bank will tighten. Here is what happened, why it happened, and what it means for anyone with a mortgage, a pension, or a savings account.

### United States: The Buyback Rally Fizzles, Jackson Hole Looms

The biggest U.S. story began Wednesday, 19 August, when Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent doubled the government's buyback of long-dated debt — raising each operation from $2 billion to at least $4 billion, targeting bonds with 10 to 30 years to maturity. The 30-year yield had just touched its highest level since 2007, and the 10-year was climbing above 4.70%, making mortgages, car loans, and corporate borrowing painfully expensive.

Markets initially cheered. But the rally lasted barely two sessions. By Thursday the 21st, both the 10-year and 30-year yields had risen more than five basis points, wiping out the earlier drop. On Monday the 24th, yields fell again as investors positioned for Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's keynote at the Jackson Hole symposium (27–29 August) — his first major public appearance since taking the helm in May 2026. The 10-year eased to 4.66% on Tuesday the 25th, and the 30-year slipped to around 5.19%.

Two fresh wrinkles appeared on the 25th. First, reports surfaced that the Treasury might tap its nearly $1 trillion general account at the Federal Reserve to finance the expanded buybacks — a move critics say blurs the line between fiscal and monetary policy. Second, Bessent announced new "economic D-Day" sanctions on Iran, warning that any country or company doing business with Tehran would face retaliation. Brent crude tumbled 3% to $89.46 a barrel on de-escalation hopes, helping push yields down. But the underlying tension remains: the Treasury is effectively capping long-term yields while the Fed holds rates at 3.63%, and markets are parsing whether Warsh will accommodate, resist, or coordinate with Bessent's yield-capping efforts.

### United Kingdom: Gilts Above 5%, Burnham's Fiscal Squeeze

UK government bonds — gilts — have been disproportionately hit. The 10-year yield hovered at 4.99% on the 26th, having briefly pierced 5.1% earlier in the week. Three structural factors make gilts uniquely vulnerable.

First, public debt sits near 95% of GDP — nearly triple pre-crisis levels — and debt interest alone now exceeds £100 billion a year. July borrowing came in worse than expected, wiping out roughly £7 billion of the £23.6 billion fiscal headroom the Office for Budget Responsibility allows under current rules. New Prime Minister Andy Burnham and Chancellor John Healey face warnings that any "fundamental" cost-of-living support will require tax rises or spending cuts.

Second, close to a quarter of the gilt market is inflation-linked — the largest share of any major developed economy. When oil spiked above $90 after the Iran ceasefire collapsed, those index-linked bonds automatically raised the government's interest bill in real time. Analysts estimate two-thirds of the yield jump after an inflation shock comes from investors demanding extra compensation to hold UK debt.

Third, the Bank of England faces a genuine dilemma. Weak growth argues for cuts; an oil-driven inflation spike (CPI 2.9% in July, highest since March) argues for hikes. Markets now price one BoE rate hike by year-end and another 25 basis points by April 2027. The BoE has also quietly backed away from mandatory gilt clearing — a reform meant to strengthen the market — leaving the UK on a different regulatory path from the US, where mandatory Treasury clearing takes effect this December.

For UK households, this isn't abstract. Higher gilt yields feed directly into mortgage rates, and the government's interest bill crowds out spending on public services.

### Europe: Bunds Near 15-Year Highs, ECB Hawkishness Builds

In the euro area, the German 10-year Bund yield held at 3.25% on the 25th, just off the 15-year high of 3.275% touched the previous week. The broader eurozone 10-year benchmark stood around 3.64%. But the real action is in the spreads — the extra yield investors demand to hold French, Italian, or Spanish debt instead of German.

France's 10-year OAT yielded about 4.08%, leaving the OAT–Bund spread at 83 basis points — relatively tight historically, but political uncertainty and declining foreign appetite (notably from Japanese investors) keep the premium elevated. Italy's BTP–Bund spread has compressed significantly, reflecting improved fiscal credibility under the current government.

The catalyst for the recent surge was the same geopolitical shock hitting the UK: the Iran conflict. On 18 August, Euronews reported French 10-year yields hit a 17-year high and German Bunds reached their highest level since 2011. The ECB's policy-sensitive two-year Bund yield rose over 25 basis points as traders ramped up bets on further rate hikes, fearing the Middle East conflict could fuel longer-term inflation through energy prices.

Money markets now price more than 40 basis points of additional ECB tightening this year, with a September hike fully priced in. The deposit rate would rise to 2.5%, and traders see a 25% chance it reaches 3% by March 2027. Eurozone inflation accelerated to 2.9% in July, well above the 2% target, while Germany's Q2 GDP grew 0.3% — above the preliminary 0.2% — giving the ECB cover to tighten. Unlike the Fed, the ECB has no explicit dual mandate; its primary objective is price stability, which makes it less inclined to look through supply-side shocks.

### Why This Matters to You

Bond yields are the price of money across time. When they rise, everything priced off them — mortgages, car loans, corporate debt, government borrowing — gets more expensive. The last two days have shown a world where fiscal authorities (Treasuries, Finance Ministries) are actively managing their yield curves, central banks are navigating independence questions, and geopolitics is injecting volatility that no model fully captures.

For the US, the question is whether Treasury buybacks become a permanent feature — and whether the Fed accommodates or resists. For the UK, it's whether structural vulnerabilities (high debt, inflation-linked exposure, fiscal constraints) force a crisis or a credible consolidation path. For Europe, it's whether the ECB can maintain credibility on inflation without choking the fragile recovery.

Markets don't price perfection; they price uncertainty. Right now, uncertainty is abundant.
