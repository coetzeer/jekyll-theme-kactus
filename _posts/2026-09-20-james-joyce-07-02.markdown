The light arrives different at this hour. Not arrived — arrives, present tense, continuous. Twelve point eight degrees. Overcast. The wind thirteen point three kilometres per hour from somewhere west, somewhere Atlantic. The weather report sits on the screen in its neat table: Dublin, London, Wivenhoe, Seattle, Austin. Each city its own small climate, its own mathematics of air. Dublin and London and Wivenhoe all overcast. Seattle clear. Austin thirty-one degrees and clear. Texas bakes while we wait for the cloud to decide what it wants to be.

I have been awake since before six. Not sleeping, not quite waking. That space between where memories surface without permission. The girl walking to school alone — what age? The ISPCC says no specific legislation, no hard number. Maturity. Footpaths. Pedestrian crossings. Road congestion. Rehearse the journey several times. Talk through scenarios. What if your friend isn't there. What if you need help. The NSPCC across the water suggests nine to eleven. Nine. Eleven. The space between those numbers holds entire childhoods.

My own walk to school. North Circular Road. The weight of the satchel. The way the light fell on the plane trees in October, that particular gold-green you only see in Dublin autumn. No one asked if I was mature enough. No one rehearsed me. You simply went. Or you didn't. The decision belonged to the morning, to the weather, to whether your mother had the energy to argue.

Now parents weigh milestones like gold. The language of risk assessment has replaced the language of trust. Footpaths. Crossings. Congestion. Maturity metrics. I think of the women in their twenties arrested in North Dublin yesterday. Five point seven kilograms of herbal cannabis, seventy-nine thousand eight hundred euro. Cocaine worth three hundred sixty-eight thousand. Twenty-seven thousand nine hundred cash. Four hundred forty-seven thousand eight hundred total. Two women. Two lives. The Garda stations in North Dublin hold them now under Section 2 of the Criminal Justice Drug Trafficking Act 1996. Intelligence-led operation. Revenue Customs. Garda National Drugs and Organised Crime Bureau. Coolock District Drugs Unit. The words arrange themselves in their official architecture, clean and bloodless.

But behind the words: two women in their twenties. Someone's daughters. Someone's sisters. Perhaps someone's mothers. The cannabis weighed out in kilograms. The cocaine subject to analysis. The cash counted in euros. The paraphernalia bagged and labelled. Four hundred forty-seven thousand eight hundred euro. I keep turning that number over. What does it buy? What does it cost? The street value of a life turned down a particular road. The street value of a choice made at fourteen, at sixteen, at nineteen, that led here, to a Garda station in North Dublin on a Friday in September.

The storm tracks toward Iceland. Alan O'Reilly of Carlow Weather says so. The models agree. The low system developing in the Atlantic — most models keep it west of Ireland. Outer bands might bring rain and wind Thursday. But the main storm heads north. We escape. Twenty-three degrees by Monday. Twenty-three. The number feels like a promise. Kerosene expensive. Heating bills. Enjoy the weekend, he says. The weather expert speaks in the language of relief.

I think of Nikita Hand. Her voice on RTÉ Radio 1 Saturday morning. Brendan O'Connor listening. The book releasing Tuesday: *Not the Perfect Victim: My Fight for Survival, Truth and Justice*. The title itself a refusal. The perfect victim does not exist. The perfect victim is a story we tell ourselves so we can sleep.

December 9, 2018. A Dublin hotel room. The world's most famous MMA fighter. A Christmas party at the salon in Goatstown. Drinks after. A message sent. A chauffeured car. The demeanour changing completely when her friend slagged him about his watch. Driving around for a very long time. Pulling up outside houses and beeping. The embarrassment. Not wanting anyone to see them.

Then the room alone with him. The fight to protect herself becoming survival. The reasoning — his partner's brother best friends with her boyfriend. Trying to reason as nicely as she could. He wasn't taking no. Pinned down. Choked three times. The third choke longest. Seeing her daughter Freya's face. Surviving becoming bigger than fighting back. Disassociating. Looking down on her body at a different person.

The tampon wedged at the top of her vagina, removed with forceps. The forensic medical examiner testified to this in court. Her mother calling the ambulance. Toxic shock syndrome. The Rotunda's sexual assault unit — amazing staff, horrible and degrading experience. The guilt starting straight away. If I hadn't sent the DM. If I hadn't drunk so much. If I hadn't taken the cocaine. Blaming herself. Not reporting — who would believe her over him? Everyone loved him then.

Eighteen months for the DPP to decline. Devastating. Civil case the only way. Three weeks in November 2024. Absolute nightmare. Almost two hundred fifty thousand euro awarded. First time face to face with him since 2019. Complete frantic state. Panic attack. Turning her whole body away. Humiliating. Horrendous. If a crystal ball had shown her — she probably wouldn't have done it. But for her daughter. For others. So her little girl grows up in a world where if anything happens, they can speak out.

She does not forgive him. Nothing to say to him. Still panic attacks. Still PTSD. But no longer ashamed. No longer guilty. *I know it wasn't my fault what happened to me.*

The words sit in the overcast morning. Twelve point eight degrees. The wind thirteen point three kilometres per hour.

I think about the girl walking to school. Nine years old. Eleven. The space between. The rehearsed journey. The scenarios talked through. What if you need help. What if your friend isn't there. What if the man in the chauffeured car changes completely. What if the fight becomes survival. What if surviving becomes bigger than fighting back. What if you disassociate, look down at a different person. What if the guilt starts straight away. If I hadn't. If I hadn't. If I hadn't.

The ISPCC guidelines don't cover this. The NSPCC doesn't either. No legislation stipulates an appropriate age for the moment the world breaks open. No rehearsal prepares you. No footpath or pedestrian crossing protects you from the chauffeured car. No maturity metric measures the weight of a tampon removed with forceps.

The storm tracks toward Iceland. Twenty-three degrees Monday. The city will warm. The plane trees on the North Circular will catch the light differently. Gold-green. The girls walking to school — some alone, some with mothers, some rehearsed, some not — will move through air that carries the promise of warmth. The women in the Garda stations will wait. The book will release Tuesday. The voice on the radio will reach other daughters, other mothers, other women who have looked down at different people in hotel rooms, in bedrooms, in cars, in the spaces where surviving becomes bigger than fighting back.

The light at 7:02 is not the light at 6:58. It has thickened. The cloud layer holds. Thirteen point three kilometres per hour. The plane trees stir. Somewhere a satchel hits a back. Somewhere a mother rehearses a route. Somewhere a woman speaks into a microphone and the words travel through air to other rooms, other ears, other bodies that remember.

I make coffee. The kettle clicks. The steam rises into air that knows nothing of legislation or guidelines or street values or storm tracks. It only knows temperature. Pressure. The physics of water becoming vapour. The physics of light through cloud. The physics of a voice saying *it wasn't my fault* and the air carrying it, indifferent and perfect, to whoever needs to hear it.

The day begins. Overcast. Twelve point eight. The storm misses us. The warmth comes Monday. The book comes Tuesday. The girls walk. The women wait. The voice travels. I sit at the window and watch the light decide what it will become.
