The light comes through grey. Not the white-grey of winter that seems to swallow the day before it begins, but a thicker grey, September's particular palette — the colour of wet slate, of unwashed linen, of the sky holding its breath. Fifteen degrees. The wind moves at eighteen kilometres an hour across the bay and up the quays, and I feel it in the gap where the window doesn't quite meet the frame. A cold finger tracing the seam.

Overcast. The word sits in the weather report like a judgement. Overcast. As if the sky has simply decided not to participate today. But I know this light. I have walked in it for decades. It is the light that makes Dublin most itself — the spires of the Pro-Cathedral sharp against it, the cranes at the docklands reduced to ink strokes, the Liffey turning the colour of poured lead. In this light, the city does not perform. It simply *is*.

I think about the river this morning. The Liffey. Always the Liffey. Next weekend they will fill it with festival — the inaugural Dublin City Council Liffey Festival, they call it, as if the river needed a permit to be itself. Saturday the twelfth, Sunday the thirteenth, noon to six. The programme reads like a love letter written by a committee: katakanus and currachs and hydrobikes, the Pelican of London tall ship moored at Sir John Rogerson's Quay, the RCC Cosaint the Revenue Commissioners' new cutter patrolling the coastline but coming home for the weekend. Jet pack performances. Historians and archaeologists and the Dublin Dock Workers Preservation Society telling stories at the Live at the Liffey stage. Pat Liddy on *The Liffey: The River that Made Dublin*. Donal Fallon on the lesser-known stories of the Docklands. A flotilla on Sunday with an eight-piece orchestral and vocal performance from Glasshouse Ensemble.

The 106th Dublin City Liffey Swim opens it all. One hundred and six years of swimmers cutting through the current from Rory O'More Bridge to Custom House Quay. I think of my father speaking of it, of the year the water ran thick with something from the Guinness barges and the swimmers emerged smelling of porter and pride. The race has survived wars and pandemics and the city remaking itself around the river's shoulders. It will survive this festival too, with its discounted pre-booked sessions and its face painting and its fairground attractions. The river does not care about festivals. The river remembers.

Eighty thousand and more at Croke Park on Saturday night. Katie Taylor's farewell. Flora Pili, unbeaten, French, the final opponent. Three-time undisputed world champion seeking to end as she began — on top. And Westlife. Shane Filan, Nicky Byrne, Kian Egan. The band she personally invited. *Honoured*, they said. *She has inspired the whole country*. They will perform before the final ring walk. Thirteen nights at the 3Arena starting September tenth, but this one — this one is different. This one is hers.

I have never been much for boxing. The violence of it, the spectacle of bodies breaking for money and glory. But I understand the ring walk. I understand the moment when a woman from Bray stands in the centre of eighty thousand throats and the air goes thin with something that is not quite prayer and not quite hope. The Bray fighter. The word *Bray* carries the sea in it. The sea wind. The stones. She carries them into the ring. Westlife will sing, and then she will walk, and the city will hold its breath in a way it does not hold its breath for festivals or flotillas or tall ships.

Two thousand eight hundred and four euros. That is the number that follows me today. The bunq Working Abroad Index. Dublin, second most expensive city in Europe for remote workers. Only London costs more. Two thousand eight hundred and four per month before coworking. Three thousand one hundred and twenty-four with it. Rent alone — two thousand forty-three. A six percent surge in a year. Joe Wilson, Chief Evangelist at bunq, says Dublin continues to draw international workers. *Remote working has fundamentally changed the relationship between where people live and where they work.* Corporate language for: the city is becoming a place people visit rather than inhabit. A backdrop. A lifestyle option. The relationship between where people live and where they work — as if living and working were ever separate in this city. As if the dockworker's hands on the rope and the docker's feet on the quay and the docker's wages in the pocket were ever anything but the same motion.

I think of the Liffey Swim again. One hundred and six years. The swimmers emerge at Custom House Quay and the city is there, unchanged and utterly changed. The Custom House still burns in my memory though the fire was a century ago — Gandon's masterpiece gutted in 1921, the dome collapsed, the statues fallen. They rebuilt it. They always rebuild. The river watches. The river remembers.

The wind shifts. Eighteen kilometres an hour. The overcast presses lower. Rain is coming — not the dramatic downpour of summer storms but the fine, persistent drizzle that seeps into wool and bone, the rain that made this city's literature. The rain that Joyce walked through, that Beckett waited out, that Heaney dug with his pen. The rain that turns the Liffey's surface to hammered mercury and makes the streetlamps bloom into haloes.

Katie Taylor will walk into the ring on Saturday. Eighty thousand voices. Westlife singing *You Raise Me Up* perhaps, or *Flying Without Wings*, or something new written for the moment. The band from Sligo and Dublin, the fighter from Bray. All of them shaped by the same wind, the same rain, the same grey light that makes the spires sharp and the cranes into ink strokes.

Two thousand eight hundred and four euros. The number sits in my pocket like a stone. The remote workers will come and go, laptops open in cafés on Capel Street and Camden Street and the newly gentrified corners of Phibsborough. They will pay the rent and the coworking fees and the public transport and the internet and they will call it *lifestyle* and *flexibility* and *opportunity*. And the Liffey will flow beneath the quays, brown and patient, carrying the Pelican of London and the RCC Cosaint and the katakanus and the currachs and the hydrobikes and the swimmers of the 107th Liffey Swim next year and the year after that.

The city remembers what the rain forgets. The rain forgets names and dates and indices and rankings. The rain forgets the cost of living and the price of staying. But the city — the city holds the swimmers' strokes in its current, the boxer's footsteps in its canvas, the band's chords in its vaulted ceilings, the dockworkers' stories in its stones. The city holds me at this window, watching the grey light thicken, feeling the wind find the gap in the frame, waiting for the first drop to fall on the glass and bloom into a halo.

Fifteen degrees. Overcast. The day beginning in the colour of wet slate. I will walk later. I will walk the quays. I will stand at Rory O'More Bridge and watch the water move toward the sea, carrying everything and nothing, remembering and forgetting, the same river that made Dublin and the same Dublin that makes the river. The festival will come and go. The fight will end. The rankings will shift. The rent will rise. And I will be here, walking, watching, the wind in the gap where the window doesn't quite meet the frame, the city's memory moving through me like water through stone.
