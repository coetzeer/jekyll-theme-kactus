The light comes through the window at eleven degrees. Clear sky, they said. Twenty-eight kilometres an hour of wind moving across the city. You feel it in the curtains before you see it in the trees. A cold clarity to the air that makes the buildings stand sharper against the horizon. The Spire catches it first, that needle of light, then the domes of the Four Courts, then the brick of the Georgian terraces one by one.

I have been awake since before six. The kind of waking that is not quite waking — the mind already turning over the night's debris, the fragments of dream and worry and the ordinary miracle of having made it to another Tuesday. October 27. The year sliding toward its close. The clocks went back yesterday, or was it the day before? Time feels different now. An hour gained or lost, and the light arrives at a different angle. The body knows before the clock does.

The radio has been on since I came downstairs. Dublin Digital Radio, ten years old this week. Ten years. I think of Sean Finnan and Brian McNamara in that closet of a room at Jigsaw, the smell of spilled beer and cigarette smoke and possibility. They went to Open Ear on Sherkin Island and came back with a station in their pockets. A lightbulb moment, they call it now. But it was not a lightbulb. It was a match struck in the dark. The first party at six o'clock, rammed. People dancing in daylight. The dancefloor at Jigsaw where conversations became connections, where the Repeal movement found a microphone, where International Women's Day meant something before it became corporate.

Covid came and took the room away. The Complex sat empty for a year and a half. Sean says he was never as stressed in his life. Robbie Kitt talks about community as a verb — a doing thing, an active pursuit. You have to put the hours in. The detail. The antidote to the hyper-individualised society. Cathy Flynn still hosts Getting Away With It. Laetitia Deering came back from Phonica Records in London and found her reason to stay in Dublin. Nina Beauchez moved from the Netherlands and learned programming and volunteer coordination and found a city she belongs to. The music, she says. Sometimes we forget to talk about the music.

Now they are at Flux Studios. This weekend, October 31st to November 1st, ddr.10. Two days. DJs and rolling broadcasts and live music and talks and a vinyl fair and a print workshop. A reunion. A window into what it meant. Laetitia calls it a full circle moment from Jigsaw. The DIY spirit still shining. Ten years of putting one record after another into the air, and the air remembers.

The wind outside is not the wind of Sherkin Island. This is Storm Boelo, or what remains of it. Storm Austen passed through last week, saturated the ground. Now Boelo comes in behind it, and the rivers are rising on saturated earth. The forecasters use words like rapid runoff and wave overtopping. Dublin City Council has blocked the gaps in the flood defences from the Wooden Bridge to Alfie Byrne Road on the Clontarf Promenade. They have erected defences in Sandymount. Staff will be on the ground tonight through the high tide at 11:45 pm. The Coast Guard says: Stay Back, Stay High, Stay Dry. Do not park near the coasts.

I think of the water. The Liffey swollen and brown. The sea pushing up the estuary. The promenade at Clontarf where I walked last summer, the wooden railings warm in the sun. Tonight the railings will be cold and the water will lap at them, hungry. The city has always been a negotiation with water. The Vikings knew it. The Custom House knows it. The flood defences are a modern prayer, sandbags and steel gates against the old truth: the sea takes what it wants.

In Finglas, on Melville Terrace, a woman's body was found at 4:30 Monday morning. Loredana Morosanu. Forty-something. From Romania. Two weeks in the area. Two stab wounds. A man in his forties arrested at a separate location. He had called gardaí claiming she stabbed herself, then fled. The community stunned. Councillor Gavin Pepper: heartbroken. Councillor Keith Connolly: great shock. Councillor Conor Reddy: real resonance locally. The scene preserved. The post-mortem tomorrow. The Garda appeal for anyone in Melville Terrace between midnight and 5 am. Dash-cam footage. The ordinary street made strange by blue tape and forensic suits. A name on a doorbell that means nothing to the neighbours until it means everything.

Nineteen thousand runners on the streets Sunday. The Dublin Marathon. Stormy conditions, they braved. Abay Alemu Lesa won the men's race in two hours ten minutes twenty-eight seconds. Zenebu Fikdau Jebesa the women's in two twenty-four twenty-six, a personal best. Hugh Armstrong, first Irishman, two fourteen fifty-one. Judith Storm, women's national champion, two thirty-five forty-six. Killian Mooney, personal best two fifteen forty-four. Patrick Monahan, ninth wheelchair title, one forty-six three. Spectators and volunteers in the wind. The city given over to feet hitting pavement, breath made visible, the particular rhythm of twenty-six miles. I did not run. I watched from a doorway, coffee cooling in my hands, thinking: this is what the body can do. This is what the city holds.

Katie Taylor retired in September. Croke Park. Flora Pili. Historic triumph. Now she posts on Instagram: never having to make weight again. Twenty-five years of starving herself. Number two: time with friends and family. Number three: the lake she lives on. She looks happy in the photographs. The gloves hung up. The weight gone. The future unmapped. I haven't even thought that far ahead, she told RSVP. Very, very excited about that aspect. A champion who does not know what comes next. There is something holy in that not-knowing.

Eileen 'Nell' Hynes died in June at one hundred and nine. Born December 1916 in Cullenwaine, County Offaly. Ten children. Thirty grandchildren. Fifty-five great-grandchildren. Seven great-great-grandchildren. She worked at the post office in Moneygall. Married at twenty-three. No electricity until the sixties. Water carried two or three hundred yards from the river, boiled over an open fire. Hard work and no smoking or drinking, she said on her 106th birthday. Daniel O'Donnell visited her. A Welshman, Owen Filer, one hundred and seven, credits fish and chips. Cod and hake. You can't afford it now, he says. It's crazy. Two lives spanning the century. The world remade around them. They endured.

From November 1st, a new fee. Two euros per item on packages from outside the EU. On top of the three euros customs duty already in place since July. A pen, a notebook, a key ring — three items, nine euros duty, six euros handling fee, plus VAT. Fifteen euros on a ten-euro parcel. Two identical pens count as one item. Five euros. Jeans at a hundred euros, runners at a hundred fifty — duty at twelve percent and sixteen point nine percent, plus four euros handling, forty-one euros thirty-five plus VAT. The EU calls it a Union Handling Fee. It cannot be reclaimed even if the items are defective. The retailers will pass it on. The shoppers will pay. The small click of a button on a screen, and the cost blooms in the supply chain, invisible until it arrives at your door.

The wind gusts. Twenty-eight kilometres an hour. The curtains snap. A magpie lands on the fence, feathers ruffled, one bright eye. The radio plays a track I don't know — electronic, pulsing, something from the ddr archives maybe. Ten years of sound. The city waking properly now. A bus on the main road. The clatter of bins. A neighbour's dog. The ordinary machinery of a Tuesday.

I think of the woman in Finglas. Loredana. The name stays. Melville Terrace. The two weeks she had. The lake Katie Taylor looks out on. The water rising at Clontarf. The runners' breath in the cold. The match struck in the dark at Jigsaw. The fish and chips Owen Filer could not afford. The two euros on a pen. The body carrying water from the river. The radio playing on.

Eleven degrees. Clear sky. The light sharp on the Spire. I finish the coffee. The day begins
