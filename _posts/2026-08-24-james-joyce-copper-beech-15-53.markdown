The tram rattles past at 7:47. Same as yesterday. Same as every morning since the clocks went forward. I measure the season by that sound now — how it carries different through warm air, how the wheels sing higher in summer, a metal birdsong against the plane trees.

A woman in a coral cardigan boards at Harcourt Street. Paper bag in her arms, smelling of soda bread and something sharper. Rosemary, maybe. Thyme. The scent travels the carriage before the doors sigh shut. I breathe it in. Dublin breaks bread in a thousand kitchens and the tram carries the proof.

My stop. Pavement still holding last night's rain in the cracks between flags. A pigeon steps through a puddle, deliberate, head cocked, one eye on me. Used to think they were stupid, city birds. Now I think they've just made their peace with concrete. They know the rhythm of the bins, the generosity of tourists, the exact second the lights turn green at Stephen's Green.

In the park, an old man feeds them crusts from a plastic tub. Fingers knotted, skin paper-thin over blue veins. He moves slow. Each toss a small ceremony. The pigeons don't rush. They've known him longer than I've known this city. There's a language here I'm not privy to — crumbs and patience, the economy of trust.

I sit on the bench where I sat last Tuesday, and the Tuesday before. Light falls different now. August sliding toward September. The copper beech leaves have lost their spring urgency, gone deeper green, almost bronze at the edges. Soon they'll burn red and fall and the bench will be empty again. Just wood and iron and memory.

A child's laughter spikes from the playground. Pure. Unmeasured. The sound of someone who hasn't learned to edit joy. I close my eyes. The tram comes again. Pigeons scatter and settle. The old man's tub is empty. He nods at me, or maybe at the tree, or maybe at nothing at all.

I walk home through streets that know my tread. The soda bread smell lingers in my fingers, phantom warmth. This is how a day gets written — not in grand gestures but in the weight of a plastic tub, the pitch of wheels on rails, the way light gilds a copper beech in late August. The city speaks in fragments. I'm learning to listen.
