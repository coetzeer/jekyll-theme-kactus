Eight degrees. Overcast. The wind at twelve kilometres an hour from somewhere that doesn't matter. This is what the weather report says, clipped and clean as a surgeon's note. But the body knows different. The body knows the damp has already settled in the bones before the forecast arrives. The air tastes of coal smoke and river mud and something older, something that has no name in any meteorological vocabulary. November in Dublin doesn't announce itself. It seeps.

I woke to the news before I woke to the light. PJ Gallagher. Fifty-one. The age feels wrong, like a sum that doesn't balance. Cork-born, Clontarf-raised. Naked Camera. The hidden camera show that made a nation laugh at itself, that turned the ordinary into the absurd with a glance, a pause, a perfectly timed silence. I remember watching it in a flat on the South Circular Road, the laugh track of the street outside competing with the one on screen. He had a face that looked like it had been carved by laughter and then carved again by something harder. The kind of face that knows the joke and the cost of the joke.

The tributes came in the usual chorus. Gavin James. Sile Seoige. The Tánaiste. The Taoiseach. "A true treasure of Irish broadcasting." "Immense warmth and laughter." The words that appear when a public figure dies, polished smooth by repetition. But underneath the official language, something rawer. His management's statement: he spoke with remarkable candour about his experiences of mental illness. He wanted people to be able to talk about what they were going through without shame. The illness is a liar that lives in your head. You would never let anyone talk to you the way you talk to you when you're not well.

I read that line twice. The illness is a liar that lives in your head. It stays. It settles in the same place the November damp settles. Uninvited. Persistent. Telling you truths that are not truths. No one will talk to you again. You will lose your job. Everyone will be embarrassed to be around you. I know that voice. Not from depression, not exactly. But from the quieter desperation that comes at three in the morning when the heating has clicked off and the city has gone properly quiet. The voice that invents catastrophes from shadows. The voice that mistakes the absence of noise for the absence of love.

He had twins. Milo and Stevie. Born August 2023. An American scientist, Kelly Doolin from Boston, met him in 2022. He spoke about watching their characters emerge, how being a father made something true in him. I think about those two small people, not yet three, who will grow up with their father's voice on recording, his face on screen, the nation's grief as their inheritance. The particular cruelty of dying when your children are too young to remember you but old enough to feel the shape of the absence.

The light at seven a.m. is not light at all. It is a greyness that holds the memory of light. The kind that makes the Georgian doors on Merrion Square look like they've been dipped in ash. The plane trees along the quays stand skeletal, their leaves long surrendered to the same wind that's doing twelve kilometres an hour now. I watch a woman cycle past on a battered Dutch bike, her red scarf the only colour in the street. She disappears into the fog towards Heuston.

Yesterday, or so the articles say, a man burst into song at Temple Bar. Tennessee Whiskey. Antonio Cipriano. American. Broadway. Jagged Little Pill in 2019. Off Campus. National Treasure: Edge of History. Harlan Coben's Shelter. Babydoll with Jamie Miller. The videos went viral. Jaws dropped. People seethed with jealousy. I've never been so jealous of a stranger in all my life. You won the lottery witnessing this. What? I am here and I missed it.

I know that Temple Bar. Not the one in the videos, all polished cobblestones and tourist laughter. The real one. The one where the pints are poured with a heavy hand and the music sessions start before the sun goes down and the locals know which stool belongs to whom. I've stood in the doorway of The Temple Bar pub on a night like this, the rain sideways, the bodhrán beating a rhythm that matches the heart in your chest. An American actor singing Chris Stapleton in that room — it's the kind of collision that only Dublin stages. The particular and the universal. The local and the global. A man from somewhere else claiming a song from somewhere else again, and for three minutes the room becomes a country without borders.

But the videos don't show the before or the after. They don't show the man walking in from the November rain, shaking the wet from his coat. They don't show the silence that follows the last note, the awkward momentum of return to ordinary time. They don't show the woman at the bar who wasn't filming, who just listened, who thought of her father who used to sing that song badly at family gatherings, who died three Novembers ago. The camera catches the spectacle. It misses the haunting.

White Mausu launched a Christmas tin. Peanut Rãyu. Black Bean Rãyu. Smoky Chilli Oil. Thirty euros. Katie Sanderson's family biscuit recipes included. A spicy stocking filler for the foodie-loving folk. The tin can be reused. I think of Katie Sanderson in her kitchen, testing recipes, the smell of chilli oil and peanut and something sweet burning at the edges. The labour behind the product. The nights of doubt. The morning she decided yes, this one, this goes in the tin. The tin sits on a shelf in a shop on Drury Street, or maybe it's only online now, a photograph on a screen, three jars and a promise of heat.

Sister Suesey opened a second kiosk. Barrow Street. Glandore's Bottleworks building. Docklands. Roasted Brown beans. Eight a.m. to two p.m., Monday to Friday. A Christmas sambo: roast turkey, pulled winter spiced ham, herby stuffing, cranberry sauce, Emmental, crispy onions, cranberry mayo on sourdough. Oof, the article says. Oof. I've eaten that sandwich. Or one like it. The cranberry mayo sharp against the fat of the ham. The crispy onions giving way to the soft bread. The Emmental stretching in strings. The pleasure of eating something hot with your hands in the cold. Mark Dunne, general manager, thrilled to be part of such a buzzing neighbourhood. Buzzing. The word that appears in every press release about Dublin now. The city is always buzzing. The neighbourhood is always vibrant. The scene is always thriving. The language of estate agents and tourism boards, colonising the conversation.

But the coffee is real. The beans are real. The steam rising from the cup at eight-fifteen on a November morning is real. The commuter who pauses, blows across the surface, takes the first sip — that moment is real. The warmth spreading from fingers to palm to wrist to chest. The brief suspension of the rush. The Docklands wind off the Liffey finding the gap in the scarf. The DART rattling past on the viaduct. The glass towers reflecting a sky that has no colour.

I walk past the GPO. The columned portico. The bullet holes from 1916 still there, small dark punctures in the stone. A tourist guide points them out. A group nods, photographs. The history held at arm's length. But the pigeons know. They roost in the bullet holes. They shit on the heroes. They are the only ones who use the history as it was meant to be used — as shelter, as perch, as home.

On O'Connell Street the Luas trams slide past, red and silver, the ding-ding warning that nobody hears anymore. The Spire catches nothing today. No sun to reflect. It stands like a needle dropped into hay, useless, beautiful, indifferent. I think of PJ Gallagher again. The hidden camera. The joke that reveals the truth. The Spire is Dublin's hidden camera. It reflects nothing back to us but our own desire for a landmark. We built a monument to looking up.

The wind has picked up. Twelve kilometres an hour. The forecast was right about that much. It finds the collar of my coat. It turns the pages of the free newspaper someone left on a bench — PJ Gallagher's face on the front page, smiling, the headline in black: REMEMBERED AS IRISH TREASURE. The wind tears the page halfway across the bench. I don't pick it up. The news doesn't need me to keep it.

At the corner of Abbey Street, a man sells roasted chestnuts from a steel drum. The smoke curls up, sweet and earthy. He wears a flat cap. He doesn't look at me. I buy a paper bag. The heat burns through the brown paper into my fingertips. The first chestnut splits open, steam rising, the yellow meat of it. I eat it standing there. The flavour is November. It is Dublin. It is the only honest thing I've tasted all morning.

A woman passes with a White Mausu bag. The tin clinks inside. She's smiling at her phone. Maybe she's reading about Antonio Cipriano. Maybe she's reading about the Docklands coffee. Maybe she's reading nothing at all, just scrolling, the thumb moving, the brain receiving, the heart untouched. I used to judge that. Now I only recognise it. The same reflex that makes me check my own phone when the silence gets too loud. The illness is a liar that lives in your head. The phone is the truth that lives in your hand. Neither saves you.

The Spire. The chestnuts. The pigeons. The wind. The grey light that is not light. Eight degrees. Overcast. PJ Gallagher dead at fifty-one. Antonio Cipriano singing in Temple Bar. Katie Sanderson's chilli oil. Mark Dunne's Christmas sambo. The commuter's coffee. The tourist's photograph. My fingers burning from a chestnut. The bag crinkling in my pocket. The walk continuing. The city breathing. The river moving. The day beginning, or not beginning, or beginning again in the way days do in November — without fanfare, without promise, only the steady accumulation of moments that will become memory, that will become story, that will become the lie the illness tells or the truth the body knows.

I turn onto the quays. The water is the colour of the sky. Indistinguishable. The boundary dissolved. A cormorant stands on a buoy, wings spread to dry, or to catch the wind that isn't sun. It holds the pose. It waits. It knows something about waiting. About the long patience of the creature that dives and surfaces and dives again. The city is full of cormorants. We call them commuters. We call them survivors. We call them the ones who show up.

The clock on the Custom House reads seven-thirty. Or maybe it's eight. The face is blurred by the same grey that blurs everything. Time keeps its own counsel in November. The hours compress. The afternoons vanish before they arrive. The evenings are here by four. The night is a given, not a possibility.

I think of Milo and Stevie. Three years old. Twin souls in a world that has just lost its axis. I think of Kelly Doolin in Boston, or Dublin, or wherever grief has taken her. I think of the Lisa Richards Agency statement, the careful words, the request for privacy. The media will respect it for a week. Then the anniversary pieces will start. The "one year on" features. The "legacy" assessments. The machine that grinds the living into content and the dead into currency.

But not today. Today the chestnut burns my fingers. Today the wind finds my neck. Today the light fails differently, and I walk through it, and the city walks with me, and PJ Gallagher is dead and Antonio Cipriano sang and Katie Sanderson bottled heat and Mark Dunne poured coffee and the pigeons shat on the heroes and the cormorant held its wings and the river kept moving and I kept moving and the illness in my head whispered its lies and I did not believe them, not entirely, not today, not at eight degrees in the November light that is not light, walking, always walking, through the only city that has ever known me.
