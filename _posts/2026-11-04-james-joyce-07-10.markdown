The light this morning is pale, washed through with something that isn't gold and isn't grey — the colour of old letters left too long on a windowsill. Eleven degrees. The wind moves at nineteen kilometres an hour across the city, not biting, not gentle, just there, pushing the last leaves off the plane trees along the quays. Mainly clear, the report says. Mainly. That word does a lot of work.

I woke before the alarm. The body keeps its own calendar. The room held that particular stillness that only exists in the hour between night and morning, when the city hasn't yet remembered its own noise. A siren somewhere, distant. The low moan of a bus on the North Circular. The rattle of the Luas crossing the bridge at Heuston. Sound travels differently in cold air. Sharper. More honest.

Fifty European leaders descending on the Convention Centre at North Wall Quay next week. The largest policing operation in the history of the State, they say. Ten thousand Gardaí. Road closures from the 10th to the 13th. The North Wall Quay, Merrion Square, the National Gallery. A leaders' dinner there Thursday evening. I know that room. I've stood in it looking at the Caravaggio, the Taking of Christ, the light falling across Judas's face the same way it falls across the Liffey at dusk. Next week the same walls will hold different conversations. Trade. Borders. Migration. The word security used until it loses meaning.

The city prepares to become a fortress. Bollards appearing overnight. The Luas rerouted. Bus stops suspended. A map in today's paper shows exclusion zones in shades of red and amber. I think of the man who sells coffee from the cart at College Green. The woman with the flower stall on Grafton Street. The delivery drivers who know every one-way street and every loading bay timeout. Their livelihoods measured in hours lost. No one asks them about security.

At City Hall yesterday, council tenants gathered. Their rents doubled in April — from a hundred and twenty-six euro to two hundred and sixty-two a week. "You try living on two hundred and fifty-four a week," one woman said. Her adult son lives with her. He works. They still can't make it. The protest signs were handwritten. Reverse the hikes. Maintenance before increases. The Oliver Bond regeneration stalled. Mould on walls. Windows that don't close. The council voted for the increases in November, a narrow margin. Democracy as a blunt instrument.

Just twelve rental properties within HAP limits in all of Dublin last September. Twelve. One in Dublin City South for a single person or a couple. One. I walk past the new builds on the docklands. Glass and steel rising into the pale sky. Balconies empty. Luxury living banners flapping. Who lives there? Not the woman from City Hall. Not her son. Not the Garda on the checkpoint who stopped Malcolm Byrne, the Fianna Fáil TD, driving from his hotel to the airport over the summer. Drink-driving charge. Court date set. The checkpoint on the quays, the same road the leaders' motorcades will use next week. Different rules for different vehicles.

The drugs news reads like a ledger. Seven hundred and eighty-four thousand euro in cannabis seized in Coolock, Clonsaugh area, a woman in her twenties arrested. Two point five million in cocaine and three firearms in the north inner city — two men in their thirties, a woman in her twenties. Two hundred and ninety-six thousand in herbal cannabis at Dublin Port on Halloween, twenty-one point two kilograms in a vehicle from France. Operation Tara. Operation this, Operation that. The names change. The seizures grow. The street value climbs. The women in their twenties, the men in their thirties — they have mothers, they had childhoods, they once sat in classrooms learning Irish verbs and the capitals of Europe. Dublin. Paris. Berlin. Rome.

Abdullah Khan, twenty-four, sentenced to six and a half years at the Special Criminal Court. Stabbed a Garda on Capel Street in July last year. Set fire to the Black Forge Inn, Conor McGregor's pub. A self-styled jihadi, the papers call him. The judge spoke of a perversion of a legitimate faith. The Garda commissioner spoke of daily dangers. Outside the court, someone's mother waited. Someone's sister. The faith wasn't the problem. The perversion was. But perversion is a strong word for a young man who lost his way in the dark rooms of the internet, in the echo chambers where grievance curdles into certainty.

Ciara Mageean's funeral today. Determined to give everybody a day to remember, they said. The Olympian. The middle-distance runner. I never met her. But I know the feeling — the determination to make a moment hold more than its weight, to stretch a minute into something that lasts. The Dublin Book Festival starts today, runs through the 8th. Wild Lights at the Zoo from the 12th. Christmas markets from the 20th. Three Ireland rugby Tests at the Aviva. The city layers its calendars — sacred, secular, commercial, sporting — each one a way of marking time, of saying we are still here, we still gather, we still believe in the possibility of shared joy.

Roy Keane became a grandfather again yesterday. His son welcomed a first child. The news made me think of my own father, dead twelve years now, and the grandson he never met. The way grief doesn't diminish so much as settle, like sediment in water. Clear on top, thick below. You learn to read the water. You learn when to swim and when to float.

The wind has picked up. I can hear it in the chimney, a low whistle. The plane trees on the quays are nearly bare now, their branches writing fractals against the sky. In a week, the leaders will arrive. The roads will close. The coffee cart will be moved. The flower stall will be cleared. The city will hold its breath for four days, then exhale, and the damage — the lost wages, the disrupted routines, the resentment that settles in the bones of a place — will remain after the motorcades depart.

But this morning, mainly clear. Eleven degrees. The light falling across the floorboards in stripes. The kettle boiling. The day beginning, as it does, in the ordinary miracle of continuation. I will walk to the shop for milk. I will pass the Garda station on Store Street, where they're still investigating the firearms incident on Abbey Street Saturday morning, a cyclist fleeing, a magazine with ammunition found. I will pass the Luas tracks, where the trams run on schedule, indifferent to summits and seizures and rent hikes and grief. I will buy the milk. I will come home. I will write this down.

The city is not the summits. The city is not the seizures. The city is the woman at City Hall with her handwritten sign. The city is the coffee cart man. The city is the plane trees. The city is the light, mainly clear, falling across the floorboards in stripes, measuring the morning in a way no summit ever could.

I drink the tea. The wind whistles. The day continues.
