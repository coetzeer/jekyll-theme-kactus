The light comes through the curtain at ten past seven, grey as a cat's eye, the kind of light that doesn't so much illuminate as reveal the dust already there. Ten point two degrees. Overcast. The wind barely stirring — one point four kilometres an hour, the weather report says, as if that precision means anything to the body waking in a cold room. Dublin holds its breath. Halloween. Samhain. The veil thin, they say, but the veil is always thin here, the past pressing against the present like a hand against a windowpane.

I lie still a moment. The radiator clicks. A bin lorry growls somewhere down the street, the sound travelling strangely in this air, muffled and close at once. Overcast. The word sits in the mouth like a stone. Not rain, not clear, just this suspended grey that makes the city look like a photograph before the colour was invented. Ten point two degrees. My breath doesn't cloud. Not cold enough for that. Just cold enough to feel the edges of things.

The Book Festival starts Tuesday. Twenty years. One hundred and twenty events across five days. I read the list yesterday — Kevin Barry and Martin Doyle at the Royal Irish Academy of Music, Niamh Campbell and Doireann Ní Ghríofa on Sunday, their novels haunted by memories and voices from the past that linger. *Linger*. Good word. The past doesn't leave here. It settles into the brickwork. It rides the Luas. It orders a pint in the Stag's Head and stays for three.

Roddy Doyle. Anne Enright. Marian Keyes. Household names, the article calls them. As if literature were a household chore. As if the act of making sentences that survive their maker were comparable to keeping a kitchen clean. Djamel White, Booker longlisted, on the programme too. New voices. Emerging voices. The phrase makes me smile — voices don't emerge, they've been shouting all along, we just stopped talking long enough to hear them.

The DBF After Dark Club at Bartley's Lounge in the Grafton Hotel. Karaoke after the Indie Press Celebration. I picture it: publishers and editors singing badly, the particular joy of people who work with words letting words fail them. Ruth McKee, Edie May Hand, Juliano Zaffino — debut novelists. *Emerging Voices*. Thursday evening. The Grafton Hotel smells of cleaning product and old carpets and the particular sweat of tourists who've walked too far in shoes they bought for the look of them.

DCU's St Patrick's Campus. Literature and the Digital Age. An open mic. Colin Barrett, Rob Doyle, Roisin Kiberd, Chandrika Narayanan-Mohan. The fate of the written word in the content flood. *Content flood*. There's a phrase that tastes like ash. I've written things that became content. We all have. The word strips the thing of its weight, makes it flowable, measurable, stackable. A flood implies water. This is more like silt — fine, grey, settling in the lungs.

Oein DeBhairduin and Patricia Fjellgren. Thinking Aloud. Irish Traveller writer, Swedish Sámi writer. Indigenous artists in Europe. Create, remember, resist. Three verbs. The article lists them like bullet points but they're not bullet points, they're a way of staying alive. The comics thing — PANDEMONIUM at the... what was it... some venue. Phil Barrett, Alan Dunne, Katherine Foyle. Eoin Rogers editing a new journal. Comics. The form that sits in the gutter between high and low, where it's always belonged, where the real work happens.

Sunday evening at the Teeling Distillery in the Liberties. Multilingual Open Mic. Seanachaí Night. Off the Scéal. Storytelling, poetry, singing, comedy. The Liberties. The name carries its own history — liberties granted, liberties taken, the space outside the walls where the rules bent. Whiskey fumes and old prayers. I've never been to Teeling's for poetry. I've been for whiskey. The two are not unrelated.

The festival hub at the Royal Irish Academy of Music. Westland Row. I walked past it last week. The building sits there, Georgian dignity, windows reflecting a sky that never decides. Inside, pianos. Scales. The repetition that makes mastery. The repetition that makes madness. Sometimes I think writing is just scales — the same sentences over and over until one of them rings true.

***

A different article. A warning. The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission. Temu. A twelve-piece Interactive Archery Game Set. Model number 816937989081 — cn1082977006febae LH02. Batch C202432256412. Barcode 21828069545. TEMU Goods ID 601102309638984. One hundred and forty-three units sold in the Republic. Foam balls. Small components. Choking hazard.

I read the numbers twice. They have the rhythm of a litany. Model. Batch. Barcode. Goods ID. The bureaucracy of danger. One hundred and forty-three children, potentially. Or one hundred and forty-three households. Or one hundred and forty-three moments of a parent's hand closing around a foam ball, the sudden knowledge: *this could fit in a throat*.

"If you purchased one of these products, please stop use immediately, discard the product, and keep it out of the reach of children." The language of recall. Clean. Efficient. It doesn't say: *your child might die*. It says: *discard the product*. The passive voice saves everyone. The product is discarded. The hazard is mitigated. The refund is processed. TEMU will ensure affected customers receive a notification. *Ensure*. A strong word for a platform that took the money and shipped the box.

I think of the foam balls. Yellow? Orange? Bright, whatever colour screams *toy* to a three-year-old. The particular density of foam — soft enough to compress, firm enough to hold its shape in a windpipe. The archery set. Bows. Arrows. The fantasy of aim and target and hit. *Interactive*. Everything is interactive now. The screen. The toy. The algorithm that served the ad to the parent who clicked buy. The warehouse in China. The ship. The truck. The van. The doorstep. The living room. The mouth.

One hundred and forty-three.

***

Another article. Yesterday's. Halloween eve. Councillor Dathí Doolan. Sinn Féin. Children risking "life and limb" buying illegal fireworks on Snapchat. *Snapchat*. The word sits oddly in a sentence about fireworks. The border. An all-Ireland approach. "It's adults buying them, distributing them, selling them. But unfortunately, it's our children who are bearing the brunt, the physical cost."

The physical cost. Burns. Lost fingers. Eyes. The language of injury made administrative. *Dire consequences*. *Terrorised*. Vulnerable members of communities. The fireworks don't discriminate. They explode where they land. A housing estate in Tallaght. A green in Ballyfermot. A backyard in Cabra. The sound — the crack and the whistle and the thump in the chest — that's the same everywhere. The fear is the same. The thrill is the same.

"Have that conversation with your children; don't assume anything about your child that they will or won't be involved in fireworks."

Don't assume. The hardest instruction. We assume our children are sensible. We assume they know better. We assume the algorithm won't serve them the video of the boy lighting a banger in his pocket, the laugh track, the millions of views. We assume the border is a line on a map and not a conduit for the things that kill.

The Dublin Fire Brigade demonstration. Sub Officer Darren O'Connor. The dangers of explosives to fingers and hands. Water on oil-based fire — a considerable fireball. The dangers of attending illegal bonfires. 2019. The photo shows him demonstrating. The fireball caught mid-bloom. Orange and violent against a Dublin sky.

***

Cabra. Matt's site. Faussagh Avenue. The former Matt's of Cabra pub. Vacant since 2016. A decade. Boarded up by the council in September 2021. Planning permission for a six-storey, 249-bed student accommodation development. Two hundred and forty-nine beds. Student accommodation. Not homes. Beds.

Councillor Séamas McGrattan. Emergency motion. "This community will not accept second best — we need housing that is affordable for our young people to set down roots and build their lives here."

*Set down roots*. The metaphor of the tree. The metaphor of the stay. The opposite of the student bed — the transient, the temporary, the four-year lease on a mattress in a box. Grangegorman TUD campus. Two thousand one hundred student beds ready to go if the government got the finger out and funded it. *Got the finger out*. The councillor's phrase. Earthy. Real. The language of the body in a room where language is usually bloodless.

"The site has been subject to numerous planning applications by developers for years now only for them to flip the site for profit — all the while the local community must live with the eyesore of a crumbling derelict building that has been subject to serious and dangerous anti-social behaviour."

*Flip the site*. The verb of the age. Not build. Not make. Flip. The site as pancake. The site as coin. Heads: student accommodation. Tails: luxury apartments. Edge: social housing. The coin never lands on the edge.

I know Faussagh Avenue. The broad road. The trees. The brick. The ghost of Matt's pub — I never drank there but I know the shape of it, the way a neighbourhood holds the memory of a corner pub like a phantom limb. The boarded windows. The graffiti. The silence where laughter and argument and the clink of glasses and the low hum of lives being lived in public used to be.

A decade. Ten Octobers. Ten Halloweens. Ten grey mornings like this one.

***

Billy O'Hanlauin. The Billy O. His column in Totally Dublin. The Apparition of Dolly Parton, Glasthule, 1982. Altar boys. Dessie Hogan. Caffrey's caramel Time bars. Joan Jett on Top of the Pops. "She's a bang — she's a bang." The Forum cinema. The poster. *The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas*. Burt Reynolds. Dolly Parton. "Islands in the Stream." The joke. The eleven-year-old boy who doesn't get it but gets something else — the swoon, the key, the house with women in the windows.

"Our Dolly who art in Texas, beauty be thy name, blaspheme was bliss on my tongue."

I read it twice. The blasphemy. The bliss. The way the sacred and the profane braid themselves in a boy's mind — the mass, the creed, *maker of heaven and earth, of all that is seen and unseen*, and then the poster, the bleached Texan curls, the key offered. *All that is seen and unseen*. The creed covers it. The seen: the wooden frame, the women in the windows, Burt Reynolds blotted out by a raised hand. The unseen: the vast soft continents of her skin. The key. The doors.

Glasthule. Dún Laoghaire. The travel agents. Marbella. Benidorm. The JWT brochures. Women in swimsuits leaning over pool tiles. Tiny umbrellas sheltering mice. The scrapbook. The dreams of being a waiter. The dime for the juke box. A country of pounds and pence. No dimes. No juke boxes. Just the Forum cinema and the poster and the apparition.

Billy O'Hanlauin writes: "I surrendered to the sacred, secret heart of Our Lady Dolly."

*Surrendered*. The word of the believer. The word of the lover. The word of the writer who knows that the only way to tell the truth is to kneel before the lie that felt like truth at eleven.

***

The light has shifted. Imperceptibly. Still grey. Still ten point two. The bin lorry is gone. A bus sighs past. The radiator clicks again — expansion, contraction, the metal breathing.

Halloween. The shops have been selling the plastic since August. Skeletons. Witches. Pumpkins that never rot. The fireworks started two weeks ago, the early ones, the test shots. Tonight they'll peak. The Snapchat generation. The border runners. The adults who buy and the children who hold. The vulnerable terrorised. The councillor's voice on Newstalk, the particular cadence of a man who's said the same thing five years running and will say it five years more.

The Book Festival. Twenty years. One hundred and twenty events. The Royal Irish Academy of Music. The Grafton Hotel. DCU. Teeling Distillery. The Liberties. The words *emerging voices* and *indie press* and *content flood* and *thinking aloud*. The karaoke. The comics. The open mic. The whiskey.

Matt's site. Faussagh Avenue. Two hundred and forty-nine beds. Ten years vacant. The emergency motion. The finger out. The flip.

One hundred and forty-three archery sets. Foam balls. Model numbers. Batch numbers. Barcodes. Goods IDs. The refund process. The notification.

Dolly Parton. Burt Reynolds. The Forum. The key. The house. The doors. The dime that doesn't exist in this currency.

All of it happening. All of it now. The city breathing in grey. Ten point two degrees. Overcast. The veil thin. The past pressing. The future arriving in boxes from Temu, in fireworks from Snapchat, in planning permissions for student beds, in festivals that celebrate the writers who try to name it all.

I get up. The floor is cold. The kettle sings. I will make tea. I will watch the light fail to brighten. I will think about the key in Dolly's hand, the doors in the Texan house, the foam ball in the small throat, the firework in the small hand, the key that might unlock Matt's site for the families who need roots not beds.

The grey morning holds all of it. The grey morning is the only honest thing. It doesn't promise. It doesn't warn. It just is. Ten point two. Overcast. The wind one point four. The city waking. The words coming. The words always coming.
