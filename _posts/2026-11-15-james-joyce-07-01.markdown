The light arrives wet. Nine point seven degrees. The weather app calls it light drizzle, wind four kilometres an hour from the southwest, barely a whisper. I know this air. It doesn't fall so much as suspend itself — a fine grey net cast over the city, catching the sodium glow of streetlamps and holding it, trembling. The Liffey at this hour is a dark mirror. No reflection to speak of, just the suggestion of bridges, the vague architecture of quays dissolving into mist.

I walked the south quays at six. The Samuel Beckett Bridge a harp without strings, its cables lost in the low ceiling. A runner passed, breath pluming white. A fox near the Convention Centre, same as yesterday, same certainty. The city at this hour belongs to the invisible — the cleaners, the porters, the ones who keep the machinery turning while the rest of us sleep. I envy them their purpose. Their arithmetic is simple: shift starts, shift ends, wage arrives.

Memory works differently in this light. It doesn't announce. It seeps.

**Dunnes Stores in Rathmines opened Christmas delivery slots on the fourth.** December twentieth to twenty-third — the special window. Turkeys only in Christmas slots. Thirty euro minimum for delivery, no minimum for Click and Collect. You can edit your order until eleven the night before. No same-day collection between the twenty-first and twenty-third. Closed the twenty-fourth, twenty-fifth, twenty-sixth. Orders by eight pm on Monday the twenty-first arrive by Wednesday the twenty-third. After that, the twenty-ninth. Large furniture before four pm on Friday the eleventh for delivery by the eighteenth.

I read this at the kitchen table, tea cooling. The specificity of it — the dates nesting inside dates, the conditions qualifying conditions. Christmas logistics as secular liturgy. The Rathmines Dunnes where I bought apples last Tuesday, where the woman at the till asked did I want a bag and I said no and she nodded like we shared a secret. Now it's a distribution node for the feast. Three weeks until the twentieth. The slots will fill. They always do. We plan the excess with military precision.

**Ruth Dunne. Thirty-one years at St Oliver Plunkett National School in Navan.** Originally from Collon in Louth, lived in Slane. Passed at Our Lady of Lourdes Hospital on Sunday the eighth. A brave illness battle, the article says. The school's statement: valued part of the community since September nineteen ninety-five, lasting impact on generations. Her door always open. Often a queue to see her. Photocopying resources for colleagues. Loved coming to school, even battling illness. Never complained. Gave a voice to those who had none. Grá for Gaeilge. Communion gloves for a pupil's big day. Moved a talker to the front desk beside her and kept chatting.

Thirty-one years. One classroom. Thousands of children. The arithmetic staggers. A teacher's legacy isn't in the curriculum delivered — it's in the communion gloves, the moved desk, the open door, the queue of colleagues waiting for photocopied wisdom. She was sixty, maybe sixty-one. Husband Seamus. Four children: Jane, Kate, Elise, Nicky. Sister Nicola, brothers Paddy and Tom. Codladh sámh mo chara. Ar dheis Dé go raibh a hanam dílis.

I think of my own teachers. The ones who saw something. The ones who didn't. How little it takes — a glance, a word, a communion glove pressed into a small hand — and how it echoes across decades. Ruth Dunne's classroom is quiet now. But the echo travels. A former pupil, now a teacher themselves, thinking: if I'm half the teacher she was. Her kids hearing stories. The legacy living in strangers' children.

**Carer's Allowance. From January twenty twenty-seven, the maximum rate increases by ten euro.** From July twenty twenty-seven, the weekly income disregard rises to eleven hundred fifty for a single person, twenty-three hundred for a couple. Applications open now through MyWelfare.ie with verified MyGovID. You must care thirty-five hours a week, five to seven days. Full-time care means continuous supervision so they don't put themselves in danger, or continuous assistance with eating, drinking, washing, dressing. The person must be sixteen or over, incapacitated for at least twelve months, or under sixteen with Domiciliary Care Allowance.

Ten euro. Two hundred sixty euro a year. The price of a week's groceries in twenty twenty-seven, maybe less. The income disregard — eleven hundred fifty a week. Sixty thousand a year. A couple: twenty-three hundred. One hundred twenty thousand. These are not generous thresholds. They are the arithmetic of survival. The carer who gives thirty-five hours, fifty-two weeks, one thousand eight hundred twenty hours a year of washing and dressing and watching and waiting. Ten euro.

I think of Elena in my building, sixty-three, knees gone. Not a carer by title but by nature — she minds the building, the brass, the corridors, the small kindnesses that hold a place together. Her back payment from the public sector deal: twelve hundred euro. Two percent. She'll do the math while she polishes. The carer's ten euro — she'll do that math too. The arithmetic of the invisible.

The drizzle thickens. Not rain, not mist — the particular Dublin suspension that seeps into wool and bone. Nine degrees. The air tastes of wet stone and exhaust and river-salt, something older than the city. A cyclist cuts through, lights smearing white. The fox is gone.

My father on mornings like this. Docks before dawn. Thermos rattling. The same grey light. He didn't speak of the weight. The arithmetic of wages and weeks. He would have understood the Christmas slots — the planning, the anxiety, the relief when the turkey arrives. He would have understood Ruth Dunne — the job that becomes a life, the children who become your children. He would have understood the carer's ten euro — the sum that is both something and nothing, the recognition that arrives late and small.

The past isn't past. It's not even past tense. It's present continuous. The Christmas slots opening in Rathmines. Ruth Dunne's classroom door, still open in memory. The carer's thirty-five hours ticking into thirty-six. The fox. The river. The drizzle that holds all of it, indifferent and complete.

I turn for home. The Samuel Beckett Bridge behind me, cables humming in a wind I cannot feel. Nine degrees. Light drizzle. The day beginning, the day already old.
