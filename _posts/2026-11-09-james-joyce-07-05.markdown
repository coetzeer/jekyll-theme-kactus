The light at 7 a.m. in November doesn't arrive so much as seep. Grey seep, the colour of wet slate, pressing against the sash window of this room on the South Circular Road where I've sat for twenty years watching the city wake in increments. 9.4 degrees. The thermometer on the sill says so. The air has that particular density — cold holding moisture, not biting yet, just waiting. Overcast. The sky a single unbroken ceiling. No wind to speak of, 2.9 km/h, a breath held too long.

I think of Wivenhoe this morning, fogged in at 1.8. London clear at 5.9. Seattle and Austin both under the same grey lid as Dublin, 8 and 15 degrees. The world wearing different coats but the same sky. There's something in that. A shared atmosphere. The planet turning under one weather system at a time.

The news from Monday still sits in the pile by the kettle. Three pieces I read before the tea steeped.

The "Vote for Mia" scam. A WhatsApp message arriving from a name in your contacts — your sister, your cousin, the man from the pub quiz team — asking you to click a link and vote for a child in a competition. The child doesn't exist. The competition doesn't exist. The link is a key cut for your account, and once turned, the scammer walks through your address book wearing your face. The Gardaí in Kildare put out the warning. Think before you click. One click could compromise your account. I read it and thought: how strange that the thing connecting us becomes the thing betraying us. The same app that lets me send a voice note to my brother in Seattle saying "rain here too" becomes the vector for a lie that travels faster than truth. The message comes from someone you know. Their account could have been hacked. Do not click the link. I wonder how many thumbs hovered over that blue text on Monday morning. How many paused. How many didn't.

Then the New Year's Festival announcement. Three days, December 30 to January 1. Dublin Castle, Meeting House Square, the Guinness NYF Music Trail winding through Fibbers, Whelan's, the Grand Social, the Workman's Club. Aaron Rowe headlining the countdown. The Academic at the Midnight Matinee. Sharon Shannon's Big Band on New Year's Day with Jerry Fish and Camille O'Sullivan. A procession through the streets with marching bands and street performers. Free events, ticketed events, over-eighteens, family-friendly. Orla Carroll from Fáilte Ireland calling it a flagship event for the shoulder season. Kevin "Boxer" Moran from the OPW speaking of creativity and energy and atmosphere. I read the list of names — Sorcha Richardson, KhakiKid, The Scratch, BABYRAT, Bruising Shins, Bullpen, Carron, Cell Games, Ciúnas, Cruel Sister — and the city maps itself in my mind. Each venue a node. Each name a possible night. The Workman's Club on a Tuesday in December. The sound of a fiddle bleeding through the wall from O'Donoghue's. The particular smell of the Grand Social basement — spilled beer and old carpet and anticipation. I have stood in all of them. I will stand in some again. The city continues its conversation with itself through music, and we are the listeners who become the speakers.

And the Beta Festival. Running November 6 to 22, housed mainly in the Digital Hub campus. Fourth year. Art and technology. Aisling Murray, founder and director, speaking of decentering the human perspective, giving consideration to landscapes, plants, animals, machines. A new strand this year: programming for non-humans. Specifically, dogs. An installation called "The Other Audience" by Gabriela Waclawska at the Project Arts Centre for three days. Humans welcome but not the main audience. Dogs moving through the space, interacting with music and other elements. Murray inspired by her own dog, by Alexandra Daisy Ginsberg, by the concept of symbiosis. What would it look like to make art for a dog? A dog was the easiest place to start, she said, because they are one of our closest companions. I think of the dogs I have known. The terrier who barked at the vacuum cleaner as though it were a personal insult. The labrador who would place her head on my knee when the writing went wrong, as if to say: stop. Breathe. I am here. What would art for them look like? Smell-based, probably. Scent sculptures. The ghost of a fox in the grass. The ghost of another dog on the wind. A symphony of urine marks on lamp posts, each a signature, each a story. We think we own the city. The dogs know they only rent it from the smells.

AlanJames Burns' "Unmasking Nature" — multi-sensory artworks exploring how society pressures neurodivergent people to mask, to suppress natural traits. Nature depends on diversity, not masking conformity. The F Word programme — French Embassy, German Embassy, artists from Ireland, Germany, France asking questions about disinformation, fascism, authoritarian narratives. A Cyberdeck Workshop by Annike Tan, teaching young women to build their own computers. A lo-fi computer building workshop on the final weekend. Xenocene by Claudix, queer decolonial lens, real-time generative media. Asses Masses, a seven-to-eight-hour video game played collectively, about labour and collective agency.

Seven to eight hours. I have spent seven to eight hours at a stretch writing a paragraph that finally, finally worked. The time dissolves. The collective agency of a single mind arguing with itself. Is that so different?

The fog in Wivenhoe at 1.8 degrees. I have never been to Wivenhoe. It sits in Essex, near Colchester, on the River Colne. A place of tidal creeks and salt marshes. The fog there would taste of salt and mud and decaying seaweed. It would cling to the rigging of boats. It would muffle the cry of curlews. Here the overcast is different — urban, laden with the breath of a million people, the exhaust of buses on the quays, the steam from the Guinness brewery rising and mixing with the Liffey's ghost. 9.4 degrees. The air holds it. The light does not arrive. It seeps.

I think of the scam again. The message from someone you know. Their account could have been hacked. How much of what we receive is truly from the person we think? The voice note from my brother — is it him? The email from the bank — is it the bank? The article in the feed — is it written by a human or a statistical model guessing at the most likely next word? The most likely next word. The most likely next word. The most likely next word. That is how the lie travels. That is how the scam works. It wears the skin of the familiar. It mimics the rhythm of trust.

But the dogs. The dogs know. They do not read WhatsApp. They read the world through a nose that sees time. They smell the fox that passed three hours ago. They smell the fear in the sweat of the man walking past. They smell the cancer in the body before the doctor finds it. They do not click links. They follow trails. The trail is true. The link is a gamble.

The Beta Festival runs until the 22nd. Thirteen days from now. The Digital Hub campus in the Liberties, where the old powers of the city — the distillery, the tannery, the workhouse — have become something else. Startups. Studios. The Project Arts Centre on East Essex Street, where I saw a play in 1998 that I still cannot name but the feeling of it — a man sitting in a chair speaking to an empty chair — remains. The fog in Wivenhoe. The clear sky in London. The overcast in Seattle and Austin and here. One atmosphere. Many weathers.

The New Year's Festival. Fifty-two days until Aaron Rowe walks on stage at Dublin Castle for the countdown. Fifty-two days until the procession weaves through streets I have walked a thousand times, the music making the familiar strange, the strangers familiar. The Academic at the Midnight Matinee. Sharon Shannon's Big Band on the first day of 2027. The year turns. The city turns with it. The scam warns: think before you click. The festival invites: come, listen, walk, stay. The Beta Festival asks: what does the world look like from the nose of a dog?

9.4 degrees. The kettle clicks off. The tea steeps. The light seeps a little brighter, the grey thinning to something nearer white. A bus passes on the South Circular, its engine a low cello note. Somewhere a dog barks once, twice, then silence. The day begins in increments. I have not clicked any links today. I have not bought a ticket for the countdown. I have not built a cyberdeck. But I have watched the light change, and I have remembered the smell of the Grand Social basement, and I have imagined a dog in an art gallery sniffing a sculpture that smells like a fox, and I have thought: the city is not the buildings. The city is the conversations. The scams and the symphonies. The links and the trails. The overcast and the clear. The 9.4 degrees and the 1.8. The message from your brother saying rain here too. The message from your sister saying vote for Mia. One is true. One is a key cut for a lock you did not know you had.

Think before you click. Follow the trail. The dog knows the difference. The dog has always known.

I will drink the tea now. The steam rises. The light seeps. The day is 9.4 degrees and overcast and entirely, beautifully, terrifyingly open.
