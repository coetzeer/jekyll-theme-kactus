The light at 7:35 is not light at all. It is a grey suspension, a holding pattern. Nine point four degrees, the weather service says, as if precision could capture the damp weight of it pressing against the window. Thirty-five kilometres an hour of wind finding every gap in the frame. Overcast. The word sits flat on the page but the reality is thicker — a ceiling lowered to rooftop level, the city breathing under a wet wool blanket.

I have been awake since six, listening to the wind argue with the chimney. The clocks go back tonight. At two in the morning, Sunday becomes Sunday again, an hour gifted or stolen depending on how you count. The European Parliament voted to abolish this ritual in 2019. Seven years later we are still doing it, the machinery of bureaucracy grinding slower than the seasons. Most of Europe turns back with us. Egypt waits until Friday. North America holds out until November. Somewhere in the Department of Justice someone is still worrying about two time zones on one island, the border made temporal as well as political.

The extra hour in bed. That is the promise. But the body knows. The body has been keeping its own time since before clocks existed, and it will wake at the old hour tomorrow, confused by the kitchen clock's lie.

Storm Boelo. The name sounds like a cough. The National Directorate for Fire and Emergency Management met yesterday afternoon — Keith Leonard, head of the NDFEM, telling the nation to shelter in place, charge your phones, prepare for power cuts. Orange warnings for Donegal, Galway, Leitrim, Mayo, Sligo until nine tomorrow morning. Yellow for the rest of us. High tide advisory for the western counties, three this afternoon to seven tomorrow morning, storm surge and large waves and the particular violence of wind meeting water. Storm Austen barely finished with us before Boelo arrived on its heels. The ground cannot hold more water. The rivers are already high. Small upland streams will rise first, then the larger rivers will keep rising even after the rain stops. This is how flooding works — delayed, patient, inevitable.

I think of Galway this morning. The prom. The Spanish Arch. The waves throwing themselves against the sea wall, salt spray reaching the upper windows of the shops on Quay Street. I have stood there in weather like this, feeling small in a way that is almost comfortable. The world is bigger than your worries. The wind takes your breath and gives you something else — presence, maybe. The simple fact of standing upright against it.

In Clondalkin, before one in the morning yesterday, they found a man's body at a waste recycling facility. Dublin 22. The Garda Technical Bureau examining the scene. An incident room at Ronanstown station. The Office of the State Pathologist notified. A Senior Investigating Officer appointed. The language of procedure — investigating all circumstances, appealing for information, contact Ronanstown Garda Station on 01 666 7700. The words build a wall around the thing itself. A man. A body. Before dawn. Waste facility. The recycling of things we no longer want. I did not know him. You did not know him. Someone's son, perhaps. Someone's father. The wind that rattles my window this morning rattled the same air around him in his final hours. There is no poetry in this. Only the fact of it, repeated across the city every day in ways we mostly do not hear about.

Outside Dalymount Park, Phibsborough Road, ten twenty Friday night. Bohemians versus Shelbourne. The rivalry old and fierce, the kind that passes through families like heirlooms. A garda in his thirties, based in the Dublin region, fell from a moving patrol van. Serious head injury. The Mater Hospital. Still serious. The statement uses the passive — "a road traffic incident that occurred" — as if the van drove itself, as if gravity took a night off. An Garda Síochána investigating all circumstances. The match went on. The crowds went home. The Phibsborough Road carries on being a road, the trees along it indifferent to what happened beneath them. Somewhere a family waits for news. Somewhere a partner or parent or child has not slept. The wind at Dalymount tonight will not care about league tables.

In Bray, Cois Cairn estate. Jamie McGinn, thirty-eight. Bridget O'Brien, forty-five, called Biddy. Found dead Wednesday morning, October twenty-first. He found her in bed. Then he followed her — "a personal tragedy" the reports say, the phrase doing heavy lifting. Post-mortems ruled out foul play. Nine years ago, March 2017, an apartment fire in Clondalkin took their children — Jordan, four. Holly, two. Biddy survived with severe burns, months in intensive care. The fire also took her pregnant cousin Annmarie, twenty-seven, and Annmarie's daughter Paris, two. Tuesday morning, twelve noon, St Anne's Church in Shankill. Joint funeral Mass. Then Springfield Cemetery, Killarney Road, Bray. Laid to rest with Jordan and Holly. The death notices on RIP.ie list fathers, mothers, sisters, brothers, brothers-in-law, sisters-in-law, uncles, aunts, cousins, extended family and friends — the same list twice, once for him, once for her, the grammar of grief repeating itself. "Rest easy now Biddy may your heart ache be gone and your arms now full back with your babies." Someone wrote that. Someone else: "Life is so cruel. Rest in peace now Jamie and Biddy with your little darlings." The comments accumulate like stones on a grave. Nine years. Nine years carrying that fire. I think of the weight of it. The ordinary mornings — making tea, walking to work, the radio in the car — all of it shadowed. The mind finds ways to function around the hole. But the hole does not close. It becomes architecture.

Alannah Price. Sixteen. Missing from Dublin 8 since Tuesday, October twentieth. Four days now. Last seen around four in the afternoon. Brown leggings, brown crop top, black runners, small black backpack. Five feet two, medium build, blonde hair, blue eyes. Kevin Street Garda Station. 01 6669400. Garda Confidential Line 1800 666 111. The photograph shows a girl with a phone case, a half-smile, the ordinary armour of adolescence. Four days. The clock change tonight means one more hour of darkness in the evenings. One less hour of morning light for searching. Her mother — there must be a mother — has lived through ninety-six hours of not knowing. The imagining is worse than the knowing. The knowing, when it comes, brings its own devastation. But the not-knowing is a room without doors. Gardaí are concerned for her welfare. The phrase means they fear the worst. We all fear the worst. We all hope for the best. The city holds its breath in small pockets — Kevin Street, the streets around it, the places a sixteen-year-old might go. The Luas lines. The canals. The parks. The wind does not know her name.

The bank holiday Monday. Tesco, Aldi, Lidl, Dunnes, SuperValu, Marks and Spencer — all running Sunday hours. Ten to eight, nine to nine, eight to ten. The crowds expected Saturday, Halloween provisions, the quiet windows 8-10am and 7-10pm at Aldi. The machinery of consumption adjusts itself to the calendar. We stock up against the dark. Sweets for children who will knock on doors in five days. Pumpkins. Batteries. Tins of soup. The supermarkets will not close completely. They never do. Even in Storm Boelo, even in pandemic, even in the worst weather — the supply chain is the new religion, the altar always open. I will walk to the shop later. Buy milk. Maybe bread. The walk will be the point. The wind in the face. The nine degrees felt on skin rather than read on a screen.

Seattle is also nine degrees today. Overcast. Eight kilometres an hour wind. A different ocean but the same latitude almost. Austin is twenty-three point seven. Overcast. Twelve point nine wind. London ten point five, mainly clear. Wivenhoe ten point three, mainly clear. The world turns and the weather moves across it in systems we name — Boelo, Austen — as if naming gives us purchase. The jet stream does not care about our names. The azimuth of the sun does not care about our clocks. At 2am tonight, by human agreement, the hour between one and two will happen twice. The servers will log it. The timestamps will need disambiguation. The trains will run to adjusted timetables. The church bells, if any still ring, will ring an hour early by the new reckoning, on time by the old. My internal clock will not get the memo.

I remember a morning like this in 1994. Or maybe 1997. The year slips. The date was the last Sunday in October. I was living on the South Circular Road then, in a flat above a shop that sold religious statues and candles. The Virgin Mary in plastic, the Sacred Heart in plaster, vigil lights that burned for three days if you were careful. The landlady, Mrs. O'Shea, believed the clock change was a British plot. "Stealing our light," she called it. She would not change the kitchen clock until Monday morning, running her house on summer time in defiance. The rest of the country moved on. She made her own time zone, a micro-sovereignty of one room. I liked that about her. The small rebellion. The refusal to let the schedule dictate the body.

Now I am the age she was then. The kitchen clock will change itself — radio-controlled, atomic time from a signal in Anthorn, Cumbria, or maybe Mainflingen, Germany. The phone will change. The laptop. The car. The watch on my wrist if I wore one. The devices talk to each other, synchronised, obedient. The only clock that will not change is the one in the hallway, the mechanical one with the brass pendulum that my father gave me. It stopped in 2019. I never had it fixed. It reads 3:17 permanently, afternoon or morning, summer or winter. A frozen moment. Sometimes I think that is the truest time of all — the time that refuses to participate.

The wind gusts. The window frame sings its high thin note. Nine degrees. Overcast. The day stretches ahead — grey, wet, the clocks turning back, the storm arriving, the funerals preparing, the missing girl still missing, the recycling facility in Clondalkin cordoned off, the patrol van at Dalymount impounded, the supermarkets opening on Sunday hours. All of it happening at once, all of it connected only by the city that contains it. Dublin. Baile Átha Cliath. The ford of the hurdles. The dark pool. The Vikings named it for the black water where the Poddle met the Liffey. The Poddle runs underground now, culverted, forgotten. The Liffey flows brown and tidal under the bridges. The wind pushes upstream against the current, making the surface choppy, confusing the gulls.

I will make coffee. I will watch the light fail to arrive properly. I will think about the man in Clondalkin, the garda in the Mater, Jamie and Biddy in Bray, Alannah somewhere in Dublin 8. I will think about the clocks and the storm and the nine degrees. I will walk to the shop later and buy milk and the wind will find my face and for a moment I will be nothing but a body in weather, anonymous, present, here.

The hour we gain tonight — what will I do with it? Sleep, probably. Dream of black water and hurdles. Wake at the old time tomorrow, disoriented, the kitchen clock lying to me. The body keeping its own counsel. The city continuing its million small dramas, most of them unrecorded, all of them real.

Nine degrees. The wind. The waiting light.
