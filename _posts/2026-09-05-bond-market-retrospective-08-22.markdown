This was a week when the world's borrowing costs briefly touched heights not seen in years, then gave some back. The story beneath the zig-zag is simple: a war-driven energy shock re-ignited inflation fears, governments are borrowing heavily and investors are starting to demand a premium for it, and the central banks that would normally calm things are instead expected to keep tightening. Here's what happened and why it matters.

## The single thread: oil and the Iran war

Everything this week traces back to the renewed US military campaign in Iran and its effect on energy prices. Brent crude climbed to six-week highs and briefly touched $100 a barrel, and European natural gas rose to its highest level since January 2023. Because energy sits at the root of most inflation, rising oil prices instantly repriced the outlook for interest rates everywhere. When President Trump suggested the campaign "would not last long" and crude retreated from its highs, bonds breathed out. That one sentence explains most of Thursday's relief rally.

## Europe: an inflation scare and a certain rate hike

Eurozone inflation accelerated to 3.3% in August, the highest since September 2023 and well above the ECB's 2% target, driven largely by energy. That cemented expectations for a quarter-point rate hike at the ECB's September 10 meeting, and money markets now price the deposit rate reaching around 3% by mid-2027.

The result was a genuine bond rout. Germany's 10-year Bund yield touched 3.38–3.40% on Wednesday, its highest level since April 2011; French yields hit their highest since November 2008, and Italian and Spanish yields climbed to near three-year highs. Thursday brought relief — eurozone bonds snapped a six-day losing streak as gas prices fell, with the Bund easing back toward 3.33% — but they were still heading for a fourth straight weekly rise. A Reuters poll captures the tension: economists expect the ECB to hike on September 10 for the second and final time, in what would be its shortest hiking campaign in 15 years, because policymakers are wary of piling pressure on a fragile economy already dealing with surging sovereign yields.

## UK: gilts caught between a squeeze and the budget

Britain had the most dramatic moves. Yields on 20- and 30-year gilts — the very long end investors watch most closely for fiscal credibility — surged on Tuesday and Wednesday to levels last seen in 1998, before falling back later in the week. The 10-year gilt yield touched a 19-year high around 5.29% on Wednesday, then fell 9 basis points to about 5.14% on Thursday, its biggest one-day drop in a month. Friday saw it ease to roughly 5.07–5.15% as markets waited on US jobs data.

Two forces are at work. First, energy-driven inflation has markets fully pricing a Bank of England rate hike by year-end and another by March 2027. Second, the cost of the government's borrowing is becoming a story in itself: on Thursday Britain sold £900 million ($1.2 billion) of inflation-linked debt at the highest real yield in 25 years, a stark illustration of how expensive long-dated issuance has become ahead of next month's budget, where Chancellor John Healey faces questions over the UK's fiscal rules.

## US: the Fed's messaging whiplash

Across the Atlantic, the debate is whether the Fed is about to hike again. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish remarks, combined with rising oil prices, had markets pricing around a two-thirds chance of a September hike. But on Thursday, Governor Christopher Waller pushed back, saying recent progress on inflation could justify easing expectations of a near-term hike — sending the dollar lower and helping global bonds rally. US 10-year yields stood near 4.78% as of mid-week, with investors closely watching Friday's employment report for the final steer.

## Why it matters for a normal reader

Bond yields are the world's base interest rate — they influence mortgages, corporate borrowing, pensions and even the prices governments can afford to borrow at. When long-term yields jump because investors doubt a country's fiscal path or fear inflation, it raises the cost of almost everything financed with debt. The week's big lesson is that the two old forces — inflation from energy and public-debt worries — have recombined. Governments are borrowing more for defence, the energy transition and ageing populations, and the market is beginning to charge them for it. Even if the ECB and BoE deliver just one more hike each, today's elevated yields are likely to stick around.
