Over the last two days, government bond markets in the US, UK and Europe sent a clear, unified message: investors are nervous about inflation, and that nervousness is pushing borrowing costs around the world.

## What happened

In the US, the 10-year Treasury yield sat around **4.69%** on 17 August, barely changed on the day but still notably higher than a year ago. The two-year note yielded about **4.17%**. The pattern is the same as it has been for weeks: rates drifting upward rather than collapsing, even as people keep betting that central banks will eventually cut rates.

In the UK, the pressure has been sharper. The 10-year gilt yield climbed back **above 5%** early in the week. That is a psychologically important level because it means the UK government is paying more than 5% a year to borrow for a decade.

In Europe, the mood is calmer but still uneasy. The gap between German Bunds and Italian BTPs — the classic “core versus peripheral” spread — has narrowed sharply this year, suggesting investors are more willing to lend to weaker eurozone countries. But yields are still moving on inflation jitters.

## Why it happened

The common thread is **inflation**. In the US, prices have proved stickier than many expected, which makes investors think the Federal Reserve may keep interest rates higher for longer. In the UK, inflation is still a problem, and the government’s tax plans have added extra uncertainty: markets worry that more borrowing or higher taxes could reignite price pressures.

In Europe, oil prices and global shipping costs have been feeding through to energy and goods prices. That has traders scaling back their bets on early rate cuts by the European Central Bank.

Another, more modern, factor is also at work: big technology companies are borrowing huge sums for artificial-intelligence infrastructure. That borrowing binge is changing the shape of credit markets and keeping demand for capital high.

## Why it matters

When government bond yields rise, it costs everybody more to borrow: homeowners, companies, and governments. In the UK, a 10-year yield above 5% makes the government’s debt bill heavier and tightens the squeeze on public finances.

In the US, higher long-term rates can slow the economy by making mortgages, car loans and business investment more expensive. They also hit the prices of existing bonds and growth-focused stocks.

In Europe, the narrowing Bund-BTP spread is good news for countries like Italy and Spain, but if inflation stays high, the European Central Bank may be forced to hold rates steady, which would keep the cost of credit elevated across the eurozone.

For ordinary people, the message is simple: cheaper loans may take longer to arrive than many hoped. Bond markets are telling us that the era of very low interest rates is still not back.
